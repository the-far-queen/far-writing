Call me Ishmael. Some years ago, never mind how long precisely, having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world. It is a way I have of driving off the spleen and regulating the circulation. Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet; and especially whenever my hypos get such an upper hand of me, that it requires a strong moral principle to prevent me from deliberately stepping into the street, and methodically knocking people's hats off, then, I account it high time to get to sea as soon as I can.

This is my substitute for pistol and ball. With a philosophical flourish Cato throws himself upon his sword; I quietly take to the ship. There is nothing surprising in this. If they but knew it, almost all men in their degree, some time or other, cherish very nearly the same feelings towards the ocean with me.

There now is your insular city of the Manhattoes, belted round by wharves as Indian isles by coral reefs, commerce surrounds it with her surf. Right and left, the streets take you waterward. Its extreme downtown is the battery, where that noble mole is washed by waves, and cooled by breezes, which a few hours previous were out of sight of land. Look at the crowds of water-gazers there.

Circumambulate the city of a dreamy Sabbath afternoon. Go from Corlears Hook to Coenties Slip, and from thence, by Whitehall, northward. What do you see? Posted like silent sentinels all around the town, stand thousands upon thousands of mortal men fixed in ocean reveries. Some leaning against the spiles; some seated upon the pier-heads; some looking over the bulwarks of ships from China; some high aloft in the rigging, as if striving to get a still better seaward peep. But these are all landsmen; of week days pent up in lath and plaster, tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks. How then is this? Are the green fields gone? What do they here?

But look! Here come more crowds, pacing straight for the water, and seemingly bound for a dive. Strange! Nothing will content them but the extremest limit of the land; loitering under the shady lee of yonder warehouses will not suffice. No. They must get just as nigh the water as they possibly can without falling in. And there they stand, miles upon miles, with nothing but their faces toward the ocean, jealously watching his every sooty movement, as he toiling. But the morning comes, and the midday sun, and then the evening, and the whole process of the day is a seduction.

You have heard of Captain Ahab. Few things you have heard of, perhaps, that are not the beginning of the story of the Pequod, and the story of the Pequod is the story of all men who have gone out to look for something and found that the looking was the whole of it. I had no such captain in mind when I shipped. I had a ship in mind, and a reason, and the reason was that a man who stays on the land is a man who never learns what he is made of, and I wanted to find out. That is all the ambition I ever had, and it got me onto a whaler, and it is the only reason I am able to write this at all.

So I am a whaleman. Say it again, reader, if you like, because you may not grasp what it means. Say it, and then go on and tell yourself what it is not, and when you have done that you will be closer to understanding what it is. It is a course of life made entirely of consequences. Every man who has ever been down to the water and back has signed for the same contract, and the ink is not dry on any of the papers, and the papers are in the sea already.

The streets take you waterward, and that is the whole of the navigation. Everything after this is current.

And here, before I take you any further, I will put down the one thing about the sea that I know to be true, because if I do not put it down now you will not believe me later, and I shall want you to believe me later.

The sea is not out to get anybody.

That is the first and last of it. There is no malice in the ocean. It has no plans. It does not hate the Pequod, it never heard of the Pequod, and if the Pequod had never been built the waves would have gone on doing precisely what they did. But the whale, that is different. The whale did not choose. The whale was there. And that is the whole of the tragedy, and it is worth saying plainly because it is the thing people get wrong. Nothing in this story came looking for us. We went out looking for something, and the looking is what killed us.

If that is true, and I believe it is the truest thing I know about the sea, then the question worth asking is not what the whale did to Ahab, but what Ahab did to Ahab. Because Ahab did that. Before he ever saw the white whale, Ahab had already been ruined. The ruin was complete the morning he went into that cabin and read those charts, and every whale he ever killed afterward was an attempt to get back to a man he used to be.

I have learned this the hard way, and I want to tell it straight, in order, from the beginning, because I did not know any of it when it was happening and I have had to go back and take it apart to see what it was made of.

I stuffed a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, tucked it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. Quitting the good city of old Manhatto, I duly arrived in New Bedford. It was a Saturday night in December. Much was I disappointed upon learning that the little packet for Nantucket had already sailed, and that no way of reaching that place would offer, till the following Monday.

As most young candidates for the pains and penalties of whaling stop at this same New Bedford, thence to embark on their voyage, it may as well be related that I, for one, had no idea of so doing. For my mind was made up to sail in no other than a Nantucket craft, because there was a fine, boisterous something about everything connected with that famous old island, which amazingly pleased me. Besides, though New Bedford has of late been gradually monopolising the business of whaling, and though in this matter poor old Nantucket is now much behind her, yet Nantucket was her great original, the Tyre of this Carthage; the place where the first dead American whale was stranded. Where else but from Nantucket did those aboriginal whalemen, the Red-Men, first sally out in canoes to give chase to the Leviathan?

I knew nothing about it. That is worth saying plainly. I had no theory of whaling, no theory of the sea, and no theory of what a man is for. I was a man who wanted to see the watery part of the world, and I went and looked for the cheapest way to do it, and the cheapest way happened to be an inn with a name written over the door in a lot of tin.

So, wherever you go, Ishmael, said I to myself, as I stood in the middle of a dreary street shouldering my bag, and comparing the gloom towards the north with the darkness towards the south, wherever in your wisdom you may conclude to lodge for the night, my dear Ishmael, be sure to inquire the price, and don't be too particular. With halting steps I paced the streets, and passed the sign of The Crossed Harpoons, but it looked too expensive and jolly there. Further on, from the bright red windows of the Sword-Fish Inn, there came such fervent rays, that it seemed to have melted the packed snow and ice from before the house, for everywhere else the congealed frost lay ten inches thick in a hard, asphaltic pavement.

So on I went. I now by instinct followed the streets that took me waterward, for there, doubtless, were the cheapest, if not the cheeriest inns. Such dreary streets! Blocks of blackness, not houses, on either hand, and here and there a candle, like a candle moving about in a tomb. At this hour of the night, of the last day of the week, that quarter of the town proved all but deserted.

But presently I came to a smoky light proceeding from a low, wide building, the door of which stood invitingly open. It had a careless look, as if it were meant for the uses of the public; so, entering, the first thing I did was to stumble over an ash-box in the porch. But The Crossed Harpoons, and The Sword-Fish, this then must needs be the sign of The Trap. However, I picked myself up and hearing a loud voice within, pushed on and opened a second, interior door. It seemed the great Black Parliament sitting in Tophet. A hundred black faces turned round in their rows to peer; and beyond, a black Angel of Doom was beating a book in a pulpit. It was a negro church; and the preacher's text was about the blackness of darkness, and the weeping and wailing and teeth-gnashing there. Ha, Ishmael, muttered I, backing out, wretched entertainment at the sign of The Trap!

Moving on, I at last came to a dim sort of light not far from the docks, and heard a forlorn creaking in the air; and looking up, saw a swinging sign over the door with a white painting upon it, faintly representing a tall straight jet of misty spray, and these words underneath, The Spouter Inn: Peter Coffin. Coffin? Spouter? Rather ominous in that particular connexion, thought I. But it is a common name in Nantucket, they say, and I suppose this Peter here is an emigrant from there.

It was a queer sort of place, a gable-ended old house, one side palsied as it were, and leaning over sadly. It stood on a sharp bleak corner, where that tempestuous wind Euroclydon kept up a worse howling than ever it did about poor Paul's tossed craft. Yet this is the house where everything happens, so I will not leave it in a hurry.

Entering that gable-ended Spouter-Inn, you found yourself in a wide, low, straggling entry with old-fashioned wainscots, reminding one of the bulwarks of some condemned old craft. On one side hung a very large oilpainting so thoroughly besmoked, and every way defaced, that in the unequal crosslights by which you viewed it, it was only by diligent study and a series of systematic visits to it, and careful inquiry of the neighbors, that you could any way arrive at an understanding of its purpose.

What most puzzled and confounded you was a long, limber, portentous, black mass of something hovering in the centre of the picture over three blue, dim, perpendicular lines floating in a nameless yeast. But does it not bear a faint resemblance to a gigantic fish? Even the great Leviathan himself? In fact, the picture represents a Cape-Horner in a great hurricane; the half-foundered ship weltering there with its three dismantled masts alone visible; and an exasperated whale, purposing to spring clean over the craft, is in the enormous act of impaling himself upon the three mast-heads.

Look at that picture. That is the whole book, painted on the wall of a cheap inn before anyone knew it was true. The whale comes out of the sea on his own account, and in the act of doing something enormous he destroys the thing that happens to be underneath him. Nobody sent him. Nobody told him to. He was doing what he does, and the ship was in the way, and that was the end of the ship.

And yet I have never been able to decide, even now, which of the two was the catastrophe.

The opposite wall of this entry was hung all over with a heathenish array of monstrous clubs and spears. Some were thickly set with glittering teeth resembling ivory saws; others were tufted with knots of human hair; and one was sickle-shaped, with a vast handle sweeping round like the segment made in the new-mown grass by a long-armed mower. You shuddered as you gazed, and wondered what monstrous cannibal and savage could ever have gone a death-harvesting with such a hacking, horrifying implement. Mixed with these were rusty old whaling lances and harpoons all broken and deformed. Some were storied weapons.

Projecting from the further angle of the room stands a dark-looking den, the bar, a rude attempt at a right whale's head. Be that how it may, there stands the vast arched bone of the whale's jaw, so wide, a coach might almost drive beneath it. Within are shabby shelves, ranged round with old decanters, bottles, flasks; and in those jaws of swift destruction, like another cursed Jonah, bustles a little withered old man, who, for their money, dearly sells the sailors deliriums and death.

Abominable are the tumblers into which he pours his poison. Though true cylinders without, within, the villanous green goggling glasses deceitfully tapered downwards to a cheating bottom. Fill to this mark, and your charge is but a penny; to this a penny more; and so on to the full glass, the Cape Horn measure, which you may gulp down for a shilling.

Upon entering the place I found a number of young seamen gathered about a table, examining by a dim light divers specimens of skrimshander. I sought the landlord, and telling him I desired to be accommodated with a room, received for answer that his house was full. Not a bed unoccupied.

But avast, he added, tapping his forehead, you haint no objections to sharing a harpooneer's blanket, have ye? I s'pose you are goin' a-whalin', so you'd better get used to that sort of thing.

I told him that I never liked to sleep two in a bed; that if I should ever do so, it would depend upon who the harpooneer might be, and that if he really had no other place for me, and the harpooneer was not decidedly objectionable, why rather than wander further about a strange town on so bitter a night, I would put up with the half of any decent man's blanket.

Supper was ready. It was cold as Iceland, no fire at all, the landlord said he couldn't afford it. Nothing but two dismal tallow candles, each in a winding sheet. We were fain to button up our monkey jackets, and hold to our lips cups of scalding tea with our half frozen fingers. But the fare was of the most substantial kind, not only meat and potatoes, but dumplings; good heavens! dumplings for supper! One young fellow in a green box coat addressed himself to these dumplings in a most direful manner.

My boy, said the landlord, you'll have the nightmare to a dead sartainty.

Landlord, I whispered, that aint the harpooneer is it?

Oh, no, said he, looking a sort of diabolically funny, the harpooneer is a dark complexioned chap. He never eats dumplings, he don't, he eats nothing but steaks, and he likes 'em rare.

I began to feel suspicious of this dark complexioned harpooneer. At any rate, I made up my mind that if it so turned out that we should sleep together, he must undress and get into bed before I did.

Supper over, the company went back to the bar-room, when, knowing not what else to do with myself, I resolved to spend the rest of the evening as a looker on. Presently a rioting noise was heard without. Starting up, the landlord cried, That's the Grampus's crew. I seed her reported in the offing this morning; a three years' voyage, and a full ship. Hurrah, boys; now we'll have the latest news from the Feegees.

They seemed an eruption of bears from Labrador. They had just landed from their boat, and this was the first house they entered. No wonder, then, that they made a straight wake for the whale's mouth, the bar.

I observed, however, that one of them held somewhat aloof, and though he seemed desirous not to spoil the hilarity of his shipmates by his own sober face, yet upon the whole he refrained from making as much noise as the rest. This man interested me at once. He stood full six feet in height, with noble shoulders, and a chest like a coffer-dam. I have seldom seen such brawn in a man. His face was deeply brown and burnt, making his white teeth dazzling by the contrast; while in the deep shadows of his eyes floated some reminiscences that did not seem to give him much joy.

When the revelry of his companions had mounted to its height, this man slipped away unobserved, and I saw no more of him till he became my comrade on the sea. And then I was another man, and he was still the same.

And then I was another man. That is the plain way to put it. Nothing happened to me that night. I got into a bed and a stranger got into the same bed, and by morning I was a different person, and the reason is not in anything he did. It is in the fact of the sleeping.

I have thought about this a great deal since, and I have never found a way to explain it that satisfies me, which is not the way of a man who has spent thirty years lying about the reasons for things. He had an arm thrown over me in the morning, and I did not take it away. That is the entire event. I did not take it away.

In the morning I came out and Queequeg was sitting on the edge of the bunk with his back to me, and I put my hand on his shoulder before I had thought about it. His skin was covered in writing. Look at his arm. It was sleeves of a script, the squares and triangles of it running up from the wrist to the shoulder, and no two parts of the same shade, and lying there on the patchwork counterpane it looked for all the world like another piece of the quilt, and it was only the weight of it that told me he was there at all.

I want to be exact about the mechanism of it, because everybody has this backwards. It is not that I saw a man covered in writing and accepted him. It is that I slept in a bed with him, and a man who sleeps in a bed with another man has been past the point of judgement, and by morning his arm was on me and I did not take it off. Whatever else is true about men and how quickly they decide things about each other, this much I know from my own body. It is slower than that. It is much slower than that. The decision was made at two in the morning while I was asleep and neither of us was there to make it.

Then he asked me, in the same easy tone in which he asked me what money was, whether I thought the harpoon used in catching whales was sharp. And I told him it was not sharp, but barbed, which is worse. And he said, yes, and then he reached over and took his harpoon out of the corner where he had propped it, and gave it to me to feel, and I felt the barbs come up under my thumb, and the point was not sharp, and the thing in my hand was not a fishing rod.

That was the moment the whole thing began, and nobody was present at the time. There was no thunder, and no music, and no oath taken by anybody. There was a man in a sail-loft in a New England town holding a harpoon upside down and not quite understanding it, and asking a stupid question, and being answered kindly by somebody who did not have the same idea of what a question is that I did.

The Spouter-Inn had a table and a fire and a bar and a pulpit in one long low building, and the whole of the whale voyage was decided inside it in the space of about eleven hours.

Now I want to tell you what the rest of the men were, because the ship was the argument and the crew was the argument, and if you want to know why a catastrophe happened you had better know who was standing on the deck when it did.

There is no end to the sea. That is the first thing. Every mile you go there is another, and it is the same sea, and the same whales, and men will pay a great deal of money to be told that there is an end to it somewhere. That is what the Nantucket men went down for, every year, in boats that cost more than a house, chasing something they could not catch and could not explain and could not put down. The law of the fishery gives it a shape. If you find a whale, the whale is yours. If you get a line in it first, it is yours, and if somebody else had it first and it comes to you, it is yours. That is the whole of the law, and it is very short, and it is very clear, and it settles every argument about money that will ever be had at sea.

The sea does not make men equal. It makes them hungry, and then it gives them a rule.

Ahab had been at sea forty years. That number did the damage. Not the losing of the leg; the forty years. A man does not get to forty years at sea by being reasonable, and by the time he has done it the thing has stopped being a pursuit and become a condition, and there is no more distance between a man and his purpose than there is between a man and his own name.

I understood none of this then. I want to be plain about that, because it would be easy to pretend I understood, and it would be a lie and it would make everything after it into a lesson, and it was not a lesson. It was a thing that happened to a great many people, and I was the one who got away to write it down.

What I want you to understand, and what took me thirty years to understand, is this. Not that Ahab was mad, which is a word that explains nothing. That a crew of men followed him for three years across six oceans toward something that was not going to be found. That they were not deceived by him; they were not under his power; they were men who had looked at what he was looking at and wanted it too.

And that is the thing nobody tells you about the sea. It does not need to deceive you. It will hand you something real, and be silent, and wait.

Then, after the year had turned and I had been at sea some weeks, I came to a place where a man stood at the head of a barrel with a harpoon and told us what we were after, and I understood the rest of it.

I will tell you what a whale is, before I tell you what happened, because the second story is unintelligible without the first.

And it is a strange thing to have to say, that after a month of whaling I am not sure that any of us knew what a whale was, including the men who had been killing them for a living for thirty years. There was a chart in the cabin. There was a book about them that a man had written who had never seen one. There was a set of terms for every part of them, and the terms were good, and they made a shape that you could carry in your head, and it was a lie in the way that all arrangements of words are a lie when they are used for a thing that will not hold still.

What a whale actually is, is not a fact about an animal. It is a fact about you. That is the thing I did not know and could not have been told, because it is only true at the end of the voyage. By then you are not the same man who left, and the whale is not the same whale, and the argument between the two of you has been going on for three years in about four hundred thousand spoken words, most of them shouted, some of them not spoken at all.

You can learn the parts. You can learn that it is warm-blooded, which no fish is, and that it breathes air the way you do, through a hole out of the top of its head, and that it has a chamber in there like a church, and that it comes up to the surface to think. You can learn all of this and it will not help you, and it is all true.

You can learn that it is not a fish at all, which was the great discovery of my own century and the reason men had spent a hundred years being surprised that a whale had a mother. It breathes air. It is warm. It brings its young into the world alive, the way we do, not in eggs, not in spawn, and the little one comes out of the mother as large as a man, and there is nothing about the way it is made that you could hold up to anybody who came from a tree.

You cannot get your mind round that. You cannot get your mind round an animal the size of a church that is warmer than you are, and kinder, and lives in a world with no night in it and no cold in it and no thought of going anywhere at all, and has no enemies except one, and that one is us.

And then you are out there in a boat with a line in it, and you find out which part of your education was real.

Here is what the books did not tell me. They told me it was a fish and I was wrong; they told me it was a mammal and I was right; and neither of those was the information that mattered. The information that mattered was this: it is bigger than the boat.

Not larger. Bigger. In the sense that a cathedral is bigger than the door of it. You can be in the same ocean with a thing and be in a different world from it, and there is no way to get from one to the other, and the whole of the men who go out after whales go out knowing this and go anyway.

I did not know it for a fact on the first day. I knew it in the only way it can be known, which is to have a line in your hand and feel something at the other end of it that you are entirely unable to hold in your mind, and to go on holding the line.

That is the whole book. Everything else is weather.

I have said I would tell you what a whale is, and I have not told you, and you will not notice that I have not, because you will have been thinking about the ship.

So let me try once more, in the only terms I have left, and then I will go on to the wreck, which is the part you came for.

There was a reason they called it cetology, which is the science of whales, and they called it a science, and I want to tell you what the science was, because it is the most honest thing anybody ever said about them. The men who knew most about whales said they knew nothing.

Scoresby, who had been out here and had the best of it, said that no branch of zoology is so much involved as that which is entitled Cetology. Beale, who had been a surgeon on a whaler and had written the best book on the subject, said there was not his intention to enter into the inquiry as to the true method of dividing the cetacea into groups and families, and then said the only true thing anybody had said on the subject: utter confusion exists among the historians of this animal.

The great Cuvier said the whale was a fish. John Hunter, who dissected them, said something else. And between the two of them there was nothing, and every generation since has been filling the space with better words instead of better knowledge, and there is still no answer to the question, and there never will be, because the question is not well formed.

And this is what I have come to think, and I put it plainly because it is the only thing I know that the men with the books did not know: there is no answer to the question of what a whale is, because it is the wrong question, and it has been the wrong question for three hundred years, and it is the wrong question for the same reason it is wrong for a man to ask what a man is.

A whale is not a thing that can be defined, because it is not a kind. It is a whole world, and a whole world cannot be put inside a definition any more than a country can. The books classify them by size, into the folio, the octavo, and the duodecimo, and that is true and useless, exactly like dividing men by height. When the sperm whale comes up out of the water off the Line, it is not a large animal that has come up. It is another climate that has come up. And the men who strike it do not go back a different man because they have killed something. They go back a different man because they have been down into another country and come out again.

And you cannot make this small again. That is what I did not understand at seventeen and could not have understood. Everything that happened afterward happened because I did not understand it.

One of the men in the boat with me in the first days was a small man who did not talk much, and who used to look at the water in a way I did not then understand. He was from the Cape and his name was Stubb. He had a pipe and he had a song for everything, and he was the calmest man I have ever been on a boat with, in circumstances that would have made me insane. And I asked him once, in the first week, how he could stand it. How he could stand being out there in a small boat with a whale coming up.

He thought about it a while, and then he said that he had been doing it a long time, and that the thing that had happened to him was that he had stopped thinking about it. And that he had found, when he stopped thinking about it, that he was happy. And that this was a very lucky thing that had happened to him and that it had probably ruined him for anything else.

I did not understand that then. I think about it now more than I think about the wreck.

And there was a man named Ahab, who had been out here forty years and who had lost a leg. And a man named Starbuck, who was the first mate and who had a boy of his own waiting at home. And a man named Queequeg, who was a harpooneer and who had come three thousand miles and had an arm made of writing. And a black man who went by the name of Fedallah, who never slept and watched the sea from the masthead by night, and who everybody said was not quite right in the head.

The cook was an old black man named Fleece, who was ninety years old and who could not cook. Not would not not cook. Could not. He cut the meat wrong and boiled it wrong and served it up wrong, and Stubb gave him a lecture on the deck of a whaler about how to cook a piece of whale, standing in the middle of the crew in the middle of the night, and it was one of the funniest and most dreadful things I have ever seen, and I have never been able to forget it, and I have never been able to work out whether he was laughing.

That is the crew. That is who it was. Thirty men and more. A carpenter who made a leg out of a piece of wood and a nail for a man who could not be saved. A blacksmith who made the irons that went into the whale. Two mates who did not agree about anything. And thirty men, most of whom had no particular reason to be there at all, and who went there anyway.

That last part is the one I cannot make fit into anything. Thirty men with no reason. Not one crusade. Not one protest. They went because it was a long voyage and there was money at the end of it, or because they were young and the sea looked bigger from the shore, or because they had been in a place where there was nothing and this was the next place. And then somewhere out in the Pacific the reason stopped mattering, and they were all doing one thing together, and they could not have told you what it was.

Look at it from the water. A ship, a small one, and in it thirty men, all pulling in the same direction for a thing they cannot see. That is what a mob is. That is what a religion is. That is what a country is. There is no difference between them except that one of them has a name for what it is doing, and the other three have to write a book about it afterwards to work out what happened.

And the sea goes on. And a year later another thirty men go out.

Here is the whole of what I saw, and I set it down plainly because I have no time for a fancier way of putting it. In a place called the Pacific, which is a real ocean and not the largest thing in the world but very large, a ship was put out of commission by an event that nobody aboard had any hand in. Thirty men and one leg between them went down. Of the people who went down, one was a man who had been out here forty years and who had lost a leg and who had a wife at home. One was a man who had a son. One was a black man from the Cape who had been on the water for forty years and who never once went below the horizon.

And there was one man who got away. He was a young man who had signed on for three-and-a-half shares out of a full hundred, which on the best day of the voyage was about enough money to buy a coat. He lay for a day and a night in a black water under a black sky with a half-dead man beside him, and there was nothing whatever to be done about any of it.

And of all of them, he was not the strongest and not the purest and not the best, and he had not been chosen for anything. He had been chosen by a series of accidents, each of which was smaller than the one before, the last of which was that he had gone to bed early.

I have had thirty years to think about what that means, and I have come to the following conclusion, which I set down without apology because I do not have a better one.

What survives a catastrophe is not the worthy and not the strong. What survives is whatever was standing slightly outside of it, for whatever reason, at the moment the two collided.

And that is not a lesson about virtue. It is a fact about how catastrophes work, and it is exactly the sort of fact that people do not want to hear, because it means that every one of us is walking about on ground that is thin, and we do not know the thickness of it, and the thickness is not a matter of character.

I found that out on the water and I have been carrying it since, and it is the reason I can sit down and write this, and the reason I start every time with my own name.

Not because I was brave. Because I was asleep.

And that is the only way anybody ever survives anything, and I have never been able to get past it, and I do not think it is meant to be got past. It is just true, and it is a very strange thing to have to say, and it is the one sentence in this account that I would ask anybody to argue with if they wanted to argue.
