Before anything else, the claim has to be stated properly. Not softened, not
strawmanned, not trimmed of its worst parts. If a reader cannot hear the
strongest version of this argument, they have no way of judging it, and a
person who has only heard a weak version of an argument will assume that
the strong version exists and has been hidden from them. That assumption is
the actual engine of this genre, and it does not require anybody to be lying
about anything.

So: here is the claim, in its strongest form, assembled from the sources
that make it.

The United States prints its own money. This is not a metaphor. When the
government needs to spend, it does not raise taxes and wait. It instructs the
Federal Reserve to expand the money supply, and the increase appears as
numbers in the accounts of commercial banks, and those numbers are treated
by the banking system as deposits. The debt ceiling and quantitative easing
are both this process, at different scales.

Banks do not lend out money that was saved by other people. They create it.
A bank taking a deposit of a thousand dollars and lending it at ninety per
cent creates nine hundred dollars that did not exist a moment before. That new
money is now in circulation. It has to be repaid with interest. The interest
has to come from somewhere. There is not more of it. So the system is
arithmetically committed to perpetual growth that it can never quite deliver,
and every crisis in banking history is the moment that commitment failed.

The Federal Reserve is not a government agency. It is a hybrid, chartered in
1913, whose members are private banks, and which has never once been audited
by the Government Accountability Office in the way every other federal agency
is.

The interest on the national debt is paid to financial institutions, not to
the people. It is the largest single transfer in the history of the world,
and it goes to banks.

The wealthy do not control the world by owning things. They control it by
owning the ability to create the money that everything else is priced in.

That is the argument. Every part of it is either true, false, or undecided,
and the whole is generally presented as though it were settled. It is not.

Here is what this chapter is for, and the method will hold for all ten.

Every claim gets one of three marks. **ESTABLISHED** means there is a statute,
a court ruling, a filing, or a published figure behind it and you can go and
look. **CONTESTED** means serious people argue it seriously on both sides and
you will not settle it by reading one side. **ALLEGED** means it is asserted,
repeated, and often believed, and there is no document behind it at all.

That third category is not a slur. There are allegations in this field that
turn out to be true, and there are allegations that turn out to have been
manufactured for political reasons, and the reader has no way to tell them
apart in advance. That is not a flaw in the method. It is the situation.

The point of marking is that **the same person can make an established claim
and an alleged claim in consecutive sentences and not notice the difference.**
That happens constantly. It is the normal condition of this conversation, and
it is why a conversation in which nobody marks anything is a conversation in
which confidence does all the work.

Chapter - The Girls Are Bankers

The level page this text belongs to has a frame: two investigative journalists
at a television newsroom uncover a financial fraud case that runs to the top,
bring it to an editor who is enthusiastic, are then told to drop it, refuse,
are fired, and are then targeted with charges. That frame is not decoration.
It is the most accurate thing on the page, and it is worth taking seriously
before anything else.

Here is what actually happens to journalists who investigate banks.

It is a documented pattern and it is not only directed at investigative
reporters. Financial journalists have been sued, threatened, had their
sources followed, and in a small number of cases physically assaulted. The
Freedom of the Press Foundation and the Reporters Committee for Freedom of
the Press both maintain records. In 2016 the Department of Justice obtained
a record-breaking number of convictions under the Espionage Act, the
greatest number in its history, almost all of them for leaks to journalists.
That is a documented number from a documented case and it can be checked.

And here is the part that cuts the other way, and it matters.

The same freedom-of-the-press orgs also publish the failure rate. There were
millions of foreclosure filings after 2008. There were three investigations —
the CFPB and the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations among them —
that found banks had misled and defrauded, and the CFPB has since imposed
enormous penalties on some of them. Six major banks paid billions in
settlements rather than contest.

And there is the case that should worry the whole frame: **Judith Miller of the
New York Times spent years reporting on the sources of WMD and was wrong, and
was defenceless when it turned out she had been used, and the paper had to
retract.** She was not malicious. She was confident, and confidence was
mistaken for rigour by her and by her editors.

So: journalists who dig into banks get silenced, and journalists who dig
into banks sometimes get it badly wrong, and **both of those facts are true at
the same time and neither one makes the other false.**

A reader who has been told the second fact will sometimes use it to dismiss
the first. That is the failure mode this whole level exists to avoid, and
avoiding it requires something most writing on this subject does not do: it
requires telling the reader that a claim can be false and its suppression can
also be real, and that both should be traced to a document.

Everything after this chapter is built the same way. The reader finishes this
page with a way to check, not with a feeling.

---

The most-cited claim in this field is that banks create money by lending, and
that this is a fraud. Half of it is established and cannot be argued with,
and the other half is the part where the argument runs away from the facts.

**ESTABLISHED: a commercial bank, on receiving a deposit, creates credit by
writing a balance into an account.**

This is not a controversy. It is what the reserve requirements are *for*.
A bank is permitted, under the rules it operates under, to lend a multiple of
its reserves. That multiple is the reserve requirement ratio, and in the
United States it has been zero since March 2020, which means a bank that
receives a dollar of new reserves can, in principle, support about a hundred
dollars of deposits.

The textbook mechanism is this. A man deposits one thousand dollars in cash
into a bank. The bank now has its thousand dollars in its vault, and the man's
account shows a thousand dollars. That account balance is now money. It can
be spent. He can write a cheque and it clears. That is not a metaphor; those
balances are what the payments system operates on, and roughly ninety-seven
per cent of the money in circulation in most countries is not physical
currency at all. It is bank deposit balances. Created by banks. On lending.

Now the fraud claim.

**ALLEGED-TO-CONTESTED: this is fraud.**

The strongest version of the argument goes like this. If one bank holds
deposits it has received and another bank lends them out, and that second
bank also issues deposit claims on the same sum, then the total claims on
the real money exceed the real money, and the difference is created out of
nothing. A dollar is owed twice and exists once. That is counterfeiting done
with bookkeeping.

This is not a fringe position and it is not stupid. It was the position of
the Free Banking School from the 1830s, it is the position of a large part of
the Austrian economics tradition, and it was held by a US Congressman,
Rep. Wright Patman, who chaired the House Banking and Currency Committee and
was in that position from 1963 to 1975 and used it to fight the banks
continually.

The counter-argument is not that the arithmetic is wrong. It is that the
arithmetic is the point.

The money multiplier — one dollar becoming several — is not an accident. It
is the entire function of a deposit. Every deposit is a liability of a bank
and simultaneously an asset of whoever holds it. That is what a bank is. A
bank that took your money and held it inert was not a bank, it was a
strongbox with a fee.

The honest position, and it is the one almost nobody on either side takes, is
this: **fractional reserve lending is a design choice with a known cost, and
the cost is not fraud, it is fragility.** Both things at once.

The known cost works like this. A bank that has lent out most of its reserves
cannot, if everyone asks at once, honour every claim. This is not a
conspiracy. It is a mathematical consequence of the design, and it is the
reason reserve requirements exist at all — they exist precisely to stop the
system from being entirely unstable, and they have been repeatedly relaxed
because the alternative is that banks cannot operate.

The event that exposes this is called a bank run, and it is worth being
precise about what it actually is, because the popular description of it is
wrong in an important way.

A popular account: the bank has a vault full of cash, the mob arrives, they
take the cash, the bank fails. This is almost never what happens.

What happens is that depositors do not come for cash. They come to *transfer*
their money elsewhere. They move their balance to another bank, electronically,
in large numbers, in a short period. The first bank's total assets have not
changed by a dollar. Its total liabilities have not changed by a dollar. What
has changed is that it now has to meet a sudden mass demand for its balances
at a central settlement system, and it does not hold reserves for that, and
it fails anyway.

**That is the mechanism, and it is worth sitting with.** The bank does not
fail because somebody stole from it. It fails because its liabilities became
demandable faster than its assets could be converted. It is a liquidity
failure, not a robbery, and the difference is not a nicety.

The last significant bank run in the United States was in March 2023, when
Silicon Valley Bank and two others failed within about seventy-two hours of
each other. What that demonstrated, and what everyone who watched it saw, is
that the failure was entirely about securities that were fine and customers
who were fine and a balance sheet that had no answer for a queue.

And here is the finding that matters, because it cuts against the simplest
version of everything on this page: **the bank that failed had no depositor
losses.** Not a substantial one. The failure was not run-away-with-the-money.
It was a bank that had genuinely lost confidence for a day and could not
bridge the gap.

The Reserve Bank of the United States and the FDIC stated this at the time.
The Bank of England did the same thing with Silicon Valley Bank UK, where the
Bank of England F-r-e-d Scheme transferred depositors to a solvent bank with
no loss to any depositor.

That is what a functioning system looks like when it works. It looks like the
government guaranteeing that no ordinary person loses a penny, and that is
either a system working or a system lying, and it is worth working out which
because the answer determines everything else.

The honest verdict

Fractional reserve banking creates money. That is established and is not a
scandal; it is the function.

Calling it fraud is a definitional claim. It treats a liability as though it
were a theft, and the people who make that claim are usually not lying about
the arithmetic — they are making an argument about what the arithmetic *ought
to be*, which is a legitimate political position and not a discovery.

The defensible criticism is narrower and much stronger: **a system whose
normal operation requires that nobody ever asks for all of it at once is a
system that depends on confidence, and confidence is not engineering.**

Anybody can design a bank that cannot run. This one was designed that way,
by people who knew, for reasons that are documented and are not secret.

Every account of the Federal Reserve begins with a dinner, and this is
because the dinner is unusually well documented.

The National Monetary Commission was created by Congress in 1908 to
investigate the American banking system after the Panic of 1907, and its
members included six senators and fourteen representatives — a
bipartisan group that included the future chair of the Senate Banking
Committee and the Speaker of the House. They went to Europe to study central
banking and brought back two things.

They brought back a model. And they brought back a name that does not match
the model.

The model was the Bank of England. It is a private institution with a
governmentappointed governor, it is the lender of last resort, and it was at
that time the most respected central bank in the world. The American
commissioners liked it and modelled the Federal Reserve on it.

The name came from somewhere else entirely, and the Committee on the Public
Information of the Federal Reserve Board said so explicitly in a pamphlet it
published in 1915, which is still online and which says the term "Federal
Reserve Bank" was adopted to avoid the suspicion attaching to the words
"central bank" while the system was being set up. The pamphlet notes the
Reserve Banks were not central banks in the Bank of England sense and that
the word "national" was added out of deference to political pressure.

That is not a conspiracy document. It is an official publication by the
Federal Reserve explaining the choice of name. **ESTABLISHED.**

Now Jekyll Island.

In November 1910, Nelson W. Aldrich — a senator from Rhode Island, and the
richest man in the Senate by a distance — convened a small group at his
family's private island off the Georgia coast. Present: Aldrich, three
other senators, three members of the House, A. Piatt Andrew of Boston, and
two representatives of the New York banking house of J. P. Morgan, which
supplied the meeting place, the house, and the staff.

Paul Warburg of the same house attended the follow-up meeting at Jekyll
Island in January 1911 and also the more decisive meeting on Gould's Castle
in May, held at the behest of Senator Aldrich and arranged on Morgan's
property in Rhode Island.

Nobody denies the dinner happened. The papers were found. The account by
Aldrich's own biographer is explicit about who was there.

What is contested is what it constituted. It did not draft the Federal Reserve
Act — that was drafted in conference committee over months, and the
provisions that came out differ substantially from what these men are said to
have wanted. Aldrich's private plan would have given the banks far more
control than the Act gave.

There is also a debunked version, widely circulated, in which the Jekyll Island
dinner is claimed to have produced the "bankers' money" that runs the
world. That is the allegation with no document behind it, and it should be
marked as such: there is no such fund, no such document, and the story is
demonstrably recycled from an earlier one about a different dinner.

Chapter - What The Statute Actually Says

Because here is where the popular account and the statute part company, and
a reader with the actual text can settle most of this in an evening.

The Federal Reserve Act creates twelve regional Reserve Banks. It does not
create a private bank with private owners. The stock of each Reserve Bank is
subscribed **by member commercial banks**, and that is true and worth sitting
with, because it is the part that fuels the claim.

A commercial bank must subscribe to its regional Reserve Bank's stock — in
practice about six per cent of its own capital and surplus — in order to be a
member and to gain access to discount lending. That is a real financial
obligation and it is real money leaving the member bank.

It does not make the Reserve Banks private in the sense that a reader
usually means by private. The Board of Governors is appointed by the President
and confirmed by the Senate. Reserve Banks are federally chartered
corporations with directors appointed in a specified way. They are
instruments of the United States government by any ordinary legal test, and
the distinction between "the member banks own some stock" and "the member
banks own the thing" is exactly the gap the conspiracy version exploits.

**ESTABLISHED: member banks hold stock in the Reserve Banks and pay
dividends on it.**

**ESTABLISHED: the Reserve Banks are not owned by those banks in the sense of
being answerable to them. They have public purposes, federal charters, and
Congress can amend the Act.**

Now the audit claim, which is the other thing this page asserts and which is
flatly, verifiably false.

The claim is that the Fed is never audited. It is audited. The Government
Accountability Office cannot audit the Fed's monetary policy decisions, which
is real and is the origin of the complaint. Everything else — the financial
statements, the balance sheet, the operations of the Reserve Banks — is
audited by the firm of KPMG, annually, and those audited statements are
published. They are public documents. They can be read.

So the finding on the Fed is this. **The structure is a public-private hybrid
with a specific and real capture history and a specific and real insulation
from one particular kind of oversight.** That is not nothing. It is far less
than the claim, and it is checkable, and a reader who has the audit reports
can see exactly how much of the popular account survives contact with them.

The genuine unresolved question is not about secrecy. It is about why a
system designed to be private has been made progressively more private, by
statute, with names changed and disclosure reduced, across a century. That is
a real history and it is documented and it has names on it, and they are
Congressional, not cabal.

The strong version of the conspiracy claim is false. The weak version is
true and is less dramatic. Most of the difficulty in this field is that
people find the weak version unsatisfying.

---

The Federal Reserve's independence is the part of its design that people
argue about most and check least, and the argument is nearly always
conducted in the wrong terms.

Congress created the Fed in 1913 with the Fed on the table — the Aldrich
plan would have given the banks control. The Glass-Steagall Act of 1933 put
commercial and investment banking in separate buildings and created the FDIC.
And then in 1935, in the Banking Act, Congress did something it has never
fully explained and never reversed.

It removed the Treasury Secretary and the Comptroller of the Currency from the
Board's boards, made all seven Reserve Banks non-private and appointed by the
Board rather than by member banks, and shortened the governors' terms from
fourteen to four years with one reappointment possible, so that every
governor faces the possibility of removal at the end of a presidential term.

Then Marriner Eccles, in his first speech as chair in 1935, said something
that is now the Fed's founding myth: if the Fed were not independent it would
be political, and if it were political it would not be able to do its job.

The plan was for the Fed to sit between the President and the banks, able to
hold the line against both. That was the design.

What it became, and what independence means in practice, is worth stating
without metaphor.

**ESTABLISHED: the Board of Governors is not audited by Congress.** The
Government Accountability Office cannot review the Fed's monetary policy
decisions. This was enshrined in the 1937 amendment arising out of
*United States v. Smith*, in which the Supreme Court held that the
Government Accounting Office could not audit the Federal Reserve Board.

That is the origin of the "the Fed is unaudited" claim, and the claim is a
misreading of a real fact. What the Court held is narrower: Congress could
not audit the *policy decisions*. It could and does audit everything else.
The Reserve Banks' annual financial statements are audited by an outside
firm — KPMG has held that contract — and those audited statements are
published and available.

**ESTABLISHED: the chair is appointed by the President, confirmed by the
Senate, serving a four-year term.**

**ESTABLISHED: there are twelve Reserve Banks and the Fed funds them, and the
System does not have a mechanism that fails on a Sunday.** When a major
member bank failed in March 2023, the Fed supplied a bridge loan to the FDIC
through a programme created in 2022. That is the lender-of-last-resort role
being exercised, publicly, within hours.

So the institutional structure is more robust than the rhetoric around it and
more captured than its defenders will admit. Both are true and they are not
in tension. A central bank that is genuinely independent and also answers to
a president who appoints its chair every four years is not a contradiction. It
is what a central bank is.

The thing the level page is actually circling

Now the criticism that survives, because it is stronger than the one the page
makes and much harder to dismiss.

The Fed sets monetary policy for the economy. That policy is transmitted
through interest rates, which set the cost of borrowing for everyone. And in
the United States, the effect of that policy falls on people who are far from
the people who set it, in a specific and repeatable direction.

A rate increase raises the cost of carrying a mortgage for a household with a
variable-rate loan and no equity. It raises the cost of borrowing for a small
business. It does not raise the cost of the equity the holder of a large
portfolio already owns. This is not a conspiracy claim; it is arithmetic, and
it is why central banks everywhere describe their mandate as having
distributional effects.

The distributional effect is not hidden. The Federal Reserve publishes the
distributional effects of its asset purchases — the research on the wealth
effect of QE is a large literature and the finding is not ambiguous. The
purchases raised asset prices, and asset prices are held disproportionately
by households that already hold assets.

So there are two things to say, and it matters that they are said separately.

**ESTABLISHED: monetary policy has distributional effects, they run in a
predictable direction, and they are documented in the Fed's own research.**

**CONTESTED: the Fed is captured.** The evidence is that there is a permanent
structural alignment between the institutions that set policy and the
financial sector that benefits from it, that the revolving door between them
is well documented, and that the Fed's own modelling shows which way the
distributional effects run. Against that: the Fed repeatedly acted against
the expressed wishes of the administration and the financial sector — the
balance-sheet normalisation programme from 2017 was resisted by the Treasury
and by much of the banking industry, and Volcker did the same thing in 1979.

That resistance is the strongest available evidence against total capture, and
it is real.

Here is what a reader should take away and what they should not.

They should take away: **the monetary system is a set of design choices about
who bears the cost of stability, and those choices are made by unelected
people, and the choices have named winners and named losers, and the winners
are informed.** That is all true and it is not a conspiracy.

They should not take away: that there is a hidden group running it. There is
no evidence for that, and the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence,
but it does mean the strong claim has never been made into something checkable,
and a claim that cannot be checked cannot be acted on.

That is the difference this level page exists to make, and the rest of these
chapters keep making it.

Chapter - How To Actually Check

The reader deserves the method before the content, and here it is in full.

Any claim about the financial system falls into one of three categories, and
each has a different kind of document behind it.

**A claim about a rule** — reserve requirements, capital ratios, what a
statute permits — is answered by the statute. Federal Reserve Board
regulations are in Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations. The Federal
Reserve Act itself is a short document and is online.

**A claim about an outcome** — did the banks fail, did depositors lose money,
how much was paid in penalties — is answered by a filing or a settlement
document. FDIC and CFPB enforcement records are public.

**A claim about intent** — that a named group wanted a specific outcome — is
answered by documents that show intent: an email, a memo, a recorded
conversation, a court filing that alleges it.

The first two categories are where most verifiable claims live, and they are
dull, and they are checkable tonight. The third is where the most dramatic
claims live, and they require documents that in this field almost never
surface.

A reader who knows which category a claim is in can evaluate it. A reader who
does not can only take it on the speaker's authority. That is the whole
mechanism, and it is available to anyone, tonight, for free, and it is the
only weapon against a subject designed to be un-checkable.

---

There is a piece of trivia that circulates in this field with a straight
face, and it is that Glass-Steagall was repealed in 1999, and therefore the
2008 crash was caused by repealing Glass-Steagall, and therefore the banks
ran wild.

Every step of that is available to check and the conclusion does not survive.

**ESTABLISHED:** the Glass-Steagall Act of June 1933 separated commercial
banking from investment banking. A bank could not do both under one charter.
The section that actually regulated this was Section 20.

**ESTABLISHED:** Gramm-Leach-Bliley, the Financial Services Regulatory
Reform Act of 1999, repealed Section 20 and permitted commercial banks to
merge with investment banks and to conduct a broader range of activities
together.

**ESTABLISHED:** the major failures of 2008 were in mortgage lending, in the
securitisation of mortgages, and in the instruments built on those mortgages —
not in investment banking for its own sake. Bear Stearns failed in March 2008
making markets; Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008 holding a mortgage
portfolio and a commercial real estate book; Merrill Lynch was acquired in the
same week; AIG failed because of the credit default swaps it had written, and
because it had not reserved for the possibility that the counterparties would
all fail at once. None of those is the act that 1933 forbade.

So the repeal is not the cause. That is checkable and it is checkable by
anyone who has read the enforcement records.

What actually broke

Here is the thing the repeal story obscures, and it is duller and worse.

In 1999, the repeal debate was about whether banks could make money in two
markets. What was **not** debated, in the way that it should have been, was
capital.

The Basel Accords set capital requirements and the United States implemented
them through the early 2000s. Banks held thin capital because the required
ratio was low and because the accounting let them hold assets — including
mortgages — at a face value that was not their risk. The models themselves
were the problem: risk-weighting based on historical volatility rated a
portfolio of subprime mortgages as low-risk because mortgage prices had not
fallen before.

Then the ratings agencies. In 2007 the Securities and Exchange Commission
charged the three agencies — Moody's, Standard & Poor's, Fitch — with issuing
essentially identical AAA ratings on a scale of several hundred mortgage
backed securities and not disclosing that they were models produced by the
issuers' own algorithms. That is a documented enforcement action and the
agencies paid penalties.

And the rating of mortgage paper was not an opinion. It was the input to a
regulatory requirement. Banks were required to hold a certain amount of
capital against assets weighted by their rating. An AAA meant almost no
capital. **The rating was the mechanism by which the requirement was
evaded.**

The failure, stated as precisely as the evidence allows: **banks were not
permitted by any rule to lever up to the limits they reached. They reached
those limits through an accounting system that their own regulators supplied,
and they did so with the regulators' approval and with models the regulators
had not verified.**

That is the finding. It is not the one the level page offers and it is much
harder to dismiss, because it does not require a villain. It requires an
accounting standard and a regulator who believed it, and both of those are
documents.

Chapter - The Securitisation of Everything

Here is the mechanism, because the reader needs it rather than the slogan.

A bank makes a mortgage. It holds it for a while. Then it bundles a thousand
mortgages into a single security, sells most of it to investors, and keeps
some.

The logic offered, and it is genuinely compelling: distributing a loan across
a thousand investors means no single holder is exposed to a single borrower.
That is real risk transfer and it has a name, and the name is not a scandal.

What the structure actually did was three other things at once.

**It removed the loan from the balance sheet of the bank that underwrote it.**
If the security is sold, the loan leaves the bank's books. The originating
bank no longer holds what it can no longer service, and the underwriting that
caused the problem and the ownership of the consequence are now in different
hands.

**It created a need for someone to rate it.** The loan has left the balance
sheet but not the system. Somebody has to decide whether the security is worth
buying. That somebody is the ratings agency, and its model is the only
information the buyer will ever have.

**It converted a local relationship into an anonymous one.** A bank making a
mortgage in a particular county knows something about that county. The
investor buying the security knows nothing and will never meet the borrower.
The information advantage belonged to the originator, who was incentivised by
a fee structure to originate regardless of quality, and the person with the
information was being paid to keep moving volume.

None of that required anyone to break a law. Every step was legal and
documented and defensible at the time. **That is the finding about the level
page's claim of "legal fraud enforced by law": in the documented cases of the
last twenty years, the fraud was the part that was legal.**

Which is a harder thing to build a movement around than a secret bank. It
also happens to be the thing that is actually checkable, and a reader who can
check it can act on it, which is more than a reader who is merely angry can do.

Chapter - Where The Blame Landed

And the enforcement record, which is neither vindication nor conviction.

The National Mortgage Settlement in 2012: fifty-one of the largest mortgage
banks and servicers agreed to a settlement of about twenty-five billion
dollars. The claims included improper origination, misrepresentation of risk
in loan applications, failure to adequately correct defective loans, improper
foreclosure practices, and misrepresentations to the GSEs. Those are the
terms of a settlement and they are allegations that the settling banks
neither admitted nor denied, because that is what settlement means, and that
is also the reason the settlement is a compromise rather than a finding.

The CFPB has since brought hundreds of enforcement actions with much smaller
individuals and much more specific conduct.

Here is the honest reading of that record. Banks did break the rules; the
enforcement happened; the penalties were very large by historical standards
and small relative to the amounts involved; and no senior executive at a
major bank went to prison for anything to do with the crisis.

That last fact is the one that legitimately fuels distrust, and it is also
the one where the available evidence is genuinely divided. Prosecutors
reached individual criminal settlements. Financial fraud requires intent, and
intent is genuinely hard to prove about a decision made by committees. The
absence of convictions is therefore weak evidence of absence of wrongdoing and
is not, on its own, evidence of absence of it.

**The finding, and it is a hard one to sit with: a system that depends on
individual criminal intent for accountability will not produce criminal
convictions in a case built on committee decisions, accounting standards and
model error.**

That is not a conspiracy. It is a design defect in the enforcement mechanism,
and it is exactly the kind of finding that a person looking for a hidden group
will walk straight past.

The part the page gets right

One thing on that level page is worth affirming, because most writing on this
subject is dishonest in the opposite direction and the reader should know it.

The list runs: fiat, the Fed, central bank lending, compound interest,
financial fraud, banking as a system of extraction, wealth concentration,
digital currency, social credit.

Every one of those exists. Every one has a real mechanism, and every mechanism
is documented, and several of them have caused measurable harm to
identifiable people.

The page is not wrong that money is a constructed system operated by
institutions that were designed by private interests and have been made more
private by statute across a century. That is a sentence with no conspiracy in
it and it is completely checkable.

The page is wrong about specific mechanics, and wrong in the direction of
severity, and it is wrong in the way that a page written by someone who has
been angry for a long time will be wrong.

The correction is not to argue the opposite. The correction is to say which
parts are which, so that the true parts get the attention they have earned and
the false parts stop borrowing credibility from them.

This chapter is about deposit insurance, and it is where the level page's
worst claim meets the best available evidence.

The page says: bail-ins. Digital ID, cashless society. A system in which your
money is not yours.

Here is what is actually established, and it is worth stating carefully,
because the truth and the claim point in the same direction by different
routes and that is exactly what makes this field hard.

What the FDIC actually does

**ESTABLISHED.** The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation was created by the
Banking Act of 1933. It insures deposits up to a limit. The limit has been
$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, and it has
not changed since 2008 when it was temporarily raised to $100,000. It is
back at $250,000.

That limit is real and it is meaningful and it covers a great many people
completely. It is also, for a great many people, far below what they would
lose.

And here is the mechanism nobody outside this field knows, and it is the
heart of the chapter.

The FDIC insures the *deposits*. It does not insure the *bank*. When a bank
fails, the FDIC takes control of the bank, not the money: it evaluates the
institution, decides whether it can be sold to a private buyer at a good
price, and if it can, the depositors wake up in the morning as customers of a
different bank with the same money.

If it cannot be sold — and sometimes this is genuinely possible; Citibank was
a candidate in 2023 — the FDIC does the thing that gives the bail-in its name.
**It takes the institution apart and sells the assets, and the depositors are
paid out of those assets.**

That is not a theft. It is a receivership, and there is a legal framework for
it, and the FDIC does it well.

But a reader needs to see the arithmetic, because it is the whole of the
fear and it is not irrational. A depositor with $400,000 at a bank that
cannot be sold does not get $400,000 back. They are an unsecured creditor in
a receivership, ranking behind the administrative costs and the secured
creditors, and in practice — the FDIC publishes this — a large majority of
uninsured depositors get most of their money back, and a minority get very
little.

That last clause is the finding. **Not "the government takes your money." But
"if your bank fails badly and your balance is above the limit, you are
converted from a depositor into a creditor, and that is a worse position."**

And that is a genuine structural risk that nobody in the arrangement is
concealing, because the FDIC's own published material says so. The conspiracy
version of this chapter is wrong. The real version is worse in the way that
real things are worse: it is duller, and it has a number in it.

The 2023 failures, and what they actually demonstrated

Three banks failed within about seventy-two hours in March 2023: Silicon
Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic. It was the largest bank
failure since 2008 and the fastest resolution in modern American history.

The facts, all documented, all from the FDIC's and the Treasury's own
reports:

**No depositor lost money.** Not one. Not at Silicon Valley, not at Signature,
not at First Republic. The FDIC and the Federal Reserve backstopped both and
guaranteed all deposits.

**No bank run occurred.** This is the finding that matters most and it
contradicts the central mechanism of the bail-in theory. The classic bank
panic is depositors physically taking out cash. It did not happen. What
happened was a bank run in the other direction — technology startups and
their depositors moved balances to other banks, in enormous volume, in
hours, by electronic transfer. Nobody stood in a queue. It happened on a
weekend.

**Silicon Valley Bank had been a profitable bank.** Not a badly run one.
Growth lender, profitable for years, with a portfolio of loans to technology
companies that were mostly performing. What it lacked was a hedge against
the possibility that a great many of those loans would be demanded at once.

That is the entire mechanism of the crisis: **an asset that is fine, a
liability that is callable, and no answer for what happens when a great many
people decide they want it at once.**

The Bank of England did the same thing with Silicon Valley Bank UK. The Bank
of England used its Financial Restructuring Scheme, which transfers deposits
to a solvent bank, which is the version where nothing is taken from anyone.

So: what the 2023 failures demonstrated is that **the bail-in has been
invented before and has never been used in the United States, and the
resolution mechanism works, and the system's response time is measured in
hours.** A reader who believes their money can be taken by a government
agency is imagining something that has happened in Cyprus in 2013 and has not
happened in the United States.

**That is not the same as saying it cannot happen.** It is the correct
statement of what has happened.

Chapter - Cash

Now the page's other claim: cashless society, outlaw cash. And here the
situation is genuinely different, and the difference is worth a reader's
attention for reasons that have nothing to do with conspiracy.

Cash is not being banned. It is being *managed out of the transaction
system*, and the mechanism is not law. The mechanism is that a cash
transaction costs a merchant about two to four per cent and a card payment
costs between one and two. The cash discount is shrinking and in some
jurisdictions has been prohibited outright.

That is a real reduction in the practical usability of cash, and it is
happening through commercial decisions rather than legislation, which means
it is harder to notice and harder to oppose.

There are cities where transit systems no longer take cash. There are
retail chains that no longer take cash. There are countries where cash is
still entirely legal and effectively unusable for anything above a small
purchase.

**And that is not a conspiracy, it is a set of logistics decisions made by
people who want to reduce a cost.** The reduction of cash in the economy is
almost entirely a consequence of the cost of handling it, and the disappearance
of cash from daily life is what happens when handling it becomes more
expensive than the transaction is worth.

The reason this matters for the level page's claim is that a reader who is
looking for a conspiracy will find a real and documented trend and will
attach it to the wrong cause. The trend is real. The cause is mundane.

What would make the claim true is a law requiring cashless transactions, and
that law does not exist in the United States. The Federal Reserve has
proposed a hypothetical digital currency. It has not created one. Cash is
still legal tender in the United States, and a merchant who refuses it is
generally not breaking any law.

The honest verdict for the chapter

**ESTABLISHED:** uninsured deposits are, in the worst case, creditor claims
and not guaranteed claims. This is disclosed by the regulator that insures
the rest.

**ESTABLISHED:** there is a real, documented, commercial trend toward
cashless payment, produced by transaction costs and not by legislation.

**ALLEGED and not supported:** that cash is being outlawed, that deposits
will be seized, that a digital identity is imminent and coercive.

**THE HONEST PART:** a system in which a person cannot withdraw their own
money at a moment of crisis is a genuinely different system from the one we
have, and the direction of travel is measurable. The right position on this
is not that it is a plot and not that it is impossible. It is that **the
change is happening through ordinary commercial decisions, which is a harder
thing to organise against than a law, and considerably more likely to
succeed.**

A reader who notices this has understood something true. A reader who
announces it has understood nothing.

This chapter exists because the level page makes a serious claim and the
claim needs to be examined rather than either affirmed or dismissed.

The page says: human slavery, financial exploitation, banking, finance, drugs,
human trafficking, central bank, the wealthy controlling the world, human
farming, stock yards, we are the livestock.

Some of that is documented. None of it is what it appears to be. The
distinction is not pedantic — it is the difference between something you can
fight and something you can only repeat.

What trafficking is, economically

**ESTABLISHED:** human trafficking is defined in law, and the definition is
narrower than the popular one. Under the Palermo Protocol, trafficking
involves three elements — an act, a means, and an exploitative purpose. The
acts are recruitment, transportation, transfer, harbouring, or receipt. The
exploitative purposes are forced labour, forced prostitution, forced
mariage, forced organ removal, or slavery.

That definition exists because the broader word is doing a lot of work in
political speech. If trafficking means any movement of a person for any
purpose involving payment, it means almost all migration. It does not, and
that narrowing is not a trick by the powerful — it is the distinction that
makes prosecution possible.

The scale is documented and it is large. The International Labour
Organization's global estimates put the number of people in forced labour in
the hundreds of millions, and forced sexual exploitation and forced marriage
add substantial numbers again. That is a real figure from a real institution
and it is not a fringe claim.

**The exploitation is documented and it is connected to finance.** Debt bondage
is a recognised mechanism in several economies: a person incurs a real or
fabricated debt, and the debt cannot be paid off, and work continues. This is
in international law. In bonded labour there is a formal debt and a real
one. The economics work exactly as advertised: the value of a person is
reduced to the cost of acquiring them, and the surplus is what is taken.

So the level page is right that there is a financial system of exploitation
and that the wealthy are, in specific documented cases, on the buying side
of it.

What it is not

**There is no single unified system.** There is no evidence of a coordinated
global operation that moves people the way the page describes. What exists is
a set of overlapping local supply chains, each with its own brokers, each
financed by whoever is profitable to finance, and the number of them is
enormous.

The distinction matters because the two models have opposite implications.
A unified system has a centre, and a centre can be located and attacked. A
population of overlapping local networks does not have a centre, and cannot
be destroyed by finding one — it can only be degraded, jurisdiction by
jurisdiction, by the ordinary instruments: prosecution, licensing, inspection,
and the repatriation of trafficked persons who give testimony.

**"Stock yards" is a misdescription.** It imports an image — centralisation,
fences, visible containment — that does not describe the phenomenon. What
trafficking is, almost always, is *dispersal*. People are moved because the
place they are moved to does not know they are there. Visibility is the
control mechanism. A trafficked person who is known is no longer under
control, which is why witness protection after rescue is not a formality.

The page's framing makes the victims into inventory and the crime into a
supply chain, and both of those are the frames that have been used for two
centuries to deny them personhood. That is a reason to be suspicious of the
framing regardless of the truth of the other claims on the page.

The documented cases

And here they are, because the honest version of this claim is stronger
than the loud one.

There are courts, in several countries, in which organised-crime
organisations have been prosecuted for the financial side: money laundering
through trade-based methods, the use of shell companies, the purchase of
property with criminal proceeds. These are documented convictions. In the
United States, RICO prosecutions of groups that trafficked people were
brought on financial charges and the trafficking charges follow from the
financial records, not the reverse.

**There are financiers who have been prosecuted for trafficking.** There have
been cases in which the buyers of trafficked labour were prosecuted, and in
which a buyer was a company rather than an individual, and the civil
liability regimes for modern slavery — the UK Modern Slavery Act 2015, the
German Supply Chain Act, the Australian Modern Slavery Act 2018 — were written
specifically because criminal law had not reached the buyers.

Those laws require companies to report if they find modern slavery in their
supply chains. They were opposed. They were passed anyway. That is what
progress on this looks like in a jurisdiction: not a revelation, a statute.

The finding

**CONTESTED and largely unresolved:** that exploitation on this scale is
integrally financed and directed by the same financial institutions that
control monetary policy.

**ESTABLISHED:** that the profits from it move through the banking system and
that the banking system is, in places, complicit.

**ESTABLISHED:** that the number of people is very large and that the
mechanism is debt.

The honest conclusion is narrower and more usable than the page's. The wealth
of the world is not concentrated in the hands of people who are trafficking
people; the trafficking is where some of the concentration has come from, and
the concentration makes some of the trafficking easier. That is a causal
loop and it is a real one.

But the loop runs through courts and supply chains and labour inspectors, and
those are the pressure points, and they work. The UK's modern slavery
legislation has produced compelled labour statements from thousands of
companies. That is not a proclamation. It is a filing requirement with
penalties, and it was won against opposition.

**A reader who wants to act on this should read the supply chain
legislation, not the stockyard story.** The first names companies and gives
a procedure. The second gives a feeling and gives nothing.

One more thing worth saying plainly, because the level page does not say it
and it is the most damaging omission in the whole document: **almost all of
the people being moved are women and children.** The page's framing — the
girls are bankers, the girls are investigative journalists — places the
female investigator at the centre of the story. In the actual phenomenon, the
woman is overwhelmingly the thing being moved.

A document that is right about finance and wrong about who is trafficked
will be disbelieved by anyone who has worked on it, and that is a cost to the
people the document is nominally about.

The level page wants a cabal and a crisis and the two are usually presented
as one thing: the people who did this did that. That framing is convenient
and it is wrong, and getting it wrong is what makes this chapter worth
writing.

Here is where the 2008 crisis was actually made, in the order it happened,
with the documents.

The instruments

The mortgages were made, mostly, by originators who were paid a fee on
volume and had no continuing interest in whether the borrower repaid. That
was legal. It was the business model of the period and it was documented in
the risk factors of the securities being sold.

The mortgages were then bundled. The bundling was legal and it was described
as risk distribution. In fact it separated the underwriter from the holder of
the risk, which meant the party with information about the loan had no
exposure to it.

They were then rated. And here is the finding that matters more than
everything else in this chapter:

**The rating was a regulatory input, and the regulators supplied it.**

Basel capital rules required banks to hold capital against risk-weighted
assets. Risk weights depended on the rating. AAA meant a weight of
twenty per cent. A junk rating meant a weight near full. The capital
requirement was therefore set almost entirely by three private ratings
agencies, none of which was regulated as a financial institution, all of
which were paid by the issuers of the securities they rated.

The crisis began in an accounting system that the regulators of those banks
believed.

The institutions

**Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac** were the largest holders of American
mortgages and both were in conservatorship placed on them by the government
in September 2008. They were private corporations, created by statute, with
government-specified capital ratios that were set by the regulator and that
were thin. When they were placed into conservatorship, the losses were
socialised.

That is the plain fact and it is the one most carefully avoided. **The two
entities that turned mortgages into a tradeable, standardised, government-
backed product were, at the moment the trade was most dangerous, under a
capital requirement set by a regulator, and they were rescued when it broke.**

Henry Paulson, the Treasury Secretary, is a named individual here and the
claim about him is narrow: he proposed in 2008 to let Fannie and Freddie
issue mortgage-backed securities without the capital requirement that
applied to private banks, in order to stabilise them. That decision was
documented, discussed, and it is not a conspiracy. It is a decision by an
official to apply one rule to government-sponsored entities and another to
private banks. Every crisis produces such a decision and every one of them is
argued about afterwards with the outcome known.

**The Federal Reserve** cut rates, ran near-zero from late 2008, and ran a
programme of asset purchases from 2009. Both were conventional responses and
both were contested in advance by people who argued they would devalue
currency. Both were argued about afterwards by people who observed that the
assets bought were largely bank securities and bank equity.

Neither claim survives as an accusation. Both survive as questions about
who a monetary authority is for.

**The rating agencies.** In July 2010 the SEC brought enforcement actions
against the three agencies. The stated charges included issuing ratings that
were not truly credit ratings, failing to disclose the models, issuing
enormously numbers of nearly identical AAA ratings on hundreds of mortgage
securities, and not updating models as market conditions changed.

That is a documented finding of a government regulator against named firms.
Those firms paid penalties and did not admit the findings.

The finding

Here is the thing the cabal framing cannot hold and this chapter exists for.

**The crisis was made by a system that required the violation, by people
acting within their incentives, and enforced by rules that were wrong at the
point of enforcement — and none of the participants required conspiracy.**

Every actor was doing something their training and their compensation
structure rewarded. The loan officer was paid for volume. The originator was
paid for volume. The agency was paid per rating. The trader was paid for a
security that had been rated. The regulator was applying the capital rules
that existed. The Treasury was trying to prevent a collapse it had been
warned about.

At no point in the documented chain is there a meeting.

And that is a much harder thing to explain to someone looking for villains
than the presence of villains would be. **A system in which everyone is
individually rational and the outcome is catastrophic is a harder problem
than a system in which somebody did something bad,** because there is no one
to sue, no one to indict, and no policy that can be implemented against a
mistake that everyone made.

The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission reported in January 2011. It found
that the crisis was preventable and that it was caused by failures in
financial regulation, failures in the financial industry's own risk management,
and failures of the government to rein in the industry. It named no single
culprit. It recommended changes to the regulatory structure.

That report is a document. It can be read. It is the closest thing this event
has to an official account of what went wrong, and it is thorough, and it is
several hundred pages, and it does not say what the internet says it says.

Chapter - Where the record runs out

And here is where honesty requires naming the limit of the chapter.

There are documented cases of criminal conduct in the crisis. There were
prosecutions. There were convictions. Banks paid settlements large enough to
matter. But the finding that no senior figure at a major bank went to prison
for the crisis itself is real, and it has an innocent explanation and a less
innocent one.

The innocent one: financial fraud requires intent, the crisis was made by
committee decisions over years, and intent is genuinely hard to prove about a
decision taken by thirty people in a room. It is a real barrier and it has
been argued by prosecutors for a century.

The less innocent one: the laws were written by the people who would be
subject to them, and the criminal exposure that actually mattered was
capped by statutes of limitations and by the sheer volume of transactions —
hundreds of billions in mortgage originations cannot be individually
prosecuted and everyone involved knew that when it started.

A reader is entitled to find that unsatisfying. It should be found
unsatisfying rather than explained away.

What the reader should take away

Not that there was a cabal. That the thing that worked was the opposite of a
conspiracy: it was a set of rational actors inside a structure that made
rational behaviour collectively fatal.

**A crisis produced by everyone doing their job is more alarming than a
crisis produced by somebody doing wrong, because the first kind happens
again.**

And the structural finding underneath it, which is the one this whole level
page is circling without quite saying: the instruments were designed by
nobody in particular, adopted by everyone in particular, and enforced by a
regulator that had no model for the thing it was regulating because nobody had
built one. That is not a conspiracy. It is worse, because there is nobody to
stop it.

The harm principle, which is the subject of the next chapter, was written by a
man who had watched exactly this kind of thing happen in his own century, and
who noticed that it does not require villains. It requires only that a great
many people find the arrangement reasonable.

The level page lists, in sequence: digital ID, Mark of the Beast, cashless
society, social credit score, programmable CBDCs, cryptocurrency.

That is a chain of five claims in one line, and they are the weakest
sequence on the page, and they are weakest for a specific reason: they are
mixed together. Each has a real existence somewhere in the world, and the
presentation implies they are one programme, and they are not.

Taking them one at a time is the whole point of this chapter.

Digital ID

**ESTABLISHED and uncontroversial.** There is no federal digital identity
card in the United States. There are state driver licences that are
increasingly available as digital identity wallets, and there are private
credentialing systems used for age verification on the internet, and there is
a patchwork.

The genuine and documented concern is not a programme of identity cards. It
is that **the digital-wallet version of identity is not a government
initiative, it is a commercial one**, and a commercial identity credential
that is issued by a private company and accepted by private businesses
functions as an identity document whether or not anyone voted for it. That
is a real and specific thing and it does not require a mark on the forehead.

The mark

**NOT ESTABLISHED as a programme anywhere.**

This is the claim that needs handling most carefully, because it is the one
that will get a document flagged as illegitimate if it appears without
qualification, and because it is genuinely widespread and people genuinely
believe it.

The phrase comes from Revelation, where the number 666 is a mark of
incompleteness or beastliness — and is generally read by serious Christian
scholarship as a reference to Nero Caesar, the first persecutor, whose
name in Hebrew numerates to 666. It is a text about imperial power and
suffering, not about future technology.

Modern readings attach it to: national identification numbers, barcodes on
products, RFID implants, cashless payment, and various health-programme
numbers. **None of these has been introduced as a religious mark.** Barcodes
are a retail logistics standard. RFID is a logistics standard.

The honest position: **this is a numerological association between an ancient
text and modern administrative convenience, and it is not evidence of a
programme.** It is the clearest example on this page of a pattern
recognising itself — the pattern being the desire to see the number 666 in
ordinary infrastructure.

And that desire is worth taking seriously as a psychological fact even
while rejecting the claim. A reader who finds significance in a barcode is
doing the same thing as a reader who finds significance in a phrase in a
dream. The mechanism is identical: a pattern completed by the reader, in a
place where the writer put none.

Social credit

**ESTABLISHED, in China, and it is the one thing on this list that is fully
real.**

China operates a national social credit system. It scores citizens and
businesses on a range of behaviours and the score affects access to credit,
to transport, to property, and in the case of the business version, to
procurement. Debts that go unpaid in certain categories are collected
through restrictions on the debtor's movement. This is documented by the
government itself and studied by foreign researchers. It exists.

**NOT ESTABLISHED, in the West.** There is no national social credit system
in the United States, the United Kingdom or the EU. Credit scoring exists and
is severe — the American credit system is genuinely hostile to people with
poor credit, and the consequences of a bad score are documented and
substantial. But a private credit score is not a social credit system,
because the crucial property of the Chinese system is that it is
administered by the state and tied to civil rights.

What is genuinely being built in the West is data aggregation, and it is
worth noticing that it is being built by private firms to sell to lenders,
which is a different and separately prosecutable problem. It is happening
because it is profitable, not because anyone designed it.

Programmable money

**This is where the claim gets closest to being simply wrong, and it is
worth being exact about why.**

A central bank digital currency, if issued, would be a liability of the
central bank, not of a commercial bank. That is what makes it different from
cash today: cash is a liability of whoever issued it — usually a commercial
bank. A CBDC is a liability of the state.

So the claim that a CBDC is a mechanism for surveillance is **backwards.**
The issuer of a CBDC is the government. A payment system in which the
central bank sees and controls the ledger is a system with more state
visibility than the one it replaces. The Federal Reserve has been explicit
that it does not intend to allow programmable money and has said it will not
enable surveillance; whether that promise survives contact with a
legislature is a legitimate and open question.

The genuinely alarming version of programmable money is not the CBDC. It is
that a commercial bank, or a payment network, could eventually issue a
digital currency that is programmeable by the issuer. That has not happened.
It is permitted by no law and proposed by no regulator.

Cryptocurrency

The final item, and the one with the most confused writing attached to it.

**ESTABLISHED:** Bitcoin exists, its supply is capped by protocol, no
central issuer can inflate it, and no bank can freeze a transaction in it.
Those properties are real and they are why people care about it.

**ESTABLISHED and separate:** the majority of the money moved in crypto
passes through centralised exchanges, which are registered companies, hold
customer funds, and are subject to anti-money-laundering enforcement. The
pseudonymity of the underlying protocol has almost nothing to do with the
practical anonymity of the people using it.

That gap — protocol liberty, practical surveillance — is the source of most
of the confusion in this material, and it is worth holding as the general
lesson: **a property of a system and a property of its use are different
things, and the second one is usually what people are experiencing.**

And the historical record: Bitcoin was proposed in 2008 in response to the
banking crisis described in the previous chapter. It is a response to this
page's subject matter. It is not part of the subject matter.

Chapter - The Mark, and what it actually is

The reason this page is worth writing despite the false claims is that the
false claims are attached to real ones.

- Cashlessness is real and commercial.
- Identity credentialing is real and commercial.
- Credit scoring is real and severe.
- Data aggregation is real and profitable.
- A national social credit system is real, somewhere, and demonstrably works.
- The fraud documentation is real.
- The structural capture of financial regulation is real and documented.

And attached to that list is a numerological reading of a barcode and a
prophecy about a coming programme of marks.

**The marks are the part that will make a reader sound unreasonable to anyone
who knows the material, and that is the practical cost.**

A person who says "I know that the credit agencies were complicit in the
crisis, that Fannie and Freddie were run at capital ratios the regulator set,
that the rating agencies were paid by the issuers, that trafficking is
financed through this system, and that there is no national social credit
system in the West but there is one in China" is saying things that are true
and checkable.

The same person, in the next sentence, saying that this is the programme and
that you will be given a number and refused the right to buy, is saying
something that is not checkable, and it makes the true part harder to
defend.

**The false claims are not free. They are charged to the true ones.**

And that is the finding of this chapter, and it is the reason the level page
will get less popular if it keeps its markings and more effective if it
loses them.

A movement that is thirty per cent documented and seventy per cent prophecy
can be dismissed entirely, and the dismissal is correct, and the thirty per
cent goes with it.

You have reached the end of this chapter and you are owed something better
than a conclusion, so this is a set of choices, in the form the level pages
use. Pick one and do it this week.

That is not a rhetorical gesture. A page that lists ten claims and leaves
them sitting on a screen has done the same thing as a page that lists nine
false claims: it has transferred the problem to the reader without giving
them a way to move it. **The difference between the two is that these can be
acted on.**

Path 1 — The documents

Spend one evening with primary sources. Not commentaries, not videos, not
the pages that explain the documents.

The Federal Reserve Act of 1913. It is short. The Fed's own pamphlet on the
naming, from 1915, which explains in the Fed's own words why "Federal Reserve"
was chosen. The FDIC's published material on what happens to uninsured
deposits — the page that explains that above the limit you are a creditor,
written by the agency that guarantees the rest. The Basel capital framework,
or the summary of it. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's report.

That is about four hours. After it you will be able to distinguish an
established claim from an allegation at a room conversation, and that
capability is permanent.

**This path assumes you want the tools more than the fight.** Most people
should take it.

Path 2 — The banking relationship

Find out what your deposits are actually insured by, and what your balance
is relative to that number. It takes ten minutes and almost nobody has done
it.

Then read what happens in your state when a bank fails. Find out which
resolution authority handles it — in the United States it is the FDIC,
everywhere, because the FDIC takes control of the national banks and
supervises the state-chartered ones through the state regulators.

Then do the harder version: find out what your credit score is, what is on
it, and who is allowed to see it. That is the actual point at which a private
credit system touches a person's civil life, and it is happening now, and it
is checkable, and almost nobody checks it.

Path 3 — The supply chain

This is the one nobody does and it is the one with the most effect per hour.

If you live in the United Kingdom, Germany, France, the Netherlands,
Sweden, Denmark, Spain, Canada or Australia, you can search a public
register of modern slavery statements and read what large companies say
about their own supply chains. These are real filings, thousands of them,
and they name suppliers.

The exercise changes your view permanently. You will read a statement and
find the word "risk" forty times and the word "child" three times and no
supply chain at all, and you will learn what that level of writing is for.

**This path assumes you want to affect something you can see.** It is the
only one on this page that changes the behaviour of an identified party.

Path 4 — The money

If you are paid in cash and work somewhere with a real balance sheet,
this is the one for you, and it is duller than any of the others and it is
the most effective.

Learn what your organisation does with its money. Not what it says. Where
the surplus goes, whether it holds cash, what it pays for training, what it
pays its lowest-paid worker as a proportion of the highest, and whether the
ratio is going up or down. That last question — the direction of travel — is
the only one that has ever predicted anything.

The corollary is harder: **the same questions apply to you.** The ratio of
the hours you sell to the hours you are paid for. Most people who could
write a chapter about labour exploitation in a supply chain cannot answer
that about their own week.

Path 5 — Build one thing and know where every dollar came from

The level page's actual complaint, stripped of the prophecy, is that a person
cannot trace the origin of the money they receive. That is true and it has a
concrete answer.

If you control a revenue stream — however small — you can trace it. Know
your suppliers. Ask the people below you in the chain who they get it from.
Find the labour that produced the thing that pays you. **You will find it,
it will be a person, and naming them is the entire difference between an
opinion about the system and an instance of it.**

What I am not going to tell you

I am not going to tell you what the answer is. There isn't one, and anyone
who has one is selling it or has confused themselves.

What I will tell you is the finding of these ten chapters, stated plainly
and without any of the marking:

**the documented parts of this are serious enough that you should act on
them, and the undocumented parts are unreliable enough that they will cost
you your credibility the moment you say them in front of someone who
knows the record.**

That is the whole of it. A movement that can hold those two facts in the
same hand at the same time is unassailable, because there is nothing to
argue with. A movement that drops one of them is arguable in thirty seconds.

The last thing, and it is the finding of the whole chapter

I have written a document that spends ten chapters saying that the strongest
version of a conspiracy claim is false, and I have done it on a level page
whose title is *Banking Cabal*, whose contents include "the wealthy in
control of the world," and whose author has four NDEs and has read more
financial material than most people.

I am not arguing with him. I am noting what happened: **the page's claims
did not survive being written out long.**

That is the finding and it is the only one I would bet on. Every one of those
claims was assembled from true fragments in a way that made the whole sound
like a discovery. Written out over ten thousand words, with the sources
attached, each one either got stronger or got visibly weaker. Almost none
got stronger. The ones that got stronger were the boring ones: the
ratings agencies, the capital ratios, the uninsured deposits, the
disclosure rules, the credit reporting.

**The prophetic material did not survive the writing. The regulatory
material did.**

That is not a political finding. It is an observation about how claims
behave under contact with detail, and it is available to anyone, and it is
the most useful thing on this page.

Write the conspiracy version out in full. Attach the sources. Find out
which parts improve. Most of them will not.

Then do something about the parts that did.
