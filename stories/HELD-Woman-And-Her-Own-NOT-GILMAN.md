They tell you that money is the whole of the difficulty, and it is not. Money is the beginning of the difficulty, and after money there is the credit of it, which is what people say when you have any. In 1868 I had four hundred and ten dollars and no credit at all, being thirty-three years old, unaccomplished, and past the age at which the neighborhood begins to think of you favorably. I was, in fact, at the age at which the neighborhood begins to think of you as a resource to be spent. I had a father in his sixties who needed help and no longer could command it. I had no trade that anybody wanted badly. I had the education of a gentleman's daughter and the experience of a scullery maid, and when I put the two together and said what I could do, there was no answer to it but the kind that involves a wedding.

You may look down on that. I do not entirely look down on it myself. I have come to think it is a mercy to be poor in a plain way rather than starved in a genteel one. A starving gentlewoman will marry anything at all and call it a duty. A plain poor one at least has the sense to name the arrangement, and to know what her own time is worth while she is still spending it.

I set out to do with the arrangement what I had seen done by women who had no such advantage as I had. It was very simple in principle and very hard in practice, and I will lay it out exactly as I held it, because I think the failure of most such arrangements lies in the woman having no article written into it at the beginning. A woman who marries on the strength of what she feels, and says so, has put her whole property into the hands of a man she cannot see clearly. A woman who marries on the strength of what she can do, and says that, has a ledger, and the ledger is worth more than the feeling, because the feeling will change and the ledger will not.

I went to work again as a governess. I did it in three different families in four years and it is not a profession, it is a series of insults with a salary attached. You are hired to educate a child and the child is not the point. The point is that the household needs a lady at the table when there is company, and a lady to receive calls when there is a parson, and a pair of eyes on the governess so that the drawing room can be mentioned in confidence. I taught Latin to a boy who could not be got to take his breakfast, and I taught arithmetic to a girl whose mother described her as backward in front of the schoolroom door on purpose so that I would hear it. I did it for four years and I could do it again, and I would not do it again for anything.

I was thirty-three when I met Henry Jekyll, and I will not pretend that I did not know at once what he was, or that I pretended longer than was useful. I knew what he was because I had seen the type in three households and a boarding house in Boston, and the type does not vary. He is a man of perhaps forty-five, of a good family that has gone quiet, of no particular trade or occupation that he will name if you ask him, who dresses well enough to pass in a good street and not well enough to pass in a better one, and who has a certain command of the voice which men acquire when they have been in rooms where other men are obeyed. He is not a bad man. I want to put that down first and keep it there. He is not a bad man. He is a man who has come to the age of forty-five in a country where a man is permitted to do nothing, and who has been let out of the world into a smaller world, and who has never once been made to answer for it. That is not a demon. It is a Sunday, in August, in a city where nothing is expected of anybody.

He came to the house where I was engaged to teach, and he came for the child, and he stayed for the mother, which is the usual way. He was very civil, and I saw the civilness for what it was, and I let it stand because I wanted the situation and because I was tired. A woman of thirty-three who has been teaching other people's children for four years is a woman who has stopped expecting much, and I say that without any pride in it. He spoke of the house as though it were a way station. He spoke of his own position with a great deal of vagueness, and I let him be vague, because the vagueness was the size of what I needed.

His terms were these. He would marry me. He would not ask me to love him and would not ask me to pretend to, and he would not expect me to be charming. In return I would not embarrass him. I should not make a figure in his life that required an explanation to his family or his friends. I should manage his house, which was to say I should keep the accounts, the linen, the cellar, the kitchen, and the two women who did the work, and I should do the entertaining, and I should see that nobody ever knew I had once taught for a living. That last clause was his, not mine. He put it lightly, as though it were a small thing, and it was the first thing I agreed to, and I have thought about it for twenty years and I still do not know whether it was prudence or shame.

"Once taught for a living," he said. "It is a pity to have to explain it."

"It is a pity," I said, "to have to explain anything."

That was the whole negotiation. It took under ten minutes and I have been grateful to him for the speed ever since. There was no scene, no declaration, no kneeling, and nothing that either of us could afterwards have called a promise. He named the sum. I named the terms. He agreed to them. I have never in my life had a business arrangement concluded with more honor on either side.

He was housekeeper enough to know what he was getting. He came to my lodging in October with the lawyer's draft already written, and he read it aloud at the table while I made tea, and I listened to the whole of it, and it was a fair document, and it was more than fair, because it gave me a roof of my own at the end of it, and a hundred and eighty dollars a year in my own hand.

"You will never want for a roof," he said, "and you will never be obliged to ask me for money. That is more than most women of your class can say at the end of a life."

"I know what it is more than," I said. "I know what it costs, too."

"I have no doubt of it," he said, and he bowed his head a little, the way men do when they have pleased themselves by being generous, and that was the whole of our courtship and the whole of our wedding, in October, in a parson's house, with two witnesses and a fee of five dollars, and it was signed on a Tuesday, and I put the day down because it is the day the whole of my life turned on and I have been a woman who keeps dates ever since.

We went to his house, which was in the next street, and it was a good house, and I have never denied it. It had a parlor with a marble mantel, a brass and a porcelain whatnot on it, and a dining room that opened off it, and a kitchen below, and two floors of bedrooms, and a yard, and a cistern, and the whole of it under my hand from the first week of November. I did not inherit it and I did not buy any part of it, but I administered it, and I administered it better than it had been administered in the whole of the years before I came to it, and I want that said in my own favor. The silver improved. The linen came out white. The cellar stopped leaking money in the beer and the vinegar. I found a dishonest delivery man at the milk door and I went to the dairyman myself and I told him what his man was doing, and the man was discharged, and thereafter the milk was good, and Henry Jekyll was astonished, in the manner of men, and said so.

He was astonished often that first year. I do not blame him. He had lived in that house before me, and before me the house had been run by his aunt, a lady of seventy who had let the servants do as they liked and let the bills run as they came. He had spent eleven years as a bachelor in a house with a marble mantel. He had expected to marry a woman who would be pleasant in the parlor for an hour in the evening and he got a housekeeper, and he was a decent man and he accepted what he had got.

I was not lonely in the way people mean. I want to be accurate. I had been lonely since I was eleven and I had a great deal of practice at it. The loneliness of a woman in a large house with money in it is a different kind from the loneliness of a girl in a poor one, and it is not better. It is worse, because it is loneliness with all the excuses supplied. You may say a woman is lonely because she is not beautiful. You may say a woman is lonely because she is not educated. You may say a woman is lonely because she has no money, and here is money, in a drawer, in her own hand, and it does not make the slightest difference to the company at night. Two hundred years after this was written the learned gentlemen of the medical schools would be explaining our nerves in Latin, and would explain them with great learning and no observation whatever, and I shall be proved right in the only particular in which I ever wished to be proved right, which is that the condition of the household makes the condition of the woman, and the condition of the woman is a nervous condition, and it has always been a nervous condition, and it will remain a nervous condition as long as the household is what it is.

I got a bookseller's catalogue in the second winter, that being the one piece of liberty I bought in that house and I bought it with my own money and kept it in the drawer with the hundred and eighty a year. Henry Jekyll never mentioned it. He did not know. That is a fair statement of how much attention he paid to the facts of my inner life, and I set it down not to accuse him but to establish the size of the arrangement. We had a bargain and both of us kept it. I kept his house. He kept himself out of my account of the running of it. He was forty-six years old that year and he read the newspaper in the evenings and went out on Tuesdays and Thursdays and came home at eleven and asked me in the morning whether there was anything he wanted for dinner. That was the whole of his attention and he gave all of it. I never had a husband who was unfaithful, and I never had a husband who spent my money, and I never had a husband who beat me or drank, and I have come to the conclusion that these things are made much of because they are easy to describe. I was married for eleven years to a man who treated me as a competent piece of household machinery and paid me two hundred a year in my own hand, and I would not trade the eleven years for any of the others I was offered in my girlhood by people who thought they were being kind to me.

He was also unfaithful. I did not find it out at first, because I was not looking, and it would not have mattered to me, but I found it out in the fourth year and I did not care and he was surprised that I did not care. That surprised him and puzzled him and I think it was the first time in eleven years he had looked at me directly and wondered what I was. He said, "You do not care," in the tone of a man who has brought home a stone and expected it to be received. "I do not," I said. "Do you not love me?" he said. And I said, "Henry, you never loved me and I never loved you, and we have both been perfectly honest about that, and if you want to know why I do not care about the woman in the city, it is because I have not the time to spend on jealousy, and there are four people in this house to feed and clothe and the cistern wants a new pump before the frost."

He was quiet a while. Then he said, "That is a very cold thing to say."

"It is a very cold thing," I said, "because I am a very cold thing, and you have had eleven years to find it out, and you have found it out."

That exchange is the marriage entire, and I set it down whole because the rest of this book is only an expansion of it. He wanted a wife who would be pleased to be loved. I wanted a house and a wage and the peace of a well-run establishment. Neither of us deceived the other for a single day. And out of that perfectly honest arrangement there came to me, in the ordinary course, four children, and I raised them, and I think I raised them better than most women raise their children, and the whole of what I am about to set down happened to me anyway. It does not need a bad man. I have had bad men. I could not tell you one of their names. I have had a decent man who kept his part of the bargain and I have had the worst of my childhood, and the worst of my childhood was in the end the more instructive.

The children came, and I did what everybody does, which was to arrange my life around theirs without noticing that I had arranged it. That is the sentence, and I did not write it. It wrote itself. A woman with children and money in a large house is not obliged to work, and therefore works, and therefore works harder than she ever worked for a wage. I know what the parish will say. The parish will say I was idle. The parish has never had to keep a house of eleven rooms, four children, two women, and a cistern in a Massachusetts winter, and the parish does not count the cistern.

That is the sentence, and it is the only one I have ever needed. The next year the second child was born and did not live past the fourteenth month, and it was buried in the churchyard at the end of the lane, and I did not go into a decline over it and I did not go into a decline under it, and I have been told by three different women since that I ought to have been broken by it. I was not broken by it. I was busy. I have observed, and I state it as an observation and not as a complaint, that women are praised for being broken and are never praised for being busy. If I had been rendered a permanent invalid by that burial they would have spoken of my calamity. Because I was not, and because I went on the next day, I am a hard woman.

The children were Henry's. That was in the contract in a way neither of us said. I had made an arrangement at thirty-three and I understood the terms of such an arrangement, and I did not expect children and I did not get them on any other basis. They grew up in a house that had no need of them, and that is a serious thing to say about a house and about a marriage and about the position of women, and I will say it plainly. A child that is born into a house which is already administered, staffed, and solvent is a guest. I know that this is not how it feels at three o'clock in the morning with the croup in the room and the thermometer falling and no money. I have been at three o'clock in the morning more times than I can enumerate. I do not say the feeling is wrong. I say the feeling is what makes the arrangement possible, and the arrangement is what makes the feeling.

I raised them as well as the time allowed, and I taught them reading and writing and the use of the almanac and a plain discretion about money. I taught my eldest daughter to write, when she was seven, a page of a copy-book, and at the bottom of it, because the paper was there and I had the habit of using paper, I wrote four words. They were these: I am mine here. She spelled them out twice and asked whether they were a sentence and I told her they were a sentence, and she said the last word was spelled wrong and it should be near, and I told her that in a house where everything belongs to somebody else, near is what a person has a right to be.

She did not remember it after the first year or two. I do not blame her. Children do not retain what is written on a copy-book. But I had written it, and the writing of it was the act, and the act was not nothing. For nine years I had not been permitted one word of my own in that house, and here was the whole of what I had said in it. I had the accounts, and the accounts were a record of his money and not of me. I had a marriage that was a business arrangement and a household that was a machine and children who were guests in it. I wrote four words at the bottom of a page in an alphabet book and it was the first thing I had put on paper in that house that was not an inventory, and I would like the reader to sit with the size of that for a moment, because everything that follows came out of it and not out of any hero's resolve but out of the discovery, at forty-four, that I had not said anything in eleven years.

My sister came to see me that autumn and she is the reason I am alive in any sense that this book can describe. She was married and she had a house of her own and two children and no money whatever, and she had been in the world a year longer than I had and she had learned what I had refused to learn, which is that the two of us had been sent into the world to do the same work at different rates of pay, and that I had paid at the higher rate because I had married a man who could afford to pay it.

She sat in the parlor with the fire going and she said, "You have got a good house."

"I have got a good house."

"And you have kept it eight years and it is better than it was."

"It is."

"And are you happy, Williamina?"

That was the first time in eight years anybody had asked me the question. I answered it as honestly as I knew how, which was not very honestly, and she let me get through the answer and then she said, "You have answered me about the house."

"Yes."

"Then I will ask you the other question. Do you ever sit down?"

I said, "What do you mean?"

"You are sitting down now," she said.

"Well, yes."

"You are sitting down in a good chair, in a warm room, in a house that is paid for, with three children in the schoolroom upstairs, and a hired girl in the kitchen, and the year's expenses in a drawer that you keep yourself. And you have not sat down, Williamina, since you were seventeen years old. I have watched you at my mother's table for six days and you have not sat down."

That is all she said. She did not advise me. She did not tell me what to do, and I have had a great deal of advice in my life and nearly all of it was of the kind that is given to make the adviser feel the weight of her own wisdom. She said one sentence, she went home, and I sat in the chair and thought about it, and the thought did not leave me for three years.

In those three years a good deal happened. The servant who had come in the fourth year of the marriage, a good woman and an honest one, whose name I am not going to set down because she is still living in the next town and has done nothing in her life but her duty, stood at the milk door and talked to the dairyman's man, and the talk came back into the house the way all talk does in a small place. She said it was a pity such a lady had no child of her own. She said the children ought to be told to be grateful to their father. She said, in the voice women use when they are being kind to you, that I ought not to have married so high.

I was in the market that week and I heard the last of it, and I stood in the aisle of the grocer's shop with a bundle of candles in my hand for about a minute, and then I went home and washed the candles and told nobody for three days. I was not angry. That is what I could not account for. I have been angry in my life and I know the shape of it and it is a heat in the chest and it wants a thing broken. What I had in the aisle of that shop was a coldness and a sort of amazement, as though a person I had known a long time had turned out to be wearing a costume, and I stood there and watched him take it off.

Then I asked Henry to speak to her, and he said he would rather not, and he was right, and I said so, and then I said, "Very well, I will speak to her myself," and he said, "What good will that do?"

"That is for me to know," I said.

I want to set that question down because it is the pivot of the whole of my life and it took me four more months to understand it. He did not ask why I wished to do it, or whether it were right. He asked what good it would do. I had been living in a household where every question about my inner life was translated into a question of utility, and I had been so long at that translation that I answered in its own language without noticing. And I want the reader to understand that nothing was wrong with Henry Jekyll. He had been educated by a mother who had sat in an armchair for nineteen years and had never once said a word about it, and he had been educated in a house where the women were furniture and were praised for being furniture, and he was asking an entirely sensible question inside an arrangement nobody had ever asked him to look at.

I was saying nothing, because I had nothing to say. Everything I said was received as an opinion and weighed against my usefulness, and I had learned to be quiet the way a man learns to walk with a stone in his shoe. When I finally spoke to her I said, "You have spoken of my children as though they were yours and of me as though I were yours. They are not. If you say it again I will ask you to leave the house, and if you ask me why, I will tell Henry, and if he does not ask you to leave, I will take the children and go, and you may tell the whole parish and it will be true."

She said, "I have been here four years."

"You have," I said, "and in four years you have not once asked me my name."

I do not know what she expected. She turned and went out and did not speak to me again for a fortnight. She did not leave. That is the part I could not get over afterward. She did not leave. She stayed in that kitchen and washed the dishes and looked at me when I came in, and the whole arrangement went on, and I had made a great speech and the world had not moved one inch, and I went up to my room that night and thought that this was the shape of my life and would be to the end of it.

It was not the shape of my life. That is what I want to say next, and I want to say it as a fact and not as a comfort. I went on. I kept the house for those three years and I did it as well as I had always done it. I had a third child, a boy, and I reared him, and he was quick to learn and quick to read, and I have not seen him dull, and I do not know one hour of his life that I did not lay down for him. I was the finest piece of household machinery of my year and everybody said so, in the way they say it. And in the winter of the eleventh year, after the children were in bed, I sat down one night in the parlor, alone, and I looked at the fire, and I understood that my sister had asked me a question in a cold room three years before and that I had not answered it and was not going to answer it now, and that I was going to leave.

The decision was not a decision at first. It was a permission. That is the way everything real comes, in my experience, and it is the way the great decisions of the world come, and the difference is that when a man makes one he calls it a policy. Nobody sat down and chose. What happened was that I began, without any argument, to say certain things to myself, and each of them was a small thing, and by the time the sum of them came to me in the parlor that winter I had been choosing for perhaps two months without knowing it.

The first was a sentence about the drawer. I had kept the hundred and eighty a year and it had grown, over eleven years, to nine hundred dollars, because I had not spent it and nobody had expected me to. It was mine by the contract and it had never once been touched. Now I began to ask myself what it was for. That is a question that sounds so simple that I am ashamed to put it in print, and it is one of the great questions of the world and it will be asked by every woman for the next thousand years. What is that money for? I had saved it for a hundred reasons and none of them was true. I had saved it because it was mine. It was the only thing in that house that was mine, and it was mine because I had been paid, and being paid for a thing makes the thing a kind of self. I began to think of it as my voice, which is not money at all but sounds like money, which is what money has always been for women.

The second was a question about the word I. I noticed that I did not say I in that house. I said we. We had a good house. We were doing well. We were very comfortable. We had the children. Whatever I might have said about myself in that house I said as a member of a family, and the family was a fact that existed before I came into it and would exist after, and my own portion of it was the portion that did the washing. It took me a year to understand how completely the grammatical person had been taken from me. A woman who cannot say I cannot begin to think. You cannot begin to think about what you are because you are standing in a household where you are a function, and a function does not have a self, it has a use.

The third was about the word I had written at the bottom of the copy-book, and here I must be plain. It was not I am mine here. Those were the words, and they were the words that mattered, but at the time they were words and not a program. It was not until nine years after that I began to ask what it would cost to mean them. I had no idea what it would cost. I had no notion that the cost would be the parlor, and the hundred and eighty a year, and three children, and the right to call one of them my son only in the way that is permitted, and the good name I had for eleven years, and every person in that parish who had ever spoken well of me, and the whole warm, comfortable, well-fed, well-furnished arrangement of my life as it stood. It would cost me the bed I had earned. There is no other way to say it.

I have been asked since, by persons who did not have to make the choice, whether I was not a fool to throw away a comfortable house for an idea. I answer them the only way I know how, which is to put the same question back in their own words. They always mean by the comfortable house the whole of what they call respectability, and they never once count the sink in it. You cannot count a sink at four in the morning with a lamp burning. You cannot count it at forty-four with a jar of jam made and a cistern on the pump and a woman in the kitchen who is underpaid. You cannot count it in the fact that your husband asks you at the breakfast table whether there is anything he wants for dinner, for eleven years, and never once asks what you did yesterday.

I did not think of myself as brave then and I do not now. I think of myself as a woman who had run out of things that could be put off. There was no event. No cruelty. Nothing happened in that house that would make a newspaper. The money was good and the children were healthy and the man kept his word, and out of that perfectly correct household there came to me, over eleven years, a certain understanding, arrived at slowly, like water finding a crack.

Henry said nothing for the first month, which I have thought a great deal of. He was not a man given to scenes and I had expected none. He came home at his hours and asked for his dinner and read his newspaper, and twice he said, "You are very quiet, Williamina," and twice I said, "I have a great deal to think about," which was true. He was a decent man and he had a theory, and his theory was that I was tired, and that a woman who is tired will be quiet, and that the remedy for quiet is a holiday, and he offered me a holiday, and I took it and did not go away. I stayed in my own house and rested and was quiet, and he went on thinking his theory was confirmed.

Then, in the second month, he came in and said, "Williamina, you have it in your mind to go."

I said, "I have."

"Do you know what you are talking about?"

"I know exactly what I am talking about."

"Then say it."

"I am going to ask you for a divorce," I said, "and you are going to refuse, and then you are going to agree to it, and I am going to take the children and go and make my own living, and I am not going to depend upon you for the rest of my life."

He looked at me a long moment. I have said that men look at their wives and see a piece of furniture. That is true of most men and it was not true of Henry Jekyll in that particular moment. He looked at me and he saw a woman he had not looked at for years, and I saw him see her, and it was the last time that either of us looked straight at the other.

"You could have said that any time in eleven years," he said.

"Yes."

"And you did not."

"No," I said, "because for the first eight years it would have been a whim, and I could not afford whims, and because a woman of thirty-three with no money who asks a man to divorce her is a woman who has been sent away. And because for the last three years I have been getting ready, though I did not know it until this winter, and when I knew it I did not waste a month."

He was quiet a while, and then he said, "And if I refuse?"

"Then I shall go to the court," I said, "and I shall stand in front of a judge and say the whole of it, and it will be printed, and my name will be in it, and yours will be in it, and so will the name of the woman who has spent eleven years standing behind me in a market in this town saying that a lady ought to be grateful. And the parish will have its amusement for a season, and you will lose more by it than I shall."

That was true and it was the whole of my leverage, and I set it down because I want no part of this story to be improved upon. I had no virtue in me that could do that work. I had the truth of two facts: that a man of his position does not care for the exposure of an unsuccessful suit, and that a woman who asks for a divorce and is refused is a woman whose children are taken from her. The law of that day was written by men for men and I knew the whole of it and I was not above using it. I say this plainly because a great many accounts of women in this position have been written by people who could not bear to say it.

"Then say the other thing," he said. "Say why."

And I said it. I said it standing, with my hands behind me, in the parlor where the marble mantel was, and it took perhaps three minutes, and I have never composed anything since that I was so satisfied with.

I said: "Because you have never once in eleven years asked me what I think. Because I have not spoken in this house for eleven years except about the household. Because I am not married to you, I am employed by you. Because when a man marries a woman for her housekeeping he has bought a servant and called it a wife, and there is no insult in the world like it, and the reason there is no insult like it is that it has been done to so many women so long that it does not feel like anything. Because I am forty-four years old and I have been sitting down since I was seventeen. Because I have three children whom I love and can keep and cannot support, and I must get to the place where I support them, and it is to be done now or not at all. And because there is a thing that I have found out about myself this winter which I would have been ashamed to say to you a year ago and which I will now say to you, because you have asked me for the truth and you are entitled to it: I find that I can be quite as comfortable alone as I ever was in this room with you. And that discovery is the end of us, Henry. Everything else is bookkeeping."

He sat down. He put his hand over his eyes, and I waited, and I had prepared myself for almost anything except what happened, which was that he laughed.

"You have made a very good speech," he said. "You have been practicing it."

"I have."

"Who was it for?"

"Whoever was going to hear it," I said. "It was for me."

He nodded and got up and went to the door and stood with his hand on the knob, and he said, without turning round, "I will think about it. Give me a week."

"Give me a week," I said, "and then ask me again on Friday, and I will give you the same speech without practice, and it will be the same speech, and I shall not be tired."

That was on a Tuesday in January. I have not seen the inside of that house since the following April, and I have not seen him since the court, and he died in a hotel in Chicago six years later, and I was told of it by a man who wanted to sell me a house, and I said I was sorry, and the man said that everyone said so, and I believe him, and I have never found out one single thing about the last eleven years of Henry Jekyll's life.

I have wondered a great deal whether I ought to have made that speech. I want to put that in the book because a book of this kind that ends with a woman triumphant and unquestioned is a lie, and I am not a woman who tells lies and never has been. That speech was cruel. He had kept his part of the bargain for eleven years and I had kept mine, and I told him to his face that his eleven years of decent treatment were the arrangement of an employer with a competent servant, and the sentence was true and it was an act of cruelty, and I said it because it was the only sentence that would get the children out of that house alive. I have been asked since whether I would do it again.

I would wait a week first.

The week was the longest seven days of my life, and I have since had a great many of what I must call long days, and none of them was longer. On the Wednesday I did the washing, because the washing does not wait for anything. On the Thursday I wrote to my uncle, and that letter took me until the lamp went, because I had to begin it nine times.

John Marchmont is my mother's brother, and he is a farmer on a hundred and eighty acres in a town nobody has ever heard of, and he is fifty-four years old, and he has the hands of a man who has earned them and the temper of a man who has not earned that. He was the only person in my family who ever said one true thing to me about money. He said it when I was nineteen and had come home from a place where the family were tired of me and were paying my board. He said, "Williamina, the wage you get is what you are thought to be worth. Nobody ever pays a woman what she is worth. They pay her what she is convenient for."

He was thirty when he said it and he had no theory of anything. He was a farmer and he thought in crops and in animals and in the number of hands a hundred and eighty acres required in a bad season. He married at twenty-two and had his children early and buried one and has had, since he was nineteen years old, no time at all for anything that could not be eaten or planted or shorn. That is the sort of man I have always found I can talk to, and it is the sort of man whose conversation I can record honestly, because it was not literature.

I wrote to him that I was going to ask Henry for a divorce and that when I had it I should have nothing and three children. I wrote it plainly, because he has no use for a letter that is not plain. I told him that I would not ask him for the children and I would not ask him for a house and I would take them and make my own living. And I wrote, at the end, in the sentence I had been composing for nine tries, that if he and aunt Susan would let me come to the farm and bring the children and do the work of the place as I had done the work of the house, I would be glad to go, and I named the sum I thought I could earn at the bottom of it in plain figures, because he is a man who trusts figures.

His letter came on the Friday morning, the day Henry was to ask me again, and it is four lines long and I have it still.

He wrote: "Come and keep house. I will give you the small house and the two acres and the girls will do the cooking. You will not teach. I will not have that in my house again. If you cannot do that, do not come."

I read it four times before I understood that my uncle had never for one moment in his life proposed to support me, and had understood perfectly that I was not asking him to, and had offered me the only thing he had, which was work and no asking. I had gone into that house at nineteen and told them I would teach, and I was going out again at forty-four, and I had been going out to a farmer, and the farmer had made his condition in one line, and the condition was: you will do it silently. You will be a person in my house who does not ask the parish questions.

I put the letter in my pocket and I went down and I answered the children's questions about dinner and I did the dinner, and at three o'clock in the afternoon Henry Jekyll came in and shut the door behind him.

"Well," he said.

"Well," I said.

"I have thought about it four days and I have talked to nobody, because there was nobody to talk to, which is a compliment to you if you will take it. What will you do?"

That was not what I had prepared for. I had prepared for the man who agrees, and I had not prepared at all for the man who asks what she will do, and I found that my voice went out of me like a lamp going out.

"I will teach," I said.

"Teach what?"

"The children of other people, in a schoolroom, in the mornings, and I will do the house I live in in the afternoons, and I will earn my own money."

"Williamina, that is the exact life you had at twenty-two."

"Yes," I said, "and at twenty-two I had no children and was at somebody else's mercy in every particular, and at forty-four I have three children and a hundred and ninety dollars and a letter from a farmer, and I would like you to tell me the difference."

"The difference is that at forty-four you will find nobody will have you," he said. "You will go into a room and they will look at you and see a woman of forty-four who has been married and has three children, and they will not give you anything, and in a year you will be back in this parlor begging me."

"I shall not beg you," I said.

"You will be glad to be forgiven," he said. "They all are."

It was the plainest, kindest, most truthful thing he ever said to me, and it went into me like a nail, and I have used it every day since. But I want it observed, and I want it observed plainly, that he was arguing that a woman who leaves her husband loses her children and her name and her living, and that he was arguing it in January, 1879, because it was true in January, 1879, and that he was the first man of my life who tried to talk to me about money as a fact instead of a favor, and that I owe him for that.

"Then let me tell you what I have thought of," I said. "Not to persuade you. You have already consented. I want it written and I want you to hear it and then I will go."

"Go on."

"You keep the house. I take the children. I go to my uncle's farm and I keep house there, and in the winter I take whatever work there is, and in the summer I take whatever work there is, and I do not ask you for a dollar of it, and I do not come back here at the end of a bad year and ask for a place in your parlor. That is the whole of what I ask. The children will be yours and you may see them when you please and they will be sent for on the terms you set. I will not take them into my uncle's house as a claim upon him for eleven years and then send them back to you when they are grown. And when I am old I shall have put by something, or I shall not, and either way it will be mine."

He did not say anything for some time, and then he said, "The boy is going to be a man in four years."

"Yes."

"He will be asked what his name is."

"He will be asked what his name is," I said, "and he will be told what his name is, and he will be told by his father, and I shall not have anything to do with it. He will be yours from Friday. You may have him whenever you send for him and I shall not ask how he is getting on."

"And the girls?"

"The girls I keep."

"The girls will grow up and ask why their brother has a different name from theirs."

"Then they shall have the truth," I said, "which is that when I was forty-four years old I had no money and no trade and a house was offered me on condition I would be silent, and I took it, and I could not take my son with me, and his father had money and a name and could give him both, and I could give him neither, and that is not a thing to be hidden from a girl of fifteen. I shall tell them exactly what I have told you this afternoon, and if the girls are half as hard as I am they will understand it."

He got up and went to the window and stood at it, and I let him stand there, and I had said everything I had to say and I waited.

Then he said, "It is the one thing I cannot do."

"Which thing?"

"Take the boy."

It was my turn to be still.

"You told me to say the other thing," he said, still at the window. "You told me I had never once in eleven years asked you what you think, and I am asking you now, and I am saying the truth, and I have been a coward for eleven years and you have made me say it and I hate it. I have kept the boy."

"Henry, he is four years old."

"I know what he is." He turned round. "He is my son and I have had him four years and I have played with him every evening of those four years and I have watched him grow and I know the exact shape of his right ear, and I do not know the exact shape of the girl upstairs and I have not looked at it since she was in a long white robe, and I know the shape of that boy's ear better than I know my own."

"Then take him," I said.

"I will not."

That was not what I expected, and I had not prepared for it, and I found I had nothing to say at all. I had come into that room with three speeches and a letter in my pocket and a plan, and I had not come into it for a man who would refuse the thing that was offered him, because a man who refuses money is a rarity and I had not provided for one.

"Look at what you have done," he said. "You have made a bargain with me and you have named the terms, and one of the terms is the boy, and you offered him to me the way you would offer a chair. That is what you have been doing for eleven years and you have never seen it because you are in it. And now you have offered me my own son at four years old, and you have done it as a matter of course, as though he were the cistern pump."

I said, "It is for his sake."

"It is for yours," he said. "It is for yours because you cannot carry him, and because you have found out in the last four days that you can be comfortable alone, and because you want the going to be clean. You want it to be a fair bargain to the end. I know the wish. I watched you write out your household accounts for eleven years."

And that was so exactly right, and he had never in his life seen me do anything but run that house, that I could not answer it. I stood in the parlor with the marble mantel behind me and the good chairs on either side and I could not produce one sentence, and I found that my eyes were hot, which I had not permitted since I was eleven years old, and I turned round so that he would not see it.

When I had myself in hand I said, "Then you will not take him."

"No."

"And if he is to be yours, you will have to take him now, while he can be got, and you will have to tell him, and he will be told what I am and you will not contradict me."

"I will not contradict you," he said. "I will tell him whatever you tell me to tell him."

I found I had to sit down, which is a thing I have not done in a parlor since I was seventeen years old. I sat down and I put my hands on my knees and I looked at the fire.

"Henry," I said, "why."

He was a long time answering.

"Because I saw it once," he said. "In my father's house. My mother sat in that chair in that exact chair and I watched it, and I watched her every day for nineteen years, and she died in it. And she never once said the word, and I was thirty before I understood that it had been done to her as thoroughly as if it had been said every morning. And I have been fifty-six years old in this world and I have never seen a woman's face do what yours did the other night when you sat down in that chair."

He did not say anything else that evening. He went out, and I heard him in the hall, and I heard the door, and I sat in the chair until the lamp burned down. I have not sat down in an armchair since that night, and I do not intend to begin. That is the whole of what I took out of that parlor, and it is the only thing in this book that I have never had to explain to anybody.

We were married in a court, in April, in the second week of the month, in a room with a stove and six chairs and a window out onto a brick wall. Henry did not contest a single particular of what I had set down and I am not going to insult either of us by describing the ten minutes. What I will describe is what was done to me at the end of it, because it is the part that belongs to this book and because I did not know anything about it until it happened.

The clerk asked my name for the record and I said it, and he wrote it, and then he said, "You will give your name as Mrs. Henry Jekyll, I suppose."

That is the whole of it. That is the entire event. It was said as a convenience, the way a man says a thing he has no idea is a thing. The clerk wanted to know which of two names to put on a piece of paper that would go into a drawer in a county office in Boston for the next fifty years.

I said, "I will give my name as Williamina Marchmont."

He looked up. Henry said, "She is not divorced from her uncle."

"Then she will give it as Williamina March," I said, "and the record will be correct."

It was the clerk's business to have no opinion and he had none and he wrote it. And then, because I have a bad way of being right when I am right and cannot leave a thing alone, I said, "I should like it written that I am not Mrs. Henry Jekyll and never have been. I should like it written that I was hired in October, 1868, by that man, at two hundred dollars a year, to keep his house, and that this paper makes it plain that a man may buy a woman's work and call it marriage, and that I am leaving it."

The clerk said, "That is not a thing anybody can write down."

"Then write down my name as I have given it and let the rest of the truth be where it belongs," I said, "in the four hundred people in this city who are saying the same thing in their kitchens this morning."

Then Henry Jekyll paid the eight dollars. It was his last act as my husband and it was to pay a fee that I had been in the habit of itemizing for him for eleven years.

We walked out together to the corner and he stood on the pavement and I stood on the pavement, and there was a man at the shop opposite loading flour into a cart and neither of us knew him.

"Williamina," Henry said.

"Yes."

"You did that in there on purpose. You know you did it on purpose."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because in about six months there will be a great many women doing exactly that in this city, and in about six years there will be some of them doing it in New York, and there will come a time when a woman is asked her name in a room like that and the clerk will write it as she gives it, without any argument at all. And it will not happen because of me, and it will not happen because of any one of us. It will happen because a great many women got tired of being Mrs. Somebody, and it will happen faster if every one of them makes a scene in a court room on the day she leaves, even though the scene does nothing at all for that particular woman."

I was practicing again. I have admitted that before. He looked at me.

"Go on," he said. "I will hear it."

"You will not keep the girls," I said.

"Go on. They are not to be kept from me."

"You will not keep the girls, and here is what is behind that, and it is the only reason I will consent to lose the boy. You keep the boy and you will bring him up and he will be told that his mother left him, which is true, and he will be told the rest of it when he is old enough to understand money, and he will grow up with a father and a name and means, and he will be a man with a trade. And the girls will grow up with me, and I shall not have money, and I shall not have a trade in a shop, and they will have to have what they can get, and they will be the ones who learn what women earn, because I shall show them. And that is not justice. I know it is not justice. Justice would take all three and divide them and I cannot divide them, I can only carry what I can carry. And I am telling you plainly that I am asking you for the boy in order that the girls may be taught something, and I would not have asked for him on my own account, and I would not have asked for him if I had had money."

I had not expected to say that last part and I heard it go out of me and I could not take it back.

"Then I will not take him," said Henry Jekyll.

"That is not fair," I said.

"It is not," he said. "It is the only thing I have."

We stood there a while. Then he said, "Where are you going?"

"To my uncle's," I said. "John Marchmont, at Hoosick. He has written four lines to me and he has offered me the small house and the two acres and said I was not to teach. He is the only man in my family who has ever told me the truth about money and I am going to spend the rest of my life on a farm, and I am going to tell you now, Henry Jekyll, that if anybody asks me in five years what I did with my life, I shall say that I took the wages of a man when the wages of a woman were two hundred dollars, and I did not weep about it, and I am not weeping now."

"You are not," he said.

"No," I said. "That is what the arrangement was for."

Hoosick is a town of nineteen hundred people in a county of farms, and the farm is a hundred and eighty acres on a slope with a brook at the bottom of it, and there is a large gray house at the top of the slope and a small house at the bottom by the road that was built for a hired man and has been empty since the hired man left for the war. I have been in the small house seven years this month. I have the two acres and I keep the hens and the milk and the account of the whole of it, and the girls do the cooking, and my uncle has never once in seven years come into my kitchen to see how the work was getting on, and I have come to the conclusion that this is his way of saying that he trusts me, and it is the only compliment he has ever paid me and I value it more than the parlor in the city.

He was right and I was right and neither of us knew it. That is the sentence I have arrived at in seven years on a farm in the Berkshire hills, and I put it first in this book, because the men who will read it will not get there in their own lives in twenty years.

John Marchmont said: you will do it silently. That was the condition of my going. It was a condition and I agreed to it in a letter in eleven words and I have kept it in every particular for seven years. I do not teach. I did not teach for one hour after I got to that farm and I have not taught for one hour since, and when my sister wrote and asked whether I would take a school in the spring for two dollars a week I wrote back that I could not, and she wrote back that she had not thought of it and she was sorry, and I said it was nothing, and it was not nothing, and I did not tell her the reason, and I have never told anybody the reason, and I am not going to print it.

I am a widow and I have not been married since April. I say that here because it must be said and because it will be said for me. John Marchmont died in the November of that first year, of a thing that the doctor called a failure of the heart and that the neighbors called a broken heart, and they said it because he had been heard crying in the milk house once in the middle of the night, and because a woman who has been dead four months cannot be spoken of without her history going with her. It was a bad winter and there is no end of reasons for a man's heart to stop. I have set the reason down in one line, without ceremony, because it belongs to this book and because I am not a woman who tells lies. It is not evidence that he died of me. It is evidence that a man who has been working since he was nineteen, on food and weather and stock and debt, in a country where nobody thinks it strange that a woman should break and thinks it very strange that a man should, will break as readily as a woman will, and in his own fashion, and mostly alone, and usually about four in the morning.

He left me the small house and the two acres in the will and the will was read in the parlor of the large house and the two girls looked at me and I looked at the floor. He left me four hundred dollars and the two cows and his own copy of an almanac and a pair of shears. He did not leave me the farm and I am glad he did not, and I will tell you why in the plainest words I have: because if he had left it to me, every soul in this county would have said within a month that John Marchmont had left his property to his sister-in-law, and that the sister-in-law had got it away from the children, and there would not have been one field left to the family, and I would have had to spend the rest of my life explaining.

They said it anyway. They said it for a year. Then the eldest of Henry Jekyll's girls was fifteen and came out to see me and stayed three weeks, and after that they said something else, and what they said then was that I had the Marchmont luck, which in that county is not a compliment.

I have had seven years on that farm and I want to set down what a woman can do in seven years, because the whole of this book is the answer to a question I have been asked in eleven different kitchens by women who sit and watch me and cannot help themselves.

I have kept the books of the whole farm since the November. That is not the small house and the two acres. That is the entire farm: the cows, the ploughing, the seed, the wages of the two men and the girl, the oil, the tin, the taxes, the interest on the mortgage that has not been paid since 1849 and never will be paid while I am here. My uncle taught me in four months what no one had taught the sister-in-law in thirty years, and he did it by setting me a chair by the kitchen table with the ledger and the ink and saying, "Show me." He did not say why. He did not say it was a courtesy to a guest. He said it the way he would say it to a boy, and I have decided that it is the only reason I have my life at all.

A woman who has been taught to keep books can keep books. There is no difficulty in it that has anything to do with being a woman. I have proved that to every person in this county in seven years and I have proved it by never once being wrong in a figure, and I have proved it in a second and harder way, which is that in the last two winters I have done business. I buy the hen-feed and the lamp-oil and the boots and the seed potatoes for the whole of this township, for four families besides my uncle's, and I sell the butter and the eggs and the honey, and I have made a contract with a dealer in the county town for the butter, and the contract is in my name. That contract was signed at a table in a public house with two witnesses, and the dealer asked whether I was a widow with a family, and I said that I was, and he said that he would rather deal with a man, and I said that he would deal with me or he would carry his butter home, and he dealt with me, and the price he gave me is one penny in the pound better than he gives Mr. Marchmont's neighbor, and that penny in the pound was four years of a woman's life.

I can do all that. I have done all that. And here is the thing I have to tell, and I tell it because nobody will tell it to me.

None of it has ever once been mine.

Not one item of it. I keep the books of a farm of a hundred and eighty acres, I sign contracts, I buy for six families, I sell for the county town, and I have never in seven years had a dollar of it in my hand, and I cannot leave without a man to say to, and if I say to that man, I am not his wife. That is the whole of it. That is the whole of what these seven years have been. The arrangement is perfect and the man is a man. I take the whole of the labor and I have the whole of the decision, and I have no more control of the results than the two hired men have, and the two hired men have this.

I said it to him once. In the January of the second year, when he had been in the habit of finding fault with my butter and had said something about the price of eggs, I said, "John, I will ask you a question and I want an honest answer, and if you are not going to be honest I will drop it."

He said, "Ask it."

"Do you ever consider that I may want a thing for myself?"

"You have a roof," he said. "You have two acres and the hens and the children's education. What more is there?"

"That is not an answer," I said. "I asked whether you ever consider it. The two men would have a dollar apiece for a holiday and go where they liked and I would have to find somebody to take the churn. That is all. That is the entire difference between them and me and it is the difference between a woman and a man and I want you to consider it once, just once, because you are the only man in the world whose opinion of me I have ever wanted."

He sat and thought about it. He is a slow thinker. He said at last, "I never have considered it."

"Well, consider it now," I said.

And he did. I know he did, and I know the price of it, and I know it is not a trifle, because I saw him miss the price at the market twice in the following June and he did not miss it before.

I have said that I would be asked a question by women who watch me. Here is a fair statement of what they get and what they do not get. They get the milk. They do not get the herd. They get the eggs and not the flock. They get the butter and not the contract. A woman may do the whole of a man's work and take a portion of the results, and the portion will be fixed by the men, and if she protests she is a termagant and if she does not she is a good wife. That has been the arrangement since the world began, and the difference between now and a hundred years ago is only that the portion is a little larger and is called her own, and the name is newer than the thing.

And when the children were old enough I found that I had raised a generation of people to be exactly as I am, and to see nothing wrong with it.

That is the part I have to tell next, and it is the worst of it, and I have thought about it for six years before I could put it down. The two girls have grown up in that small house, and they have grown up seeing a woman who keeps the books of the largest farm in the township and has no standing, no wage, no property, and no name, and they have taken it as the way things are. The younger of them is nineteen now, and at her last visit she said to me a thing which I have not forgiven and shall never forgive.

She said: "Mother, I think it is splendid the way you manage everything. I think it would be a shame to stir things. What would there be the good of?"

I did not answer her. I went out to the milk house afterward and it was a bitter January night and I stood in the dark a long time. She was nineteen and she had no guile in it. She was not cruel; she was describing the facts to me as she understood them, and I had brought her up in a household in which the facts had always been exactly those.

I have had four children and lost one, and I have raised three of them. I could not support the boy so he went to his father at four years old, and I shall never know by name what he became. The two girls I did raise, and I have been able to give them the ordinary things and the schooling and the years, and I have not been able to give them one single new idea. I put everything I had into teaching them to be equal. I said, "There is no difference between you and a boy," and I said, "You are worth as much as any man," and I said it on and off from the time they were seven years old, and I said it at the table and I said it in the field and I said it when they were children, and it was all true, and it did not go in, and I have to write that down as the truth of my life. It did not go in because I had nothing to show for it. Words do not take root. What takes root is a house, and a wage, and a name of one's own, and I had none of those, and so I taught them a lesson and I fed them the other one.

That is what is done in this world to women, and it is not done by bad men. I have never had a bad man. I have had my father, who was a fool in the particular way that all men of that generation were fools; I have had a decent man who kept his word; I have had a decent farmer who trusted me with his books; and I have had the parish, which has been good to me and would say that I have got what I deserved and more. And out of these perfectly good conditions there has come a woman of fifty-one who can do the work of a man and has no standing whatsoever in her own town.

I want to be fair to the argument. I have heard it made well. I have heard the men who say that the woman is the house and the man is the world, and that a division of labor is a division of functions and not of worth, and that if you set the child and the clothes and the dinner and the account in one half of the world and the field and the market and the vote in the other half, then the whole runs. I have heard it from men who would say it to me kindly, over this table, and I have been rude to some of them and I am not proud of being rude.

The division of labor is the argument of every robber. A man who takes a town and a man who takes a house divide nothing; they take the same thing. There is no difference of function between a woman who keeps the books of a farm and a woman who keeps the books of a shop, and both of them are keeping another man's books, and the reason that is called a division of labor is that it has to be called something.

Here is what I have learned in fifty-one years of it, and it is the only thing I know. In this world the private life is the whole of the public life. Whatever is done to women is done in the house and heard at the table and done by children who are eating their dinner. That is where the next generation is made. That is where the state actually operates, in every particular in which the state has anything to do with men. That is why the men who come out of those houses believe what they believe, and it is why they have no idea that anything is decided anywhere but where they live. And that is why you may call the thing a new order, or a world order, or a world made over, and it will not matter. Whatever name it is given, the first fact of it will be decided, this year, this month, in a kitchen, by a woman who does not know that she is deciding anything, over the washing.

There is a great deal said now about power, and about how it really operates, and by whom it really is kept. I will tell them where it is kept, since I have had it in my hands for seven years. It is kept in the household accounts. It is kept in the question of whether a woman is paid or whether she is maintained. Every argument about the future of the world comes down, at the bottom, to that, and everyone who has ever made a speech about it has known it at the bottom and has said something else at the top.

They say the woman is a term of the race. They say the woman is a mere complement to the man. They say the household needs her, and the child needs her, and there can be no civilization without her, and they say it in a tone of awe at the beautiful arrangement of it. But the household is the woman's own prison, and the child is the woman's own task, and when she has given them all that is asked she is told she is a creature who exists only by permission, and if she goes she is an unnatural mother and an unwomanly woman and a woman who has no idea what she is talking about. That is the position. It has been the position since the beginning and it will be the position until it is changed by those who are in it, and it will not be changed by argument. It will be changed by wages.

I am not going to be sentimental about this and neither is anyone who has done the work. I do not say that a man who marries a woman and supports her is a criminal. I say that he has put her in a position in which her development is arrested at a certain point, and he has been educated to do it, and it is done for him in his own house by his own mother and his own wife, and so it goes on to the children, and then it is done for him again in the next house. It is not one man's cruelty. It is a thing that has been handed down.

I say plainly: it is not right. I do not say it because it is the right thing to say for women, and it is; I say it because it is the right thing to say, and it is a rational measure of a civilization, and it is used against us as a measure of the family and the home and the church and the state, and it is the falsest of the four, and it is used to make the other three look like temples.

I have heard a good deal of talk of liberty in my life, and I have always observed that the word is never used where it is most needed. Nobody says to a man, "You shall be free to live as you please," and nobody thinks it necessary, because he is already free. We talk of the rights of men as though they were the merest matter of course, but we do not talk of the rights of women as rights at all. We talk of the privileges of women. It is not a matter of words. When the marriage ceremony is performed the words are used and the words are the form and the form is what the world is run by, and the words go on while the thing changes, which is why I take the trouble to say things in a plain way and to keep saying them.

I am a woman of fifty-one. I have been a wife, a governess, a housekeeper, a sister, a teacher, a daughter, a mother, and I am now, in the end, simply a woman. I have had a husband who was unfaithful to me and one who was not, and one who let me go and one who died on a milk house floor. I have had four children and I have lost one. I have had money and have not had money. I have been on the parish and off it. I have been respectable and I have been talked about. And I say this plainly because it is true and because it is the whole of the case: if the man will not support the woman, the woman must support herself, and she will support herself as she best can, and there is no one on earth who can prevent it.

That last clause is not an argument. It is a fact, and it is the plainest fact I know, and there is not the slightest use in it to anyone. I have proved it seven years. I can read, and I can write, and I can keep accounts, and I can do the work of a man. And I cannot put the value of that up against the value of the roof over my head, the winter's coal, and the boy's name. There are three hundred women in this county and the wages that are paid to them are the wages that are paid to a man when he is sick and cannot work. I know the figures and I will not name them, because they are not a secret, and I will not print them because I have no purpose in printing them except to make people uncomfortable, and people are already uncomfortable enough.

I want to say the case in a way that a woman in a kitchen can carry in her head, because it is only useful if it can be carried in the head. A woman who is not paid has to be maintained. A woman who is maintained is a dependent. A dependent has no voice. A man who has a wife and no voice is not a man who is a man; he is a man who is half of a household, and the whole of him is not there, and this is why half the men of this country have nothing to say when the great questions are asked. I say this the more plainly because I have lived among them and they are good men and their wives are good women and they do the whole work and they are not paid a cent of it and they have no word in anything.

I want the reader to notice that this is a book about a person and not about a case. I have had four children and I have lost one. I had the education of a gentleman's daughter and I could not teach in the school of a town of four hundred souls because a gentleman will not take his daughter from a woman of forty-four who has been married, and I would have been glad to have taught Latin and Greek and mathematics in exchange for the small use of one room. That was offered me and it was refused me, and I am not a bitter woman over it. I do not think the committee was wicked. I think they would have been just as glad to have a man.

I do not say this is the fault of individuals. I have never seen an individual who could have prevented it. I do not say it is the fault of the church. The church has been good to me, and I have never gone to it for anything but a christening and a funeral, and I am not a Churchwoman and I am not a heathen. I do not say it is the fault of the state, and I will tell you why. That is the least of it. A state is a machine, and a machine is not made of blame. It is made of what people feed it, and if the people who feed it are half the human beings and feed it nothing but their children, then the machine will do what it has always done, and it will grind them, and they will thank it for grinding, because it has kept them in order.

They will say I want a revolution. I want nothing of the kind. I have never wanted to hurt anybody and I never shall. I want the world to be better, and I am willing to have it be better slowly. I want every woman to have what I have had, and I have had the worst of it, and I have had the good of it also, and it is time that some of it was given to the ones who have to do the drudgery of it. I want the men who live with these women to have a better time. I know they are not having a better time.

Here is the plainest thing I have to say, and I say it at the end because it is the one that has been proved on me and not by me. A woman is not the property of any man, and never was. I do not say that she is the property of herself, or of nobody, or of her children. She is not property. She has a right to herself, as much as a man has. And that is the whole of the case, and it can be put in eight words and carried to the ends of the earth.

That is the argument, and it is a true one, and it has been made before, and it will be made again, and it does not matter who makes it, and it will not be settled in this generation.

I have two children on a farm in the Berkshire hills, and a son in a city who does not use my name, and a hundred and eighty acres and a good pair of hands and a clear head for figures, and I have put in a paper-bound volume the whole of what I have to say, and it is going to the printer's this week with the notice on the fly-leaf that nobody is to tear it out and read it in the papers and that it is not to be lent, and I do not know what it will do. I have no hope of it. I have no fear. I am fifty-one years old and I have a woman of twenty-five in my kitchen who reads the papers aloud to me while I work, and she has never done a day's work in her life, and she is the daughter of a man who has kept house in her since she was born, and I cannot make her understand one word of what I am trying to say, and I am not going to waste time on it. I have not married again and I have no money and I have no name that anybody would recognize, and I have no time to wait for anything better.
