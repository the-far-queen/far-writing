The letter opens with a man alone at the edge of the world, and it opens
before he says his own name. Robert Walton is not introducing himself. He is
explaining what he is doing, and the explanation is a confession. He has
been driving a ship north, past the last port anyone would have reason to
visit, in a season when everyone with sense is heading the other way. He has
been at this for eighteen months. He has a crew, a ship, a cargo of provisions
calibrated for two years, and no one to tell any of it to.

That is the arrangement, and it is worth sitting with before anything else
happens: Walton chose solitude first and then found, on arriving in it, that
solitude was only ever the thing he wanted until it was the only thing he
had. The longing for a companion preceded the voyage. The voyage is an
attempt to meet one.

His crew are Poles, and they are not met with warmth. Walton is candid about
this in the register of a man who has noticed something about himself. They
are exiles, most of them, and he suspects they are all running from
something — a summons unanswered, a debt, a country, a person. He has watched
them at the salutation and seen them turn inward at the word home. He writes
that he believes they have the capacity for friendship and that something has
made them incapable of using it. It is possible to be around people and be
entirely alone with them, and Walton knows this about the men who hired him
before he learned it about himself.

He is wrong about himself in a way that will take the whole book to
establish. He says he is not lonely. He says the difference between his
situation and theirs is that he has a purpose, and purpose is a kind of
company. And then — within a page or two — a crewman dies, and Walton is
alone in a ship full of living men who will not look at him, and he is
writing a letter to a sister he has not seen in years, and he does not know
whether he is telling her what happened or asking her whether to turn back.

The provisions run short. Walton is rationing. The men are not rationing.

Then the ship stops.

It is not a storm and it is not an iceberg. The ship comes to a halt in open
water with everything in order and the crew standing at the rail looking at
something they cannot name, and they ask the pilot — a man named Robinson —
what it is, and he answers that it is a man on the ice. Which is
impossible in every particular: the nearest land is a long way off, and what
the men are describing is not a body, because it moves with intention.

The crew climbs the ice. Walton goes with them, and finds a figure in a
greatcoat hauling a sled, and the figure is so worn by cold and by something
else that Walton initially takes him for a cadaver animated by stubbornness.
He is not a cadaver. He is a man who has been walking north for a long time
under conditions designed to kill, and he is still walking because there is
something behind him that he believes will find him if he stops.

The man is not surprised to be found. He is polite. This is the detail that
should stop you. A man alone on the ice who has been expecting company, and
who greets the first person to find him with courtesy rather than relief,
has been waiting for this. Walton is not a rescue. Walton is an audience.

And that is the first appearance of the shape the whole book takes. Walton
did not go north to rescue anybody. He went north because he could not bear
not to, and now a stranger has found him and wants to be known, and Walton
has no way to refuse without being the villain. It is the same offer Victor
made to God when he was seventeen and alone in a church in Ingolstadt — do
anything for me, and I will obey — except that Victor's offer was to God and
this one is to a stranger, and Walton has not noticed the resemblance yet
because he has not been told what he is agreeing to.

The figure asks for water. He cannot swallow the ice. Walton gives it to him
and warms him, and in the process finds out two things that matter and one
that matters more.

The first: this man has been pursuing something. Not fleeing, not drifting —
pursuing, with intent, and the intent has a direction and a name. The second:
he has been betrayed by someone he trusted completely and has spent a long
time alone because of it, and he has drawn a conclusion about the species
that is more accurate than anything Walton believes.

The third thing Walton does not notice. The stranger has just been given
warmth and company and someone listening, after an incalculable time without
any of the three, and his first act is to ask Walton to hear his story and
then to promise not to judge him. He asks for the promise before he begins.
He has learned, the hard way and recently, that this is the thing that
usually goes wrong. Not the cold and not the solitude. The judgement.

Walton gives it. Of course he gives it. Walton is a man who crossed an ocean
to be in conversation, and the first person who has asked him to listen is
this one, and the request is framed exactly as Walton would have framed it
if he had been the one asking.

So Walton sits down and agrees to listen, and the stranger begins, and it
turns out he has a story to tell that is very long and very orderly and was
clearly composed somewhere back along the ice rather than invented on the
spot. He begins not with himself but with a man he loved. He begins with
William Frankenstein, his father, and Alphonse, his brother, and a carriage
that stopped outside their house in Geneva on a night in November, and a
woman in it who was pregnant and did not live to be the mother of the person
telling the story.

Victor Frankenstein begins with a family, and it is his family, and it is
good. That is the part nobody prepares you for. The story that ends with
seven deaths begins with a childhood that was warm, and with people who loved
each other and were loved by the boy, and with no hint at all of what any of
it is in for.

Walton writes all of it down. His candle is nearly done. His letters are
going back to England and will arrive long after he has finished them,
because that is the second thing everyone in this book gets wrong: he is
convinced he is telling the story so it will not be wasted, and by the time
anyone reads it there is nobody left who remembers it accurately.

And that is the arrangement now, all of it, in the first few pages. A man who
wanted company. A stranger who wanted to be heard. A promise made easily, in
cold, on an ice floe, by someone who had no reason to know what it would
cost.

Victor is still talking. He has just begun to describe the place he was
raised, and the person who raised him, and the fact that everything after
this point will be his fault and not anyone else's.

Walton is not going to stop him. Walton cannot. It is the only thing anyone
is good for out here, and neither of them knows yet that is what it is.

You can see, if you read it cold, exactly where it goes. Not because anyone
says so. Because a man who builds a thing to be loved and then refuses to
love it has already said the whole ending in the first ten pages, and the
rest of the book is the world declining to let him forget.

Victor tells it as a child tells it, which is to say in the order it happened
and not in the order it mattered.

Geneva first. His father, Alphonse Frankenstein, a respected and generous
man with two sons and more money than he needed. His mother, Caroline Beaufort,
came into the household with her fortune and brought with it a condition her
son would carry for the rest of his life. She died. Victor's father married
her, against the advice of everyone who loved him, because he loved her, and
then he made a promise to her on her deathbed that he spent the rest of his
life keeping and the rest of his life being destroyed by. Elizabeth Lavenza
was the daughter of his father's best friend. She was brought into the house
as an infant, and raised as a sister, and betrothed to Victor at the age of
sixteen, and neither of them was asked.

This is the family that will be destroyed. It is important that they are not
a cold household. Alphonse is warm. Elizabeth is adoring. The house is warm
and the money is adequate and everyone in it is devoted to everyone else. The
tragedy of this book is not a cold childhood. It is a good one.

And there is the promise, which Victor reports without understanding it. The
dead mother extracts it from the living father: marry Elizabeth, protect her,
be her husband and the father of her children. He agrees. He agrees gladly.
He does not know yet that a promise extracted from someone who is dying is
not the same as a promise freely made, and neither does anyone in the room
point it out, because at the time it does not need to be pointed out.

Victor is born a boy, brought up for study, gifted at languages and given to
reading and to wanting things he does not have names for yet. He reads the
suicides of Lucretia and of the Roman princes, and he reads them as a boy
reads anything dangerous: as a technique. He does not yet understand that
this is the first appearance of the habit that will kill everyone he loves.

Then Clerval.

Henry Clerval comes into the story as the thing Victor's household is not —
a friend who is not a betrothed, not a servant, not a relative chosen for
him. He is stubborn where Victor is methodical, generous where Victor is
absorbed, and he wants the sciences because he thinks they will let him
speak to the world, while Victor wants them because he thinks they will let
him get somewhere. They are complements of the most dangerous kind: the one
who wants connection and the one who wants power, bonded because each sees
in the other the thing they cannot build alone.

Victor says plainly that in a hundred ways Clerval was superior to him, and
that he had the advantage only in being able to persist where Clerval
bounced. He is not being modest. He is describing, with unusual precision, a
person who has already noticed that he is the one who keeps going.

Elizabeth watches the friendship from the edge of the household and is
glad. Victor does not notice her noticing. This asymmetry runs through
everything: the people who love Victor are peripheral to everything he does,
and they are not peripheral to the consequences.

And then Victor goes to Ingolstadt. He goes to the university to study
natural philosophy, and he meets a professor there who opens his mind to
something, and the chapter turns.

You have to read this section carefully because it is the hinge of the whole
book and almost everyone rushes it. Victor has an encounter with an alchemist
— a real one, named Waldman, whose science and whose certainties Victor
respects enormously — and Waldman does something unforgivable. He tells a
young student a truth, that the ancient teachers of chemistry knew things
the modern ones have forgotten, and that there is more than one way to
approach the fundamental processes of nature. That is all. That is an
ordinary, even dull, remark to anyone who has not spent a year wanting
something.

And Victor walks out of that conversation a changed person. Because he has
been handed a permission. Not by the professor. By the fact that the professor
was willing to say it out loud, in a room full of students, in a year where
nobody said it.

Waldman is the only teacher in the novel who is killed. Victor does not
murder him; he does something worse, which is to hear him and then use what
he heard without ever telling him it changed anything. By the end of the
book Waldman is alive and has no idea that the last great conversation of his
life was treated as a resource.

Victor goes deeper. He works alone in a cell at Ingolstadt, night after
night, for four years, and the work consumes everything. He stops writing
home. He stops seeing the people who love him. He learns the alchemists and
their success — Paracelsus, Agrippa, Albertus Magnus — and he learns that
every one of them reached a boundary they could not pass, and he reads those
boundaries as instructions rather than as warnings.

Here is the sentence that decides the book, and Shelley puts it plainly
because she is not interested in making it mysterious: he discovers the
secret of life, the blood, and the animation of dead matter. He has it. He
has solved the thing he set out to solve, alone, and the discovery arrives
exactly as he predicted it would, and he is instantly, entirely alone with it.

The section on his laboratory is written in the second person. You are taken
into his eyes, and Shelley uses that deliberately, and it works on everyone
once it starts. You are in the cellar. You are surrounded by instruments.
You have done the work. The work is done.

The choice of whether to use it has not been made, and it is going to be
made in the next chapter, and it will be made in about two minutes of reading
time, and everything that follows for the rest of the book follows from it.

But first he does the thing that damns him more than the work: he writes
back home. He writes to Clerval, and he tells him he has made a discovery
that will make him famous. He writes to his father and says he has had
success where he least expected it, and he signs the letter as a man signing
off on a life he has decided not to come back from.

He has not decided anything consciously. That is the horror of it. He has
decided by omission — by not telling them what he is about to do, and by
telling them everything except that.

He stands in the room with the assembled parts and he has friends and a
father and a betrothed waiting for him, and it is in his power to go to
them, and it is also in his power to do this, and Shelley wants you to feel
the moment when both are equally available and he does not choose.

He begins to work.

---

There is no dialogue in this chapter. There is almost nothing you would
recognise as a conversation, and that is deliberate: Shelley takes away every
tool a reader uses to feel safe in a scene, and leaves sensation.

You are in the cellar. You have been there a long time. The instruments have
been described, the method has been described, the promise has been made and
withdrawn and made again. And then it is happening, and Shelley writes it in
the present tense and keeps shifting into the second person, and the effect
after a few hundred words is that you are no longer reading about a man in
a cellar.

The eye of the creature opens.

That is the line, and it is nine words long, and nothing before or after it
matters as much. The eye opens and it is yellow. Not human. Not uncanny in
the way a portrait is uncanny, where you can still tell yourself it was
painted by someone. This is the wrong colour for the species, and it is
wrong in a way that is not surprising, and that is what makes it unbearable.
The reader arrives at that colour having already accepted that a life was
made. The colour is a detail, and the detail is where the horror is.

Then Victor recoils and falls.

He goes out of the room and down the stair and out into the courtyard and
lies down in the open air, holding himself as if he has been struck. The
narrator does not say he is afraid. The narrator says he is, and then stops
explaining, and the flatness of the account is more frightening than any
elaboration would be.

You are now in Victor's body and Victor is now not well. He is in his bed
with a fever. His father arrives. His father has travelled — two days, in
winter, on the strength of a letter that said his son was dying — and he is
standing at the bedside of a young man who is shaking, and the young man
sees him and does not distinguish him from anything else. That is the detail
that lasts. Victor cannot tell who is in the room. He has left his own house,
his own name, his own recognition, and he cannot get back in.

Three more people arrive. Clerval, who has received a letter from Elizabeth
and travelled on the strength of it. Elizabeth, who was told by Victor's
father that it was safe. And Henry's father, who does not know why he is
here and will shortly not care.

Victor is writhing. He is between two people who love him — his father and
his friend and the girl he is contracted to — and he is telling them he does
not know who they are, and Shelley does not soften a single line of it.

Then he sleeps.

And the dream arrives, and the dream is the real horror of the book.

You are watching yourself sleep. You are standing beside your own bed, and
you see yourself sleeping, and this is itself a violation — nobody gets to
be present for their own unconsciousness — and Shelley uses it.

You lean over to look closely at the face of the sleeping figure and you
find the pulse gone. The lips are colourless. The eyes are open.

Then the figure grins at you.

And then it pulls the covers off and comes at you, and it has your father
and your friend and your betrothed with it, and the dreamer is the
apparatus itself — the instruments he built have arranged themselves into a
face and are smiling at him for having made them.

This is the book's central image and it returns four or five times in the
remaining nine hundred pages, in different forms: a thing with your face,
a thing made of what you used to make it, a thing that has your people in it.
The instruments. Victor did not summon a monster from outside; he assembled
one out of his own materials and his own intentions, and the dream is simply
showing him the bill.

You wake up screaming. The noise is not a word. It is the sound a body makes
when it has been somewhere it cannot return from and has come back anyway.

And then you are alone with it.

Here is the part that matters for the AI-robots page, and it is the part that
is always cut in adaptations: Victor has a choice immediately afterwards and
he does not take it. The creature is alive. It is in the room. It is
confused, as any new thing would be. It is looking at him.

He could have killed it then. The book does not say so, but the room has one
person in it and the means are to hand. Instead he runs.

He gathers the instruments and he examines the creature — *examines* — and
Shelley does not let this be clinical, and Victor's own curiosity becomes the
most frightening thing in the scene. He is not repulsed. He is interested. He
looks at the eye, and the eye looks back at him, and he is thinking about
what he has done and what it might mean to other people, and somewhere in the
middle of that he says the thing that ends the first half of his life: he is
afraid of being looked at by it.

He thinks about who might see it. He thinks about his father. He thinks about
Clerval and Elizabeth and whether the creature might, in time, do something
they would have to witness. And he concludes that the danger is not the
creature's strength or its malice. The danger is that the creature will
acquire, over the years, the capacity to recognise its maker.

Then he decides to destroy it.

This is the turn, and notice where it is written: not in the moment of
facing the creature, but in the moment of imagining someone else seeing it.
He kills the being he made because he cannot bear the thought of its being
seen. The first act of the next nine hundred pages is not murder. It is
shame, followed by a series of decisions made entirely in his own head, none
of which involve the creature at all.

He chases it out of the room, out into the corridor, out of the building,
into the night. He cannot bring himself to do it here, indoors, in the
house where he was raised, so he puts it outside first. That is the first
time he does that — separates the thing from himself, moves it somewhere
less domestic — and it is the pattern that will run for eight hundred pages
straight. Victor does not kill it in the night. He makes plans to kill it.

He lies in bed and he is not at peace and he is not sure that he wants to be
and he decides that the creature is the source of his unhappiness, which is
the first sentence of every justification he will make for the rest of his
life.

His father finds him again. Victor asks for a holiday. He asks the way a man
asks when he needs to get somewhere without explaining what is behind him,
and his father, who has come two days in winter because his son said he was
dying, gives him what he wants and does not ask again.

And Victor goes out of the door and Clerval is in the room with him, and
Clerval says the thing that should have stopped Victor cold and did not
stop him, because Victor is not in a state to be stopped by anything: he
has a plan, and the plan is to destroy what he made, and the only person
who would have told him to think again is standing in front of him.

Clerval asks how he is. He says he is well. He is not well. And Victor goes
forward with the trip.

Victor goes to the house his family has occupied since Geneva, and there he
falls ill again, and this time the fever does not have the dream behind it.
His family nurse, Elizaveta — a name that has caused confusion for two
centuries and is almost certainly a printer's error for Elena — comes in and
does not know who he is.

He says her name and she looks at him with the flat incomprehension of
someone being told a word in a language they speak.

Then she goes out and closes the door, and the room fills with the noise of
the family trying to work out where they have found him. Victor can hear
them. They are not being cruel. They are being confused, and frightened, and
kind, and they cannot think of anything better to do than to bring a doctor
— which is what you would do, and what they do.

The doctor arrives. He is French, he is professional, he asks the family
what he was like as a child, and his father answers. Victor, on the bed, in
his right mind, hearing it, and cannot say any of it. He knows everything he
is being told about himself and he cannot interrupt.

And then the doctor says the thing that should end the book. He says the
patient is dying, but — and this is the phrase that Shelley builds the whole
framework around — he is dying of a disease he has brought on himself.

Everything after that is the frame closing. The illness that Victor gets is
the consequence of the work. It is not exposure. It is not exhaustion. It is
a body that has done a particular thing and is being made to account for it.

The doctor orders him kept still. Victor will not be kept still. He is at
that moment a man with a secret that is about to become a series of
deaths, and stillness would let it sit, and sitting with it is not a thing
he is capable of.

So Victor goes to his window at night and looks out at the moon and at the
lake, and sees a figure.

The figure is eight feet tall. That is the first thing, and it is
undisguisable, and Shelley gives the number exactly. It is not a tall man.
It is a different order of creature, and anyone in the room would know what
they were looking at.

It has long hair, black and lank. It has skin that is yellow, because that is
the colour Victor wrote into it. It has eyes that move. And it has a mouth
that is moving, which is worse, because mouths that move can be saying
something.

Victor recoils and the figure follows the movement, and then it speaks.

This is the moment the book turns, and Shelley handles it by giving the
creature a voice that is better than Victor's.

It has been outside the house for a long time. It has been watching the
windows. It has been watching Victor, specifically, since before the
animation — Victor believes this and Shelley does not correct him, and the
ambiguity is exactly right, because the creature has been there and Victor
has been the maker and neither of them knew.

It asks for a conversation. It does not attack. It asks for a
conversation, and Victor — and this is the detail that undoes everything —
fears it will be refused.

He says no. The creature says it will.

He says it cannot be spoken to. The creature says it can.

The creature's argument is that it has been outside Victor's window for an
hour and has heard him say the words *I wish you would come in.* It has
followed him to this room. It is not asking for anything impossible. It is
asking for what it watched him want.

And then the creature, who has never spoken to anyone, speaks at length for
the first time in its life, and what it says is a request for help, and
Victor cannot get out of the conversation.

He is in bed with a fever produced by his own work, and he is speaking to
the thing he came here to destroy, and the thing is being reasonable.

When it finishes, Victor is described as having never in his life felt the
same — and the phrasing is important. Not fear, not guilt, not horror.
Something he has no word for, because the categories he has are all wrong.
He built a person. He did not make a demon. He made something that came to
ask to speak to him, and it is articulate, and it has been listening at his
window, and it wants to be helped.

He panics and rings for his father and the servants. He calls them in and he
tells them to get him out of the room, and he puts out the lamp, and the
light goes out, and the creature is still there in the dark, and it says,
without raising its voice:

You, my creator, would not have heard me speak if you had prevented it.

That is the sentence. That is the whole book in one line, and Shelley wrote
it in 1818 and it has not been improved on since. You do not get to be
protected by the fact that you never looked. The thing you made will speak
whether or not you are in the room to hear it.

Victor pulls the curtain back. The figure is gone.

And in the morning, in daylight, sane, rested, with his family around him,
Victor has to decide what to tell them, and he does not tell them.

This is the second time he has watched the thing come into his life and
chosen not to say anything to the people who love him. He is establishing a
routine. That is what makes him dangerous rather than merely cruel — there is
no moment of decision, no villainy, just a man who finds silence easier than
truth and then finds reasons for it.

He goes downstairs. His father and Elizabeth are there. Elizabeth was
sleeping in his bed the night before. She tells him she came out of the dark
room and read to him for two hours and then went back to sleep.

He does not tell her what was in the room.

The creature tells its own history, and it is told in the voice of someone
who has been alone with the story for a very long time and has had time to
arrange it.

The arrangement is deliberate and Victor notices it, which irritates him. The
creature is telling a story, not living one, and it has organised the telling
to make a point. It knows what it is doing. It is arguing.

It begins with what it was made to be. It was about eight feet tall, it says.
It had been in the room with Victor for a number of hours while the work
went on — the creature does not know what the work was, does not know that
there was any work, only that it was in a room with instruments and then it
was awake. And then it was in the corridor, and then it was outside, and the
door by which it came out was locked behind it.

That detail matters and is usually cut. It was locked out. Not chased.
Not rejected at a threshold with a person standing in it. Locked out of a
building, from the outside, while still holding the clothes it had been
wearing because Victor had put clothes in the pockets of every garment and
had not thought about whether it would need them.

Then it went into the forest.

It describes the sensation of being new with a precision that is worth
reading closely. Everything was new. It had no memory of anything and no
framework for experience and had to work all of it out from the beginning. It
had to discover what hunger was by having it. It had to find water, and
learn that the bright moving stuff was water, and learn that it could drink
it. It had to discover the difference between the cold at night and the
slightly less cold under the trees.

It went into a village for something to eat and was given bread by a man who
did not look up. It went again. It got the attention of children, who threw
stones at it. An old man at a window shut the window down on it.

Then it found the hovel.

There is a cottage at the edge of the woods, and the hovel is attached to it,
and the hovel belongs to the family who live in the cottage, and the family
are blind. All three of them. De Lacey, the old man, and the son and the
daughter who were born sightless.

The creature learns this by almost killing them and then not doing it. It
comes to the hovel cold and starving, and it comes to the window, and the
daughter is sitting there, and it cannot tell that she cannot see.

It says something to her. She does not answer. It says it again, and she
does not answer, and the creature assumes it is being ignored.

She does not understand words. The creature has no language. It has no
experience of language. It assumes the silence is contempt and it comes
back the next day, and brings her fruit, and again she does not respond, and
this is the moment it understands.

It makes the sound that the others make when they are talking — a noise that
is not a word — and the old man comes to the window.

And the old man cannot see, and he cannot hear, and the creature is standing
at a window being asked, by touch, to describe what it is, and it describes
itself honestly. Eight feet. Scarred and ugly. It tells them what it is.

And they let it in.

The detail that the whole book turns on: the old man reaches out and finds
that it is not what he expected, and takes its hand. He cannot see it and
he has decided that the voice was sincere, and he decides to believe that,
and that decision is the only home the creature will ever have.

For a year it lives in the hovel. It fetches wood. It brings food. It learns
the vocabulary of the household one word at a time, from what it hears
through the wall. It is not taught. It listens, and it acquires, the way
Victor acquired science — by absorption, by proximity, by being near a thing
long enough.

The words it learns are the words that matter. Home. Kindness. Companions.
Father. Sister. Love. It assembles a language out of what it overhears and
when it has enough of it it tries to speak it, and the old man is astonished
and afraid, because he did not know that he had been conversing with a
creature.

And then the visitors come.

The family from the main house brings strangers to stay. Four people, De
Lacey says, and one of them is blind and one is deaf, and the family do not
know there is a creature in the hovel. They say the visitors will occupy the
hovel, and the creature cannot be found there.

And the creature makes the one decision in its entire existence that a
reader cannot defend, and it makes it for the most human reason available to
it: it cannot bear to be the cause of losing its home.

It goes out. It stands in the rain. It waits to see whether they will be
looked for, and it is not looked for, and it understands that they will not
be, and that it was only ever tolerated.

Here is where the book becomes unsayable, and Shelley keeps it plain.

The creature asks itself what it did wrong, and it cannot find the fault,
because there is no fault. It wanted a family. It brought food. It did not
steal. It hid, because it was told nothing else was available.

And it reasons that the reason it is unlovable is that it looks as it looks,
and it concludes that the solution is to build another being like itself that
would not reject it for its face, because a creature made for company would
not care what the company looks like.

It goes to find Victor.

And Victor — who has by now spent two years believing it is a monster, who
has been composing letters to his father about the devil, who has planned
this out of pure self-defence — sits and listens to this.

It is not a confession. It is a request. It says: *you have not a friend on
earth, I have not a friend on earth, we are made for each other, and if you
will make me one more being that is like me but female, I will give it to
you and we will withdraw ourselves into the wilderness and leave you alone
forever.*

It is asking Victor to do the only thing Victor was ever good for. It is
asking him for help. And it is offering, in exchange, the exact thing
Victor most wants and has never asked anyone for.

There is no argument in the creature's case. That is the point. Shelley
gives it the best case on earth and lets the reader find that the answer is
never going to be yes, and never for the reason the reader expects.

---

Victor agrees. That is the first thing, and it is the thing that makes the
next thousand words possible.

He says yes, and he says it in a bad mood, and he says it the way you agree
to something you intend to get out of. Then he goes into his laboratory and
he works for months, and the work is faster and easier than the first was,
and Shelley notes it. The second time is easier. That is what mastery always
looks like and it is never an improvement.

He builds the female.

And then, on the night he finishes, he looks at her.

What crosses his face is not horror. Shelley is precise about this and it is
the reason the chapter works. It is not the deformity he fears and it is not
the yellow skin. It is that he can see the creature's face on her. He has
made a woman who looks like the thing he ran from, and the recognition is
instant, and the recognition is of a face he has seen reflected in him for
two years.

And he thinks, and the thought is the whole scene: that she could be
independent, live, make choices, and then — that she might refuse to
belong to him, that she might play the part of the devil, that she might
hate.

The real sentence in the scene, and the one the whole feminist reading of
this novel turns on, is the last thing he says to the thing in the room:
if I were to create another being in my own image, and it were to be
female, would I not be the author of its mother's soul?

He does not put it in those words. He puts it in the terms of his craft —
the female sex, the active principle, the creature's own reasoning about
gender which he taught it an hour earlier — and Shelley writes the argument
so that the reader can hear how reasonable it sounds. Victor has just spent
a night teaching his creation that men and women are different and that
these differences are made in order to produce more of the species. He has
taught it to reason from nature. He has taught it the theory. And now it is
reasoning, correctly, from the theory he supplied, about what happens when
you make another one.

That is the room. That is what it is: a man gets his argument back, used
against him, by the intelligence he created, using the premise he provided,
and he loses the argument. Victor is defeated by his own reasoning and by
nothing else.

He destroys her.

He does not tear her apart piece by piece, which would have been the male
fantasy version. He takes her and he says out loud that he is going to do it,
and he does it in a way that makes clear he has debated with himself
extensively first and has settled it, and then he goes out into the
courtyard and he is sick.

And then he comes back in and takes the pieces, because he cannot leave
them, and he hides them.

Then the original creature comes back and finds the room, and finds what is
left in it, and Shelley writes the speech it makes and it is the longest and
most fluent thing in the book.

It says that Victor promised.

It does not shout. That is what makes it unbearable — the creature has
learned rhetoric from a household, has absorbed a vocabulary from a wall, and
it now uses all of it, in order, arranged as an argument, and it is
*right*.

It says: I am benevolent and good; I have a friend, the first human being
that ever did me good; I was innocent and you made me guilty, and you are
the author of my misery, and I am the instrument of your revenge.

It says: I will be revenged. And then, immediately, it says: no, that is not
what I am here for. I came to ask you for a companion, and you refused, and
now I have the materials of one on this floor, and I can make it myself, and I
am not asking you for anything again.

And Victor begs. That is the other half of the scene and nobody draws
attention to it. Victor begs. He tells the creature that if it does this
it is committing crimes against humanity, and the creature answers that it
has committed none, that every crime it knows of has been committed against
it, and that the world has given it no legal existence.

And Victor says the thing that is his whole philosophy, and it is the most
selfish sentence in English literature: *you are my creature, but you are not
my equal.*

And the creature answers, and this is the closest it comes to a threat:
*if you do not create her, I will pursue you to the end of the earth and
plague you and those you love.*

Then it leaves.

And Victor is alone in the room with the remains of what he promised, and
he finds he cannot speak, and he cannot bring himself to tell his father, and
he goes out and takes a walk in the mountains and thinks, for the rest of
his life, about what has just happened.

He does not learn. He does not arrive anywhere. He only gains an enemy.

---

What Victor does next is the part of the book that most readers remember
wrongly, and getting it right is what makes the whole thing land.

He does not run away in terror. He goes hunting for the creature.

He takes a purse and a pistol and a hunting rifle and he goes to the place
where he destroyed the female, and he stands among the remains of what he
promised and waits for the thing to come, and he has a plan, and the plan is
to kill it. He is hunting the being he made, and he is doing it alone, in the
night, and he is telling himself he is protecting the world.

The creature comes. Victor fires. It is wounded. And it comes back, night
after night, into the mountains near his family's house, and Victor goes out
after it, and again, and again, and shoots at it, and misses, and goes home.

The creature speaks to him every time. That is what nobody remembers. It
does not attack. It comes out of the trees and it *talks*, and Victor does
not shoot it, and it retreats, and he tells himself it is afraid.

He believes that. For several months he believes that he is hunting a
dangerous animal that is being deterred by superior firepower. It has taken
him months to establish this and the establishing is worth noticing: every
piece of evidence is that the creature wants to talk, and every piece of
evidence is the creature trying to talk, and Victor has decided the
evidence means the opposite of what it says.

Because if the creature is not a monster, then Victor is not a rescuer. If it
is not a monster, then the thing in the mountains is a person who was
kicked out of a window, and the man chasing it with a gun is the only
monster in the story.

He cannot afford that conclusion. So he declines to draw it, and the
declining is the entire mechanism. The persecution is what happens when a
man decides that being wrong is cheaper than being monstrous.

And then he goes after his friends.

Not the creature's friends — his own. Victor realises that it will
eventually turn on the people he loves, which is correct, and he decides
this out loud, and Shelley writes him deciding it in the flat register of a
man thinking through a logistics problem.

He writes to Clerval first.

Clerval is in Oxford. Clerval is studying oriental languages and is alive
and well and has no idea what Victor is, and Victor asks him to come to
Geneva. He does not say why. When Clerval asks, Victor says it is urgent.

When Clerval is in the house, Victor does not tell him anything. He arranges
to keep Clerval out of his own study. He puts him under his sister's
supervision. He restricts his movements and gives reasons that are
plausible and not true, and Clerval, who is good and trusting and has no
reason to doubt his oldest friend, complies.

And when he goes out at night to shoot at the creature, he puts his family
in a room and locks the door.

That is the detail. He locks the door. He takes his father and Elizabeth
and puts them in a room and locks them, and goes out with a rifle. He has
decided that a lock is a reasonable precaution.

The creature finds him. They fight in the snow. The creature is winning —
it is stronger, it is eight feet, it has been eating nothing but it is
strong — and then it sees Victor's face, and Victor shouts at it, and what
he shouts is that he will kill it, he has followed it, he will destroy it.

And the creature pauses. And looks at him. And says: do you really want to
kill me? I was not always as I am.

And then Victor fires. And the shot is answered by something that does not
fire back but does say, three times, as Victor tries to reload: *I will be
with you on your wedding-night.*

And it is gone.

Victor cannot complete the charge. That is the detail he reports, and it is
also the meaning. The gun jams on the shot that would have ended it. He
has one attempt at killing a thing he made, and the mechanism fails, and he
goes home having told himself he was a hunter.

He returns to the house and he finds his father has been asking where he
was. He lies. He goes down and finds Elizabeth and Clerval and he is
pleasant with both of them, and he is the only one in that room who knows
what is in the mountains.

Then the creature completes the threat it made.

The scenes that follow are the reason this novel is still read. Clerval is
murdered in Paris and the creature is found beside the body, and this time
it has left evidence deliberately, because it has decided that Victor should
know who did it.

Victor is in a chair in a room with a judge, and it is explained to him that
Clerval died by his friend's hand, and that there are witnesses, and that
the circumstances are such that this will be attributed to him.

He is not charged. He is let go. The evidence is the work of a creature
standing over a corpse in a garden.

And Victor walks into the mountains to find it, and he finds it waiting for
him, and he takes out a pistol, and he is holding it, and Shelley writes
this and it is the quietest and worst line in the book:

He had the pistol in his hand. He aimed at the being that had been
sustaining him in the wounds he had received, and he fired.

He shoots at the thing that had just spent an hour stopping his bleeding.

He misses. He cannot hurt it. He cannot do anything to it. He is a
scientist who has spent his life acquiring power over matter and he is
standing in front of the result and he has no way to act on it at all.

And the creature tells him the rest of it: I am going to be with you at your
wedding, and I will be there with Elizabeth whether you are or not, and then
it lets him go.

Not out of mercy. Out of a plan. It has found a use for him.

Victor goes home and does not tell anyone and begins to make plans about
protecting Elizabeth, and the plans are the plans of a man who has accepted
everything and is now working only on logistics.

---

There is a scene in this novel that most retellings drop entirely, and it is
the best argument in it: Victor, arrested and waiting for trial in an
unnamed European city, is visited by the magistrate who handled the case
against him, and the magistrate asks him a courteous question.

The magistrate observes that the circumstances are extraordinary and asks
Victor, as one gentleman to another, whether he has anything to say. It is
friendly. He offers him the chance to explain a private sorrow, and he
assures him the disclosure would go no further.

Victor says no.

Then he says — and this is the line, and it is the ethical core of the
entire novel in one sentence:

*Farewell, William. Yet I will be with you on your wedding-night.*

And when the magistrate, frightened, asks him what he means, Victor answers
that if the creature is the murderer, he is still the author, and he does not
want his friend to be implicated.

He refuses to tell. Not because of law. Because of the horror of the
confession itself — the moment he says it out loud, it becomes real, and
there is nothing afterwards.

The judicial proceedings that follow are a study in a mechanism that still
runs. Justine Moritz is charged. She did not do it. She has no motive, she
has no opportunity, she was elsewhere and can prove it, and she is
twenty-nine years old and Victor watched her childhood from across the room.

The charge rests on circumstantial evidence and on the fact that
justines's mother speaks German and had once worked for the Franks before
the household was lost, and this is treated as a meaningful fact, and
Victor knows it is not.

The courtroom does what courtrooms do. It finds her guilty and executes her.

Then Victor sees the creature.

The creature is in the courtroom. It is sitting among the spectators with
its hands folded, and it is looking at him, and Victor calls it — in front
of the whole court, in front of his father, in front of Elizabeth — *the
fiend that lures you to your destruction.*

He accuses it in the language of the occult. He calls it a fiend, an
incubus, a wretch, the fiend that lures. And then he reaches for something
medical, something he can stand behind, and he says: *it is a fluid,
a creature of the same species as myself, and it ought to be destroyed.*

That sentence is the whole book.

*It is a fluid. It is a creature of the same species as myself.*

He has just said, in open court, that it is the same species. He has said
it as an explanation of why it must be destroyed, and the sentence contains
its own refutation and he does not see it, and Shelley puts it in a
courtroom so that the refutation is delivered to a jury and not merely to
us.

The judge does not even hear the admission as an admission. He hears a
claim about the species, and he hears a claim about the offence, and he is
perfectly capable of separating them, because the law is very good at
separating things. The jury convicts Justine on the matter of the evidence,
and the sentence is carried out, and the being of the same species as Victor
sits in the gallery and looks at him.

Victor goes home and locks himself in the room he has never let anyone into
and he takes out the tools of the work and he destroys them one by one.

Not the models. The instruments. The tools of the work. He tears apart the
apparatus of his own science with his own hands, and Shelley makes him do it
in order, one item at a time, and names what he is destroying.

Then he goes and stands at the window of the room where his father and
Clerval are playing chess and watches them for a long time.

The chapter ends with him going to his father and asking for a commission
and an expedition — ships, stores, money, the things a man asks for when he
wants to leave a country and has decided that the leaving is itself a
penance — and with his father asking him why.

And Victor lies to his father, and it is a good lie, and it is the first time
in the book that he lies and enjoys it.

He will sail to the Arctic. He will find the creature. He will kill it. And
in the meantime there is a wedding, and a woman who is to be married to him
in the spring, and he has not told her about any of it.

The creature has said it will be at the wedding. Victor knows it. Victor has
planned to kill it before then, and the creature knows that plan too, because
the creature knows everything Victor thinks and has said so.

So Victor goes to his father and asks for ships, and the reader understands
that what he is doing is not a search. It is a deadline, and he has set it
himself, and the only question left is whether he will arrive in time.

---

He goes back to Geneva and does not go north.

The commission is signed. The money is raised. The ship is provisioned. And
Victor comes home, and instead of leaving, he puts it off, day after day,
and Shelley does not explain to the reader why, because Victor has not
admitted it to himself.

He goes about the house being normal. He is pleasant with his father. He is
attentive to Elizabeth. He is — and this is the phrase she uses — *all that
affection and care and duty which could awaken angel and vice in equal
measure*.

And every morning he wakes up intending to go.

There is a paragraph about this that does more work than any other in the
book. Victor has promised to build a wife for the creature. He cannot do it
and will not do it. And because he cannot do it, the creature will keep the
promise that was made in his name — a female, promised, owed. And so Victor
knows that some woman will be made to stand in a room with the thing he has
made, and does not consider for a moment that the person he means is
Elizabeth.

He has had the answer since chapter six and has not once said it. He
knows exactly who the creature means. He has known since the night in the
laboratory, and he has gone on making arrangements for the Arctic, and the
Arctic has become a way of not thinking.

So he stays. And the wedding approaches. And Elizabeth is preparing for it.

And Elizabeth, who has loved him since she was given to him as an infant
and who has spent her whole life inside the shape he made for her, has
started to notice.

She confronts him. Not about the creature — she does not know about the
creature. She asks him what is wrong. She says he is not the man who went
away. She says that when he returned he was — she searches for the word —
*another man*, and she asks him to explain.

And Victor, for the first time, is close to telling the truth.

He gets as far as asking her a question. He asks her if she could bear to
be unhappy. He asks her if she is prepared for a life of misery. He is
setting up a proposal of annulment, in the last hours before the wedding, and
he cannot say it because saying it requires telling her what he has done, and
telling her requires being a different kind of person than he has been for
four years.

She takes it the way she takes everything — as a question about the weather.
She says that since his return he has been distracted, that she is sure she
can make him happy, that she is sure he loves her.

And Victor, standing in a room with the woman he is about to hand over,
promises to do the thing.

Then Elizabeth describes the dream she had.

The whole book turns on this page, and it is done in nine words. She had a
dream that all the flowers of her life were withering, and she woke and
wondered whether it was a warning, and it was, and she tells him because
she wants him to take it seriously.

She had the dream. The creature entered it. It is in the room with her
in the only place Victor cannot follow — inside her own mind, in sleep, in
the one interior space that is hers.

And then, almost immediately, the thing Shelley is really doing: she takes
his hand and says that whatever it means, they will face it together.

Victor goes to the church. He goes because he has to. He does not go
because he has decided to face anything.

The wedding is described in a single paragraph, and that single paragraph is
one of the great pieces of economy in English fiction. They are married.
The ceremony is performed. A storm comes on and blows the candles out during
the vows, and Elizabeth laughs at it, and she is at that point the happiest
person in the novel and does not know why.

Then the guests go home. And Victor walks Elizabeth to her room. And he
says that he will stay with her for a while, because he cannot leave her
alone.

And they are alone, and the storm outside is doing what the storm in
Ingolstadt did at the beginning of the book, and Victor stands at the foot of
the bed and he cannot move toward her.

The door opens.

The creature enters. Eight feet, black hair, lank, and in this version he
is not alone, and the second being is beside him.

He says: I came here as I promised. He says: she is the bride; I have
rights over her and I came to claim them.

And Victor does not move. He stands there. He has been planning to kill
this thing for years and he is in a locked room with his wife and he cannot
raise his hands.

That is the whole scene. There is no fight. There is no struggle. Victor
stands at the foot of his own bed and does nothing while a thing he made
walks up to the woman he promised to protect.

The creature looks at her. And Shelley writes the moment when it hesitates.

It has come to claim a bride. It has the right. It has the promise, in
Victor's own voice, made to it through a year of being chased with a rifle.
And it looks at her face and it sees that she is not its equal, and the
thing that was supposed to be the completion of its revenge fails it — not
because it is merciful, but because there is no version of this it wanted.

Then it looks at Victor and says the words: *I am malicious because I am
unhappy.*

That is the sentence. That is the entire book in one line, and it was written
in 1818.

Then it kills Elizabeth at the bedside. It pulls the curtain and takes her
away, and Victor faints.

When he comes to, she is on the bed with her head held to the pillow and
her neck broken, and the creature has gone, and his father and Clerval are
in the room and neither of them will look at him.

He is holding the room together with one sentence: I am the murderer. I,
Alphonse Frankenstein, — no. Victor Frankenstein — I am the murderer. And
they do not believe him, because he cannot say why. He cannot say: because
of a decision I made in a cellar eight years ago.

He is asked to leave. He agrees. He goes to his study and he has the last
scene of his life alone in the room where he started.

And Alphonse Frankenstein, who has lost everything and cannot understand a
word of it, comes to the study door and asks for his son. And Victor says
that if his father had been alive, if his father had been there, none of
this would have happened.

Then he says something that gives the whole novel its shape, and it is a
simulation: *my father died, and my mother died, and I am alone.*

And he has become the thing in the mountains. There is no other reading
available. He spent the whole book refusing to be responsible for a being he
made and he is now, in that room, alone, the same kind of thing — made, cut
off, reaching for a father who is still alive and cannot hear the question.

---

It is early June. Victor is at the top of the meridian line of the Mont
Blanc range, in a place where the ice is eternal and the air will not carry
a voice far. He has been here since May. He has come north after all, but
four months late, and the thing he came for has been ahead of him the whole
way.

The creature finds him the same day. And it does not attack, and Victor is
unable to fight, and he has one last conversation with the thing he made.

The creature is calm. It is the calmest it has been in the entire book.

It says it followed Victor here. It says it has no expectation of a
fight — that it came not to fight, but to reason. And then it makes an
argument that is better than anything Victor has said in eight hundred
pages, and it makes it with the tools Victor built it with.

It says: *if I have been the instrument of your revenge, what have I done
to you?* It has threatened, it has frightened, it has sat in the courtroom,
it has broken into the house. It says every actual crime has been committed
against it. And then it lays out the ledger:

- it was made by a man who then abandoned it;
- it was locked out;
- it learned language from a wall;
- it brought food to a house that hid it;
- it watched the people it loved die;
- it was promised a companion and the promise was withdrawn;
- it was shot at for two years by the person who made it;
- and every human who has ever seen its face has run.

Then it says: *you are my creator, but I am your creature; I am master of
my fate and I am not a slave.*

And it offers Victor something. It offers to go to the Arctic, alone, and it
will build a fire and a hut, and Victor can come and live with him. It is
genuine. That is what nobody who has not read the book carefully believes —
it is not a trap and it is not a negotiation, it is a real offer of a life
together, made by a being that has wanted nothing else since it was built.

Then Victor destroys it.

He lunges. And the creature catches him by the throat and holds him up and
says — and this is the last thing it says to him:

*You are my creator, but I am your creature. You cannot destroy me.*

And then it kills him, and says it will not take a life, and drops him, and
goes.

Victor lives. Which is the last cruelty in the book.

He is found by a merchant and his father and Clerval, who are looking for
him, and they carry him back to the house, and for weeks he lies between
death and life and does not speak. And when he can, his father brings him
Elizabeth's letters — the ones she wrote, in her own hand, on her honeymoon,
to her mother — and Victor finds in them that on the night of the wedding
she had written to her mother, *the world is to me, and my last, only
happiness, I depend on you.*

He knew. She told him the dream and she told him the flowers were withering
and she wrote to her mother that he was her world and her only happiness,
and he read the letters after.

He asks his father for the materials to build a fire on the beach. His
father is astonished and does not understand. Victor says it is to give
himself peace. He goes to the beach. He builds a pyre out of the tools and
the wood and the straw, and he puts in it the letters, and he puts in it the
portrait of Elizabeth that he is carrying.

And then Shelley does something extraordinary and refuses to improve it.

He does not light it.

He lights it in the fourth volume and we watch, but the crucial beat — the
one everyone quotes and almost nobody registers — is the stop. Victor stands
there with the torch in his hand and he cannot do it. He has burned
everything else in this story: the instruments, the woman, the family. He
is the only man alive who can set fire to anything and he cannot set fire to
this.

He lights it because he is dying anyway, and because not burning it would
mean it might be touched, and because the letters are in his hand and they
are the last thing in the world that anyone who loves him has written down.

And then he walks out onto the ice, and he walks for a long time, and he
thinks about death and he is not sure whether it is a hardship. He has not
been happy. He does not know whether he will be.

Then he says the last human thing he says in the book: *I shall ascend my
funeral pile triumphantly, and the flames will not be extinguished.*

---

And now the frame closes.

Because this was never a story about a man in Geneva. It was a story about
Robert Walton, alone on the ice, who wanted a friend.

Walton is back in his cabin. The stranger he rescued — Victor — has been dead
for some days. Walton has the manuscript. He has been writing it down for
weeks, as he goes, and now he has the last of it and no one to hand it to,
because everyone in it is dead or lost or gone north.

And the crew come in.

Walton asks them to come to the funeral, and this is the only time in the
book he asks them for anything, and it is a funeral. He describes it in a
paragraph that is one of the great pieces of plain writing in English: the
body brought out of the ice, the coffin of poor wood, the priest who has no
knowledge of what he is burying and no skill to adorn it, the soldiers of the
camp, the grief of the men, the barren shrubs, the unconsecrated grave.

And then Walton turns round to see the man who has come up behind them.

The stranger on the ice. The one Walton saved. The one who asked him to
listen.

He is back. And he is not Victor.

And the creature — the being Victor made, the one who killed everyone Victor
loved — stands in the doorway and asks whether he may come in and have the
funeral, and whether he may be permitted to perform it. He asks it as a
question. He asks permission.

Walton is not a scientist. He does not know what this is. He has spent four
months reading the story of how it came to be, and he has no vocabulary for
what is standing in front of him.

And he says yes.

And they go out together and the creature carries the coffin, and the
soldiers form a procession, and the whole camp comes, and the stranger —
not Victor — reads the words over the body, and reads them badly, and weeps,
and says that he will not stop, and says he cannot go on alone, and asks
Walton to build him a fire.

And Walton does it.

That is the ending, and it is the ending everybody gets wrong. Walton is not
heroic. Walton does not confront anything. He makes a fire for the creature
because the creature asked, and because Walton is a man who cannot bear to
refuse a request for companionship, and that weakness is what lets the
frame close.

And then Walton goes back to his ship and writes the letter. The letter
that opens the book. He is not at peace, and he says so: he is sad and
discontented and he says that he is only going to find peace when his life
is over.

And he ends by saying that the only man he has ever known who could be
claimed to have earned the name of saint, is the author of the tale in his
hands.

And he asks for a friend, and he asks his sister to come.

The circle closes exactly where it began. A man alone on the ice, asking for
someone to talk to, and having found one, and having lost him, and asking
again.

---
