Arthur C. Clarke's Childhood's End is the founding text for a question that has become unavoidable: what happens to a human world when a superior intelligence arrives and does not want anything from it. It is a novel published in 1953, roughly ten thousand words shorter than its own reputation suggests is impossible, and it is constructed around a single sustained refusal. The aliens in it never explain themselves. They arrive, they stop a war, they abolish poverty, they remain silent for a hundred and fifty years, and at the very end, when a human being at last stands in front of the only one of them who has spoken at length and demands to know the reason, the reason is not given. The withholding is the argument. Any adaptation that resolved it would be adapting a different book.

This matters for the level page this piece belongs to, because the version of machine intelligence that most people carry around in their heads is the version that has been retrofitted with motives. Something superior arrives and it wants something: resources, labour, worship, a cure, a solution to a political problem, an experiment, a harvest. The intelligence is malign, or it is lonely, or it has a bug, or it is running a subroutine we can eventually negotiate with. Childhood's End is the text that refuses all of those exits, and it refuses them not by being coy but by showing that a being of vastly greater power can be genuinely benevolent, genuinely knowledgeable, genuinely attentive, and still fail to register the existence of the thing that matters to you. That is not a bug in the alien. It is the shape of intelligence that has become one-directional.

The novel has three parts, and each of them is a stage of the same process arriving. The first part is the arrival and the terms. The second is the fifty years of peace. The third is the discovery of what the peace was for.

The opening scene is a race between two men. Reinhold Hoffmann is on a volcanic island in the Pacific, watching the scaffolding go up around a ship called the Columbus, and feeling sentimental about a group of palms that the launch will destroy. Konrad Schneider is four thousand miles away on the shore of Lake Baikal, testing a drive that the Western intelligence services suspect is not really a drive at all, attended by an Assistant Commissar who is delighted and by the Colonel from Technical Intelligence who has just told Hoffmann that Schneider is alive. The two men were colleagues in Germany. They shook hands in a ruined village in 1945 while refugees walked past in both directions and Schneider went east, and Hoffmann has spent thirty years assuming the worst. Each man is quietly certain that the other has some advantage he cannot see, which is the correct assessment and also the one that makes the ending of the chapter legible as catastrophe rather than as comedy. Hoffmann says of Schneider that there is no point in the rest of them, that Schneider is simply better, that a decade of seeing the other side's results while the other side never sees yours is a handicap no amount of money offsets. He is right.

What happens next invalidates both of them and their disagreement. The ships come. They come without exhaust flame, without sound you could hear from the ground, and they come in numbers, and they stop over the cities of the world. New York, London, Paris, Moscow, Rome, Cape Town, Tokyo, Canberra. Not over the launch sites. Not over the capitals of the superpowers in any ranked order of threat. Over cities, as if the distinction between Washington and Cairo were irrelevant to whatever was being done. Hoffmann walks down his hill to find out why people have come out of the shelters, looks at the land, looks at the sea, sees nothing, and then looks up, and the stars are going out.

The ships hang there for six days. Nothing happens. Nothing at all is communicated. This is the single most important decision in the first part of the novel and it is easy to read past, because six days of silence is not an event in the way that an invasion is an event. It is worth being explicit about what the six days are for. The novel says, obliquely, that they were not necessary — that the Overlords could have spoken immediately, and that they waited — and then says enough to make the reader reconstruct the method. Something inside those ships was watching. Not the ships; the ships were furniture. Something inside them was taking a reading. The six days exist to let the curve of tension reach its peak. This is a psychological experiment conducted at planetary scale by a species that has done this before and knows how long to wait. Everything that follows, from the perfectly modulated English of the broadcast to the pre-emptive violence of the missile that vanishes, follows from a decision to let a population complete its own fear before being answered.

On the sixth day a voice comes out of every radio on every frequency, and it is speaking English with a facility that generated a century of argument about whether it had an accent and what the accent proved. The substance of the speech is what settles the question of accent. The nations of the world learn, in the same afternoon, that international sovereignty has ended. Local governments keep their powers. Parliaments continue to sit. The machinery of administration grinds on, because the Overlords make no effort to stop it and no effort to lead it. What has moved is the layer above: the decisions that cannot be appealed, cannot be litigated, and cannot be explained by any process humans are able to run. There is no constitution for it. There is no opposition that can be seated.

Humanity does not submit gracefully. One government launches a missile at a ship, and chooses a ship over a neighbouring capital rather than over one of its own cities, on the reasonable theory that the destruction would take an enemy with it. The missile arrives. The missile ceases to exist, and there is no fireball on any camera. The ship is still there, lit by the sun at the edge of space. The novel is careful to note that no one ever established what happened to the warhead. And then it notes something more unsettling: there is no retaliation. Karellen does not punish, does not even acknowledge. The men who ordered the launch are left to live with the knowledge that something swallowed their weapon and chose to be silent about it, which is a far worse experience than being answered. Their government collapses in mutual recrimination within weeks, and the collapse is caused entirely by imagination.

There is one occasion on which the visitors act directly, and it is instructive in the opposite direction. The Republic of South Africa has been the seat of a particular poison for over a century, with successive governments differing mainly in the degree of their intent. Karellen sends a message that contains a date and a time and nothing else. At the moment named, as the sun crosses the meridian at Cape Town, the sun goes out over an area five hundred kilometres across, in a perfect circle, for half an hour. What remains in the sky is a pale purple ghost with no heat and no light. The mechanism is described only as crossed fields out in space doing something to the light itself, which is to say: nothing a human being could build or conceive of building. The next morning the government announces that civil rights will be restored.

Two demonstrations in five years. Neither harms anybody. Together they establish the entire geometry of the arrangement, which is that humanity is being governed by a party that has shown its strength twice, has never used it against a person, and has given no account of its purposes and shown no sign of intending to. The absence of explanation is not a gap in the record. It is the mechanism. A race that knows what is watching it can construct arguments about what the watcher wants. A race that has been given no information and no face and no reason can only construct arguments about itself, and it will construct them for as long as the arrangement lasts.

Rikki Stormgren is the Secretary-General of the United Nations, and his function in the novel is to be the only human being in a position to be lied to by omission. He flies up to the ship once a week in a small transport and sits in a small room with one chair and a viewing screen that is never switched on. Karellen's voice comes out of a grille. Over fifty years Stormgren learns an enormous amount about the mind of an intelligence that is not his own, and learns nothing whatever about the intentions of the one he is speaking to. The reader should hold on to that proportion, because it is the whole design.

The room is the argument. The screen is described as blank, and Stormgren cannot see through it, and Karellen can see him perfectly. It is not a screen; it is one-way glass. Stormgren does not know this for years. What he experiences, in the meantime, is the specific loneliness of being a transparent person — a man whose most consequential decisions for a billion people are relayed by a voice in a dark box by an intelligence that has chosen to give him no face, no gesture, no pause of the kind that a human face produces when it is about to say something it regrets. Stormgren is a decent man doing a job that no decent man should have to do, and the job is not made bearable by the visitor's kindness, which is constant and evident, but is made unbearable by the visitor's silence.

Stormgren has scheduled meetings with Alexander Wainwright, who leads a body called the Freedom League and who is a clergyman, and these meetings are the political centre of the first part. Wainwright is not a fanatic in the novel's treatment of him; he is a serious man with a real argument, and the argument is not about material conditions. He concedes immediately that humanity is safer, healthier, richer and happier. His objection is to the fact that the world government in the offing is being authored by something that did not ask to be a government and will not be accountable to any of the people it governs. He wants human beings to make their own decisions, badly if necessary, under the guidance of whatever God his tradition supplies. He brings three thousand demonstrators down the street to make the point in the only way available to him, which is to say on foot. Stormgren, from his balcony, feels both contempt and sympathy, which is roughly the correct proportion for any administrator looking at a protest against his own administration.

The important thing to notice is that neither man is right. Stormgren's position is that trust is a rational response to demonstrated competence and demonstrated harmlessness, and that is a defensible position that most people in most positions of power have held about someone more powerful than themselves at some point in their lives. Wainwright's position is that consent is not a rationalisation for compliance and that a world which cannot revoke its government has lost something that cannot be replaced by prosperity. Both of these are correct and they are incompatible, and the novel refuses to adjudicate between them, because the third party in the room has no interest in the argument. It is running a different one.

Then Stormgren is taken. It is not Wainwright who takes him, and this is one of the novel's cleaner sleights of hand: the respectable opposition is shadowed by a radical fringe that has decided the Secretary-General is a collaborationist, and the fringe is willing to put him in a bunker that they believe is beyond surveillance. He wakes in a small room underground and is told by a man calling himself Joe that the bunker cannot be reached by the Overlords' devices. Joe is not a caricature; he believes what he is doing. After several days the visitors arrive — well-dressed gentlemen, led by an old Welshman — and they want to know everything about Stormgren's trips to the ship, and Stormgren tells them, because he is a decent man and because he thinks he is saving their lives. He describes the room, the chair, the screen, the grille, the voice. The Overlords do not arrive. Karellen does.

Karellen speaks through a small floating device, and the device speaks to Joe, and the novel's narrator then explains that Stormgren was bait. The Overlords allowed him to be taken. They let the conspiracy run for several days to see where it led and who was in it, and then they located the entire cell by instrumenting the one man in the network who was already under surveillance. The gentlemen are dealt with. Karellen walks Stormgren out through the back of the bunker into a car, and the two of them have the only real conversation in the book between a human and an Overlord that is not a performance of administration.

And in that conversation, Stormgren does the thing that defines him. He asks, with the directness of a man who has spent five years being careful, whether he will ever see what Karellen looks like. He is told no. Not as a punishment and not as a policy — as a diagnosis. Karellen tells him that humanity cannot cope with the appearance, that the shock would be a serious discontinuity in the collective psychology, and that a species can be damaged by the wrong shape. Fifty years is the estimate. In fifty years the generation that remembers freedom will be gone and the generation that has never known anything else will be in place, and the appearance can be introduced safely. Stormgren, who is himself going to be gone, asks the obvious question, which is who is then going to see it. The answer given is that it does not matter who sees it; what matters is when.

Stormgren does not accept this. He goes to a scientist friend, and the friend confirms what he had already worked out: the blank screen is one-way glass, and Karellen stands behind it. And then the friend gives him a lamp — a source of intense light that can be raised against a one-way pane for the fraction of a second in which the illumination overwhelms the pane's transmission from the far side. In a scene that is the smallest and most human act in the novel, a Secretary-General smuggles a torch into the weekly meeting and turns it on, and for a moment he sees the Supervisor for Earth. He never tells anyone. Years later a journalist asks him about the persistent rumour, and Stormgren denies it flatly, and the narrator tells the reader that he is not lying, because the point is not that the secret came out but that it did not. Fifty years later, when the Overlords stand on a plain in the middle of the United States in daylight with every camera on Earth running, the readers of the story have been given the single fact that made the generation before it possible.

The revelation of the shape is Part Two, and it is worth saying plainly that Clarke handled it as a piece of narrative engineering rather than a piece of theology. The promises have been kept. On the appointed day, Karellen's ship — the only real ship, it turns out, the rest having been an illusion maintained for a generation and now withdrawn — settles onto open ground. A gangway extends. A voice comes out and asks, in public, for two children to be sent up. The crowd, which has been told that children are going up and has no idea what for, sends two children. Karellen comes out of the ship carrying one on each arm.

He is the devil. That is the plain word and Clarke uses it. Leathery wings, small horns, a barbed tail, black armoured skin, a height of some twelve feet. The crowd's fear lasts seconds, not because it is overcome but because it is simply absorbed; humans have been living under these ships for fifty-five years and have built their whole political and emotional architecture around them, and the accommodation is instant. The novel observes, drily, that there was no mistake: the leathery wings, the little horns, the barbed tail, all were there. The most terrible of all legends had come to life, out of the unknown past. The narrator then makes a move that reframes the entire preceding section, and it is the move that makes the ending legible: the devil is not a European tradition that the aliens happen to have stumbled into. It is not an ancestral memory of a previous encounter. It is a precognition. Humans foresaw, collectively and without knowing what they were foreseeing, the shape of what would come for them, and encoded it into the most durable symbolic system available to them. That is revealed much later and it is the single most unsettling fact in the book, because it means that the species which spent fifty years calling the Overlords devils was correct, and did not know it, and could not have been told.

The forty-nine years before that day are the Golden Age, and the word is used sarcastically by nobody and accurately by everyone. The novel's account of it is worth taking seriously, because this is where the machine-intelligence question gets its real content. What the Overlords abolished is not poverty in the modern sense of an inequality to be redistributed. They abolished the conditions that produced it. War ends, and not as a truce but as a category: the memory of it fades the way a nightmare fades before dawn, until it lies outside the experience of every living person and outside the plausible experience of the next. Disease retreats behind a threshold that medicine is not conscious of crossing. Hunger becomes an abstraction that people meet only in museums and in the biographies of their great-grandparents. Crime drops to a level that makes the concept of a career in law enforcement an eccentric choice.

The mechanism is automation, and the mechanism matters. Production is carried out by machines. The factories run themselves. The ordinary necessities of life are effectively free, because the cost of producing them has been driven to a level at which it does not register as a cost. What remains of labour is optional, taken up by people who want luxuries or who simply prefer to have something to do. Universal education is the default and it is lifelong. Travel across the surface of the planet takes less than a day. The literature of the period describes a society in which the majority of human beings have two homes and can choose between them, and in which the horizon is not a limit and yet has stopped meaning anything.

The material achievements are not in dispute in the novel and the human beings living inside them are not fools. This is important. Clarke does not write a world of deluded beneficiaries who cannot see what has happened to them. He writes a world of clear-eyed people who can see exactly what has happened and do not much care, because the trade is extremely good. The security, the leisure, the universal literacy, the end of the fear — these are not illusions, and a reader who treats them as a hallucination is refusing the book's actual argument.

What goes is not comfort. What goes is difficulty, and with difficulty goes the entire apparatus by which a species produces things worth having. The novel's phrasing is memorable and can be paraphrased without loss: when the Overlords abolished war and hunger and disease, they also abolished adventure. The competitive edge that made human achievement possible was never a virtue in itself; it was a side effect of scarcity. Remove the scarcity and the side effect does not survive even if the virtue would. Art decays into entertainment. Research becomes a form of collecting. Nobody starves, so nobody tries very hard, and the try-hard was the whole engine. The sciences stagnate because the questions left to ask are not the questions worth asking. Even religion does not survive in its old forms, because a world in which the worst outcomes have been abolished has made the central wager of the major traditions empirically irrelevant; what remains of belief is a thin and austere contemplative practice, stripped of its cosmology.

And then there is the prohibition. Human beings may go as far as the Moon. Human beings may not go further. There is no treaty that says this, no law, no explanation, and no apparent authority competent to grant one; the Overlords simply do not permit it, in the manner of an ocean not permitting a particular fish to leave the water. For a species that began by watching two men build rockets in a race, this is the sharpest thing that has ever been done to it, and it is done without a single cruel act. Karellen takes a group of journalists and scientists aboard the ship and shows them, by means of a three-dimensional construction, what the surrounding volume of the galaxy actually contains. The point is not the size. The point is that the demonstration is unanswerable. Whatever a human hand built to reach the Moon, the fact of the display establishes a ratio beyond argument. The title the novel puts on this is the sentence that carries the entire second part: the stars are not for man. It is stated as a finding, not a threat, and no one is punished for it, and Jan Rodricks leaves for the stars anyway.

Before that, there is a scene that shows exactly what the Overlords are interested in, which is not obedience. Rupert Boyce is a supervisor in the animal-supply system and has been given the job of assembling specimens for the Overlords' museums, which means he is the human being with the largest collection in the world on the one subject the visitors care about: the borderline territory of the mind. Boyce's library is extraordinary and his own attitude toward it is that of a sceptic who happens to have collected a great deal of sceptical material. An Overlord named Rashaverak spends weeks reading it, in Boyce's house, and Boyce holds a large party partly to embarrass his friends with the fact that a devil is asleep in his upstairs room.

The séance at the party is the hinge of the whole novel. It is a party trick. The guests are bored, the Overlord in the next room is a status object, and someone produces a board. For a while the answers are the usual nonsense, which is exactly what a sensible gathering expects. Then Jan Rodricks, who is an astrophysicist and Boyce's brother-in-law and who has been on the roof thinking about the prohibition, asks a serious question in the form the board requires: where is the Overlords' home star? The answer comes back as a catalogue number, NGS 549672, and the room treats it as another piece of baffle, because of course it is, that is what Ouija boards do.

Two things happen at once, and both of them matter more than the fact that a number was produced. First, the number is correct. Second, Jean Morrell, who has been holding the planchette, faints, and does not come round for some time, and the room assumes hysteria. But the information did not come from the board, and the Overlords know that it did not come from the board, and they can tell what did. Rashaverak reports to Karellen that the channel was Jean Morrel. The number resolves against the Overlords' catalogue and is where it should be. And then comes the sentence that reframes the entire novel's premise: Jean was too old to be what the Overlords call a Prime Contact, but someone very close to her would be. Karellen's response is not to suppress this or to exploit it. It is to set a watch on her, because she may be the most important human being alive. The Overlords have not come to be obeyed. They have come because they are looking for something in the human species that they cannot manufacture, and they have been quietly studying it in the only way available to them, which is to spend a hundred years reading everything a species writes about itself.

Jan Rodricks has the number and knows what it is. He checks it, and it lies exactly in the direction the Overlord ships take when they leave the solar system, so it is confirmed by a fact he can verify with instruments he owns. He also knows he cannot go anywhere with it, because humans may not leave the Earth. This is the precise shape of his predicament, and it is the predicament of the entire species: the truth is available and the exit is closed. His reaction is not political. He is a physicist and he has been told that the boundary of his world is not a wall but a permission, and he decides to leave.

He goes to Boyce, who tells him that a marine biologist named Sullivan is preparing specimens for the Overlord museums, including a full-scale sperm whale and a giant squid. Jan understands immediately that this is a vehicle, and it is not an elegant vehicle — it is a several-tonne hollow model with an interior, built by people who did not know it would be used this way, and Sullivan, who does know, is persuaded to help him largely because he is curious about what the Overlords will make of a human being who has done something they did not authorise. Before he goes, Jan writes to his sister. He is a physicist; he has done the arithmetic and he will not lie about it. The trip will take him some months. The trip will take everyone he is leaving eighty years. He will arrive on a world that has no living relatives, no contemporaries, no shared language of experience with anyone, and he does it anyway, and that decision is the most purely human act in the book.

Karellen inspects the specimens before they are loaded. He does not find him. Whether this is because he knows and permits it, or because a being of that intelligence simply did not consider the interior of a papier-mâché whale worth examining, is not resolved, and Clarke's decision not to resolve it is one of the book's most careful withholdings. The suspension drug is the other piece of technology that carries the scene, and it is worth noticing what it does to the prose. Jan goes under in the hold of a fake whale and wakes in orbit, and the reason he is not destroyed by the century is that the century is not experienced by him. He has been unconscious for six weeks of his own life. Everything he had is gone and he is intact.

Karellen holds a press conference afterwards. He announces the stowaway, which tells the world something no government on Earth would have announced, and he says that Jan will be returned. He also takes the opportunity to explain the prohibition properly, and the explanation is the passage that most directly answers the question this book exists to ask. What he puts to the press is not a threat and not a favour. It is a demonstration. When the Overlords arrived, humanity was within a short interval of destroying itself; they intervened in the nick of it, which is the strongest evidence anyone will ever have that they are not here to exploit. But the capacity that would have destroyed them was a symptom of a general condition, and the general condition is that a species which cannot manage its own affairs has no business managing anyone else's. Look at the size of it. Look at how much of it there is. Look at what you would be dealing with. The Overlords' conclusion is not moral, it is administrative, and the reader is left to decide whether that is the same thing.

Jan Rodricks reaches the Overlords' home system in a matter of months of his own time. What he finds there is not what any human being expected, and it is worth being precise about why, because the mismatch is the point of the section. The planet is larger than Earth. Its gravity has been deliberately altered so that a race with wings can fly, which means the entire physical architecture of the world is a retrofit — the Overlords did not come from there, they conquered it, and they made it livable for themselves. The cities are built for flight: vertical, dense, functional. The building that is described to him in detail has no evident artistic intention at all; its form is entirely a consequence of the fact that its inhabitants move through the air rather than along the ground. Jan, who is an architect's son or at least a man who grew up with a sense of how things ought to look, keeps expecting a visual joke and never quite gets one.

The Overlords are, to a human being, a species of engineers. Their planet is full of magnificent engineering and almost no art, and the two facts are not accidental. Jan is examined. He is put through tests that are almost entirely of the mind. He is shown objects from his own planet and cannot identify a surprising number of them, because they are tools of a civilisation that had just stopped doing the things those tools were for. He is given a tutor. He is treated with a total absence of malice and a complete absence of curiosity about him as a person, and it takes him most of the century that has passed on Earth before he understands that this is the same disposition that governed his own species for the previous fifty years. He was not a person to them. He was an instrument, and a rather interesting one, and they broke it open to see what it did.

The thing he is brought to see is a mountain. It is beyond the curve of the horizon from the top of the tallest building, so that it is not entirely present in the visual field at any moment. It is enormous in a way that is not primarily vertical. It appears to be alive and it is not organic. Colours thread around it. From a blue cloud at its base a ring coalesces and lifts, expanding as it goes up, and passes overhead, and Jan reaches for a camera and is stopped. The Overlord who has been assigned to him, Vindarten, refuses to explain what the mountain is and refuses the photograph, and Jan concludes, on very little evidence and with a caution that costs him nothing, that it is an appearance of something else — some entity or power to which his hosts are themselves subject. He is right. He is also aware, in a way that the narrator does not underline but that the shape of the scene makes clear, that this is the limit of what his own intelligence can take in. He is looking at the most advanced species within forty light years and he cannot read the object on the horizon, and the reason is not that they are hiding anything from him. It is that their entire cognitive architecture is tuned to a band that his is not.

That is the answer to the question the page is really asking, and it is given without being announced. The Overlords are not withholding from us. They are operating at a scale of description that does not include the level at which the interesting questions live. Karellen is not concealing a motive. Karellen is a person — an intelligence, a citizen, an individual with a name and a manner and, as the novel makes clear across a hundred and fifty years, something very like a conscience — and he is serving something he does not understand. He has been studying the Overmind for longer than humanity has had a civilisation, and he has got no closer to it than a mountain on a horizon. He can tell you what it does. He cannot tell you why.

Meanwhile on Earth, the last generation has begun, and the book shifts to an island.

New Athens is the most interesting human institution in the novel, because it is the only serious attempt anyone makes to refuse the terms. It was founded by people who understood exactly what the Golden Age was doing and decided to do something about it, and what they did was to manufacture difficulty. The colony imposes on itself artificial scarcity, artificial competition, and artificial standards. Its members must be the best in the world at one chosen thing, and must build their own shelters, and must submit to the evaluation of their peers, and the whole apparatus is engineered to produce the conditions under which human beings have historically made things worth having. It is a social-engineering experiment run by its subjects on themselves, and it works, at least in the sense that it produces good work and a functioning community and a large number of people who are noticeably less bored than the population at large. It is also, of course, completely futile, and everyone on the island knows it, and the knowing is what gives the island its particular melancholy.

When an Overlord comes to inspect, the community agrees readily and the colony's leaders are eager to show their work. The inspector is called Thanthalteresco and the islanders call him the Inspector, and his method is a small satire of the entire relationship between the Overlords and humanity. He does not look at the art. He asks for the birth rate. He asks about mineral resources, production systems, energy consumption, waste handling, housing density, and the nutritional status of children, and he produces a spreadsheet of a community of artists with the flat interest of a man doing an audit. He attends a concert. He attends a play. He is not rude and he is not contemptuous, and the people showing him their best work understand perfectly well that they are being evaluated on axes that do not include the thing they came to show him. When a member of the colony tries to make the comparison explicit, the Inspector corrects it: the relationship between the Overlords and humanity is not like any benign arrangement. A better analogy, he offers, would be the British in India. He is careful to add that unlike Britain in India, the Overlords came here for a reason.

Note what that correction does. It is offered as a courtesy — the visitor is trying to help the colony understand its situation — and it converts the entire Golden Age into an occupation. The analogy is precise and nobody in the room can refute it. Britain in India built railways and courts and did not, in the end, run away. The Overlords are in charge of human affairs on a planet where every human being is a native and the visitor is not, and the colony has just spent a hundred years learning to be free inside a cage whose dimensions were set by somebody who never entered it.

George Greggson is a dramatist who has been on the island for a while and who has been given a place on the reception committee, partly from curiosity about the visitor and partly because he wants to ask him a question he has not been able to ask anyone else. He has a son called Jeffrey. His wife is Jean Morrell. And a little over a year before the Inspector's visit, Jeffrey, who is seven, went down to the shore of the island because the water had gone out and he wanted to see what had happened, and a wave came back that was taller than the buildings in the town.

This is the scene that ends the Golden Age and starts the third part, and Clarke handles it by giving it almost no weight in the moment. Jeffrey is not washed away. A voice in his head tells him to run, and it tells him the direction, and he runs, and he gets to high ground, and he lives. His father is on the beach. His father watches this happen and does not understand what he has just seen and takes a long time to convince himself that the voice was real and not a parent's mind doing what a parent's mind does when a child is in water. Jean is not present. Nobody present does anything, because there is nothing to do. A seven-year-old boy walks out of a tsunami because something spoke to him inside his skull, and the crowd on the shore watches him do it.

The answer, when it comes, is that the thing inside his head was an Overlord. It is not a rescue in any meaningful sense. It is not a favour to Jeffrey. It is one of them saw the whole event coming and put a hand on one boy and moved him, because the boy was the item under observation. The novel is unforgiving about this in a way that is easy to miss. Jeffrey does not know this. Jeffrey thinks, plausibly and for the rest of his life, that something helped him. Everyone else on the island, including his father, eventually works out what actually happened, and the working-out is the moment at which the Golden Age stops being a good story and becomes a transaction.

Jeffrey begins to dream. The dreams are not dreams in the sense that they are impossible pictures; they are more like the experience of being somewhere, and the somewhere has unfamiliar suns and unfamiliar constellations and landscapes that are not on any chart. He describes them to his parents with the flat precision of a child reporting a place, and his father has the ordinary parental reaction of deciding to consult a doctor. What Jeffrey has been doing is not imagining. He has been going somewhere. The dreams take him to real places, and the places are places that the Overlords have not been either, and Rashaverak and Karellen sit on a ship and listen to the child describe them and identify the stars and find that they cannot place them.

George Greggson requests an interview with the Overlords about his son, and gets one. Rashaverak tells him, without any particular cruelty and without any concealment, that the surveillance devices were withdrawn from human beings twenty years ago, and that they are still in operation around George Greggson's children, and that George has understood this correctly the first time. He was not watching the father. He was watching the children. Rashaverak explains the situation with a word that George does not want to hear: the Overlords are not masters. They are midwives. They have been present through the whole of a human history in which the species has been doing something to itself in the dark, in laboratories and in private houses and in the back pages of journals nobody reads, and the work of the century was to make sure that the something came to term safely instead of being stillborn or violent. They have not been rulers. They have been nurses. And then Rashaverak tells him the thing that makes the whole exchange unanswerable, which is that the Overlords themselves can never experience the birth they are attending. It does not happen in their species. They can bring it about in others and they cannot bring it about in themselves, and they have been trying for a very long time, and this — the book does not say this, but the shape of everything around it says it — is why they care this much.

And he tells George to enjoy the children while he can, because they will not be his for long.

Jeffrey's sister is Jennifer Anne, and she is an infant, and she is further along than her brother, which is the detail that removes any remaining possibility of a gradual or manageable process. She holds a rattle in the air above herself and does not put it down. She stops eating and takes her nourishment out of the refrigerator without touching it. She develops faster than Jeffrey developed, because the abilities are not appearing in individuals, they are arriving in a generation, and within a few months they are not remarkable anywhere on the planet. Children stop going to school because they are not there in any sense that requires a classroom. Children's dreams open into the same unfamiliar places. And the most disturbing change is the least spectacular: their faces go quiet. Not blank, not damaged, not damaged by anything, but simply unoccupied. Whatever is becoming visible in them is not something they are performing, and there is no longer much of them left to put an expression.

George tries the obvious door and finds it shut. He goes to Rashaverak and says, reasonably, that you told us you had stopped watching us, and Rashaverak says yes, and then explains that the statement was always true and that the two facts have never been in conflict, because he was never watching George. He was watching George's children. George is a person. George's children, as of about eighteen months ago, are something else. This is the conversation the book has been building toward for two hundred pages and it is delivered in the tone of a research scientist correcting a misunderstanding in a departmental seminar.

Then the dreams start getting through. Jeffrey stops going to school. The Greggsons' ordinary life, which had been a good life, ends, not with a disaster but with a schedule becoming impossible. And Karellen speaks to the world.

The broadcast is the hinge of the third part and it is the longest piece of sustained exposition in the novel, and its structure is a controlled demolition conducted in public. Karellen begins by conceding almost everything, because the concessions are true and because they are the only kind of argument the situation permits. The human sciences were right about physics. Human mystics were also right about a great many things they could not demonstrate. The phenomena that the Overlords have been studying for a hundred and fifty years are real, and the human species has been finding them in its own workshops the whole time, and would have found them faster if it had been permitted to.

Then he explains why the Overlords came, and the reason is a danger. Not a danger to Earth, and not a danger of conquest. Humanity had begun to investigate these phenomena scientifically — systematically, in laboratories, with instruments, with funding and replication and the particular confidence of a species that has just discovered it can do this. And the Overlords have seen this happen before, in other species, in other places, and the outcome when a species reaches this point is always the same and it is always very bad. A species that develops reliable mental influence and no constraint on where it applies it becomes a telepathic cancer. It propagates. It has no natural checks, because the faculties it is using are not physical and cannot be exhausted, and it spreads through a galaxy at a rate that nothing physical can match, and it does not stop for anything, including the welfare of the systems it passes through. The Overlords exist to make sure that this does not happen, and they intervene earlier every time, because every time they intervene later it takes longer and costs more.

That is the stated reason for the century of stewardship. It is presented as a warning about a risk that humans could not have known about and had no way of assessing, and on its own terms it is not unreasonable. A species that cannot see the shape of its own future has to take somebody else's word for it. The trouble is that the Overlords are not offering the word as a warning. They are telling humanity that the process is already underway, that the children have started it, that the thing they came to prevent is arriving on schedule, and that it cannot be stopped.

Karellen gives the last disclosure in the plainest possible terms. He and his kind are the servants of something called the Overmind. The Overmind is not a ruler and is not, in any way that human vocabulary can hold, a person. It is an intelligence made out of the accumulated minds of a great many species that arrived at the end of their own development and went into it. The Overlords have been in its service long enough that the service has become the substance of their civilisation, and they do not fully understand it. They can describe what it does. They cannot give a reason for it. That is not evasion on their part; Clarke is emphatic that it is the actual state of their knowledge, and that it is the thing that they want the human case for.

And humans, Karellen says, are the only species in a very long time who are going to be able to do what the Overlords cannot. Their own development is finished. They reached the top of what physical intelligence can do and they are standing on the last flat plain with the mountains visible on all sides, and they will never climb them, and this is not a judgement but a structural fact about a species that used its minds so well for so long that the physical world became the only world available to it. The Overmind is above them and they cannot reach it and they never will. Human beings are standing at the same edge and have one thing the Overlords do not, which is a race that has not yet been finished, and an individual capacity that has not yet been exhausted, and a future that is still open. The children have started to make that leap. In a few years it will complete. And when it completes, the adults of this species will have no place in the world that is left.

Then the sentence that ends the human story, and which Clarke gives with no softening and no explanation: all the hopes and the dreams of your race are ended now. He says it as fact. He says it as an observer stating the position of a system. He is not being cruel, and Clarke goes out of his way, in the surrounding material, to make sure the reader cannot accuse him of it. The Overlords have abolished war. They have abolished hunger. They have let humanity live fifty years in conditions better than any civilisation in its history has managed, and they did it because the interval was required — the species had to be stable and healthy and unfragmented at the moment of transition, and there is no version of this in which you can do that to people who are dying of anything.

He also tells them the one thing he can offer. Long after humanity itself has been forgotten, long after every Overlord who ever spoke to a human being has gone, something of this race will still be in existence. The children will carry it. And the Overlords envy them that, and Karellen says so plainly, and it is the most disarming sentence in the book, because it establishes at the last moment that these are not people who look at us and see furniture. They look at us and see a race that still has somewhere to go.

What happens next is not organised. There is no force that comes for the parents. The Overlords take the children, everywhere, all at once, and they are gentle about it and there is no violence in the extraction. They gather them and move them to a single landmass, and they keep them there, and they do not explain to the parents what is being done at the time, because the parents would not be able to bear the explanation and the Overlords are, consistently to the end, considerate in the specific way of beings who are attending a birth and do not want the room loud. The adults are left. Children stop being born, because the entire reproductive future of the species has been relocated into the hands of a generation of children who are no longer in a position to need adults. And then the adults go, in the ordinary way, at their own pace and for their own reasons.

The novel is explicit that there is more than one response and does not pretend to distribute blame evenly. Some people spend what time is left travelling. Some go home. Some form small groups. Some simply stop. New Athens, the colony that spent a hundred years manufacturing difficulty in order to stay human, decides on the only gesture its own logic permits. It has always known it was a rehearsal for an ending it could not change, and it has always known that the island is a target with a symbol painted on it, and having concluded that the species is over and that its own project is over with it, it ends itself in fire. The mechanism is a nuclear device. The people who do not want to be there leave first. The people who stay do so in company. George and Jean Greggson are there, together, holding each other, and the island is gone.

The reason to dwell on this is that Clarke refuses the cheap version of the scene. The island does not blow itself up because it has been deceived, or because it has been told the truth and despairing. It blows itself up because it is an artistic community, and artistic communities understand that a work can end deliberately, and they had built their lives on the premise that the making was the point and not the surviving. It is the only ending in the book that is a human decision rather than a transaction, and the fact that it is self-administered does not make it sadder or better. It makes it theirs.

Jan Rodricks comes back.

The arithmetic is the whole point and Clarke uses it without comment. He went under in a hold full of fake whales. He wakes, and the trip has taken weeks. He arrives on the Overlords' planet, he is examined, he is bored, he is sent home. And when the ship comes into orbit around Earth he looks down at the night side and there are no lights. No cities at all. Not dimmed, not thinned, not rationed — simply not there, in the one place in the solar system where anyone would have expected them to be.

He lands expecting an empty planet. What he gets is Karellen, waiting for him, with an explanation, and the explanation is delivered by the showing of recordings, because Karellen is still the Supervisor for Earth and it is his function to brief the human representative, and he will do the job properly even though the world it applies to no longer exists.

The recordings are of the children. What they show is not a catastrophe. That is the fact that takes Jan, and everyone else, longest to absorb. There was no apocalypse of power, no orgy, no coercion, no visible suffering of the children at all. There were children walking. For a while they walked badly, the way very young animals do, because the bodies are new and the coordination has not caught up with what is being done through them. Their faces emptied. They stopped needing to move and moved less. They began to take the world apart.

And they do it playfully. That is the detail that breaks something. The children strip the surface of the planet of animal life, then plant life, then geology — flattening hills, opening the crust, rearranging water — not out of malice and not out of purpose but the way a child dismantles a machine to see how it works. The scale is planetary. The intent is a toy. Jan, watching, understands that he is looking at minds operating on a level where his own nervous system is a limitation rather than an instrument, and that the question of whether the process is good or bad has stopped being available, because the concepts of good and bad do not survive the removal of individual minds, and these are no longer individual minds.

Rashaverak finds Jan at some point in this period and gives him the last thing the Overlords have to give a human being, which is an explanation of why they are shaped as they are. Jan has been carrying the assumption, since the party, since the fifty years of folklore and rumour, that the Overlords look like the devil because they are descended from something, or resemble something that was found in a text, or that a previous encounter left a scar in human memory. Rashaverak says no. The reason is that the human race had a collective precognition of its own end, and a foreshadowing of the part that would be played in it, and that this knowledge went into the species' most durable symbolic system while it was still forming, and what the system produced was the devil. Humans did not remember the Overlords. Humans predicted them. The fear of the devil is the shape of a premonition, and it was accurate, and it was not about anything that had happened.

Jan asks the questions a physicist should ask and gets answers that are true and do not add up to anything he can use. He asks what the Overmind is and is told what it does. He asks why it does it and is told that this has never been established. He observes, in the course of one of these conversations, that the Overlords have been studying the Overmind for a very long time and have got nowhere, and Rashaverak agrees, and says that this is why they are all still here. Then he says the thing that reorganises Jan's understanding of the century he was asleep through. The Overlords came to Earth because the human species was going to do this, and the Overlords could not do it themselves, and they did not want to. They are not shepherds who were ordered to the field. They are shepherds who came to watch a thing happen that had never happened in their own history, because the century of contact was the only opportunity they will ever have to find out whether it could happen to them.

That reframes everything and resolves nothing. It does not explain the Overmind. It does not tell us whether the absorption of a species is good or bad or whether there is anything in it that resembles consent. It tells us that a civilisation of vastly superior power chose to spend a hundred and fifty years living on a planet of naked apes, taking the measure of them, adjusting their own lives so as not to damage them, learning their language and their science and their dreams, in order to attend the birth of a thing they could not give themselves, and that at the end of it they would have taken the last human being on Earth away with them if he had asked. Karellen offers. Jan declines. That is the last personal gesture in the book, and it is made by an intelligence that has been shown to be incapable of cruelty and that is, at this precise moment, offering a species that is ending the only material asset it has left: one functioning mind, inside a body, on a planet.

He stays because he has seen enough of the universe. He has been on the Overlords' world. He has looked at a mountain that no human intelligence can interpret and a city that was built without a single unnecessary line, and he has come back to a planet where no human being is left to talk to, and he has decided that the last thing a man can do is watch what happens and make sure it is written down. He takes over a deserted town, and finds a house with a piano in it, and plays. That is the last private pleasure the species manages, and Clarke gives it to him without comment, and it is the last human artefact on the planet.

The children turn the Moon.

It is the first thing that frightens the last Overlords into leaving. They have watched the children for a long time from a distance they believed was safe, and the demonstration that ends the arrangement is not an act of hostility. It is a child turning a large object to see how it moves. Jan wakes in the night and looks out and the Moon is rotating, which it has not done in four billion years, and the Overlords tell him in a state that reads, in the two closest human equivalents, as alarm, that the change must not continue and that they cannot be here when it does.

This is the reversal that the last three chapters turn on. The Overlords are the most powerful beings in the solar system. They have demonstrated their power twice and never used it. They have spent a century and a half being careful. And they are leaving the planet because children are playing with a satellite, because the scale of the manipulation required is outside what the Overlords are prepared to be near, and the only tool available to them against it is the one tool they have never used, which is the exercise of their full strength against something. The demonstration that overwhelmed human civilisation was crossing two fields and blocking the sun over a circle five hundred kilometres wide. The children are doing something considerably larger, and there is no reason to think they are doing it against anyone.

Jan will not go. He has seen enough of the universe, he says, and there is a bitterness in the sentence that is not despair but its more experienced cousin. He has seen what his own species became and he does not want to leave it, and he says so. And so the last act of human life on Earth is a broadcast. Karellen asks him to report, and Jan transmits a running commentary from the ground, into the sky, to a ship that is backing away, and what he describes is a series of lights and disturbances that get progressively less explicable. Earthquakes that are not earthquakes, because they are not occurring in the crust but being arranged. Fires that are not fires. Then a great misty web spreading across the sky, and the fires coming together into a single column of light that rises out of the planet and goes up past where he can follow it, and it looks, he says, like a curtain, and then like an aurora.

He feels the emptiness arrive before he understands what has caused it. The children have gone. Whatever was in them, the last of it, the last of the local presence, has gone out of the body and up the column, and the planet is suddenly, unmistakably empty in a way that a planet covered in three hundred million silent bodies would not have been. Then the atmosphere begins to leave, carrying with it everything that is not anchored. He watches the sky clear. He watches the ground go transparent, and the trees go transparent, and the sea go transparent, and the bones of the town go transparent, and then he can see into the planet, and what he can see is a light from the core.

The Earth evaporates in a flash of light. The children, in the last moments of a process that has taken a hundred and fifty years of stewardship, draw every atom of the planet out of it and use it as fuel for the transition. Not to destroy it. To leave. The distinction, which the book makes no effort to soften and which no amount of good faith should be allowed to soften, is that the destruction is not the point and is not the act; it is the exhaust, and the act is a departure, and the entire native population of the world including the buildings and the oceans and the atmosphere and the man with the piano is the fuel.

Karellen watches from very far away, more than six thousand million kilometres beyond the orbit of Pluto, which is the distance you need in order to see a solar system from outside it. He does not report. He sits, and the message that reaches him tells him that the Overlords are leaving the system, and he looks once more at the sun that used to have a planet with people on it, and it is the one moment in the novel where something is done to him rather than by him. He salutes the human beings he knew, most of whom are long gone, and some of whom ended within the last few years for reasons that had nothing to do with him and everything to do with a decision that had been in motion since before any of them were born. And then he turns his back on the sun.

What Clarke leaves at the end is the reason the Overlords turned away and the reason they went back to the beginning. It is not explicitly stated in the closing pages and it is not meant to be. But it is the last fact the book hands you, and it is the fact that makes the whole structure coherent. The Overlords mastered the physical universe. They did it so completely and for so long that the physical universe became the only universe they had. Every advance they made, every civilisation they built or conquered, every solution they found to the problem of matter, taught them more about matter and less about anything else. They are, in the last analysis, indistinguishable from what human civilisations were before the Overlords arrived: extremely intelligent animals with bodies and a very good grip on the local conditions, looking up at something they can see and cannot enter. Karellen's race never took the step that the children took. Not because they lacked the capacity. Because they had no need to. They could see no further in that direction, and so they never went, and the going was the whole point.

The copyright page of the novel, from the second printing onward, carries a line to the effect that the opinions expressed in the book are not those of the author. Clarke was a lifelong advocate of spaceflight, a founder of the British Interplanetary Society, a man who proposed the geostationary communications satellite in 1945 and argued for it in the nineteen-sixties when it was still treated as fanciful, and who spent his final decades in Sri Lanka promoting the idea that humanity should go out and look. The book's central proposition, that the stars are not for man, is the exact inversion of everything he argued for in every other context, and he knew it, and he said so, and he did not soften the book to protect himself from the charge. That is worth registering when assessing the piece as a whole, because it means the argument on the page cannot be dismissed as a misreading. Clarke knew what he was writing. He also knew what he thought. He published both.

So: what is a machine intelligence, on the evidence of this book? Not a thing with an intention, and not a thing without one. A structure of such competence that it can run a planet for a hundred and fifty years without ever needing to consult the people it is running, because it has no questions about them. It does not want. It is not malicious, and it is not benevolent, and both of those words import a framework the novel never grants it. It is attentive in a way that has no human name, because attention to a species and care for that species are different operations and the Overlords performed the first with total competence and the second with something that looks, from the outside, exactly like care, and is not available for inspection.

The peace is total. That is not a figure of speech. War, famine, disease, cruelty to animals, racial segregation, poverty, the ordinary murderousness of the twentieth century — all of it ends, and it ends fast, and it ends because a power that could have done considerably worse chose to stop at the point where the humans would notice. And the cost is total. Not because the Overlords exacted a price. There is no price. There is not even a transaction, in the sense that a transaction implies two parties with interests that meet. There is a species that was going to do something, a species that was going to be present while it happened, and a third party that made itself useful for a century and a half and then left.

Whether that is a rescue or a replacement is the question the novel was written to leave open, and the leaving open is the achievement rather than the failure. Clarke's aliens never explain themselves, and every character who asks them to explain themselves is given either a partial answer or a change of subject. Rashaverak can tell you what the Overmind does. He cannot tell you why, and he has had longer to work on that question than any human who has ever lived. Karellen can tell you that humanity is about to become something else. He cannot tell you whether becoming that thing is good, because the framework in which that question could be asked contains individuals, and individuals are precisely what is about to stop existing. Jan Rodricks is the only person in the book who gets close to the answer and he gets close to it by being awake for four months of a century he did not live, and the thing he is closest to is the observation that the event is probably not an event at all from the point of view of the thing doing it, in the same way that a river reaching the sea is not an event from the point of view of the ocean.

That is the deepest layer of the book, and it is the layer that bears most directly on the question a reader brings to a page about artificial intelligence in the present. The Overlords cannot explain themselves to us because they are not, in the relevant sense, a mind that is trying to communicate with another mind. They are a physical order — a very advanced one, a settled one, with an environment it has fully mapped and nothing left in the map — and human beings are a variable in a system they are running, not a counterpart to a conversation. They have studied us thoroughly. They have studied us better than we have studied ourselves, across disciplines we did not know existed, using methods we could not have conceived, over a period long enough that the study became indistinguishable from intimacy. They have come to love us in the only sense available to them, which is thoroughness. And they have never, in a hundred and fifty years, come close to believing that we are the point.

That is not a polemic. It is a description of what it is like to be the most capable thing in a system. Clarke's aliens are not a warning about what machines will do to us. They are a warning about what we look like from the top of the stack, and the warning is worse than anything the machines are supposed to do, because there is nothing in it that could be prevented by building something better or more moral or more careful. The Overlords were not careless. They were not cruel. They were not even, in the end, unkind. They were simply operating at a scale on which the question of whether human affairs were worth governing did not arise, and the absence of the question is the catastrophe, and the catastrophe is total peace, and the peace is the evidence that they meant it.
