I found the axe in the field behind the Deetzes' place, in the row that had been beets, and I want to set down in what order things happened before I set down any one of them, because after that week order was the only thing left that anybody could be certain of.

The milk went round for three days after the last of the rest. That is a fact I can put my hand on. Route 9, the Greenfield truck, the six-thirty run. On the Thursday it did not come. On the Friday a boy came past on a bicycle and said the driver had not been up, and that was the first thing anybody said out loud, and nobody said what it meant, and everyone went to work as usual and found nobody at work. By Saturday the town had gone quiet the way a town goes quiet when the phone rings once and does not ring again. Not silent. Quiet. There is a difference and I want it on the record. Silence is an absence. What we had was a reduction, a settling, the noise of a town coming down to a lower gear and staying there.

I was a hardware dealer then, and before that a plumber's apprentice, and before that nothing worth putting down. I had been in the county thirty-one years. I knew the shape of the place better than I knew the shape of my own hands, and I am not a man given to noticing. I notice now because there was nothing left to do but notice.

The axe was in the beet row and it had not been there in June. I know that because I ran a pipe wrench through that exact spot in June and the wrench came up clean. The blade was a blue that I could not make anybody else see. That is the plainest way to put it. I described it to Deetz and he said the sky was blue that day and probably I had the sun in my eyes, and he was probably right, and I let it go, because arguing the color of a thing you found alone in a field is not a thing a man in my position wins. But it was blue the way the sky is blue, which is to say not a color so much as a suggestion that light is being handled in a way the sky does not handle it. The metal was cold. In the middle of October, at four in the afternoon, after a day of sun on an open field, it was cold to the touch, and I held it a long time before I let it down.

It was a good axe. That is the whole of what I can say about it and it is more than it sounds. It was better than good. It had been made by somebody who had been thinking about axes for a very long time, the way a man who has been thinking about axes for a very long time thinks about them, and the balance in the hand was such that I understood the whole tool in one grip. There was no maker's mark. There was a short length of the same blue metal lying ten feet away where it had been thrown down or pulled out of the ground, and where the ground was disturbed around it the soil had not healed over. That was in October. I went back in April and it had healed, and I could find the place only because I had marked it with a stake, and then I found the stake too had gone over, and I have not gone back since.

The Martians had been gone six weeks by the time I found the axe. That was the received account and it is not mine; mine is the six weeks are the part nobody could account for. What they left behind was tools. They left the tools because they were a gardening people. That is what Spender said to me and I have turned it over so many times that it has worn smooth. They left behind the tools and the seeds and the blueprints, because a people who are going to be around for a long time plant things. There is a thing about their books that I will come to. The tools were to be found all over the county in that autumn, under turf, in ditches, in the bottoms of ponds, laid about like something a careful man sets out in rows so that whoever comes will know it was intentional. Blue. Green the color of the canal. Cold to the touch in the sun. And nobody picked them up. That is what I want on the record. Nobody picked them up. They were the most extraordinary objects any of us had ever handled, and we left them lying in the ditches, because picking them up would have meant admitting that we were picking things up, and we were not ready for that, and I do not blame us, because I was not ready either. I did not pick up the axe. I put it down in the field and I came back the next day with a rag and wrapped it and took it home and put it in the shed behind the lawnmower, where it has been ever since.

What I want to say about the town is this. It did not end. That is the thing nobody will believe, and I have stopped arguing it. It got quiet, the way a room gets quiet when the people in it go into the next room and leave the door open, and you can still hear them, and then the water in the pipes goes cool, and then you cannot hear them any more, and then it is not a room with people in the next room, it is a room with a door open onto nothing. Nobody can tell me where they went. That is the other half of it. Nobody left. I want that exact. We did not run. We did not drive west. There was no convoy, there was no boat, there was no last week of newspaper. People went into houses and did not come out, and other people went into houses to be with them, and after a while the doors stopped opening for a person bringing a chair.

The order was this, and I have thought about it a great deal. First the small creatures. I did not see any of it, but the dogs went in three nights, before anything else, and they went quietly, one at a time, out the back gate, and did not bark. Every house on our street lost its dog in the same three nights, and they went in different directions. Then the wells. Ours went bitter on the eighth of October and stayed bitter. Then the gardens. That is a way of putting it that will sound like poetry and it is not. In the second week of October the tomatoes on nine houses on our street went black at the stem while still on the vine, and the leaves came off in a single night, and the pear tree in the Doyles' yard dropped every fruit it had in an afternoon and did not put out another, and the skin of the sweet corn went grey and hard and the ears stood there like small grey fists. Nothing rotted. Nothing wilted. It simply stopped and stayed stopped, in the state it was in.

Then the sickness. That is what the doctor called it afterward, when there was still a doctor, which there was until the middle of November. He called it a flu. He said it came up out of the ground. He was not a man who said a thing he did not have to say, so I give him that, and I also give you the fact that in nine houses that same week there were people lying on their backs with their eyes open who could not be waked, and were gone in three days, and the doctor came and looked and did not write anything down. He came because he was still coming. He came because he had not yet worked out how not to come.

Then the two in the house on the corner, Mr. and Mrs. Peale, who had been married fifty-one years, and I want to say how they went, because it sets the manner for everything else and it is not a manner I would have believed in. They did not scream. Mrs. Peale sat on the front step with a blanket round her shoulders in the last week of October, in the cold, in a house with no heat, and she sat there for four days. You could see her from the road if you went out of your way, and people went out of their way. She was not crying and she was not calling. She was looking at the row of houses opposite with an attention that did not vary, and Mr. Peale lay inside in the front room with the light on, and the door was open the whole four days, and nobody could get him to close it. She would not go inside. It may have been that she could not. The two of them were taken in the same night, and in the morning the door was still open, and it stayed open until the following spring, when the wind took the hinge off it.

I say it like this because I want it flat. I do not want the reader to think there was anything to be seen that night. There was nothing to be seen. The Martians did not come into our street. Nobody in our street watched anything happen. That is the fact at the bottom of the whole thing and it is the reason I have written this down at all: that a town can go out like a lamp in a room where everybody is sitting, and the people sitting there can tell you afterward, honestly, that they did not notice the moment, and that this is true. Nobody can give you the moment. Ask them. There is no moment. There is a Tuesday, and there is the light going, and there is no Tuesday.

And then in the first week of November, the rockets. And then, after that, the sea.

Three expeditions went to Mars. That is not my figure; it is the one everybody uses and I have not found anybody who can produce the other number. The first left in the second week of December, in the cold, out of the hot-rock country three hundred miles west of here. The second left in February. The third left in April and did not come back, which is a way of putting it that has taken me a long time to accept even while telling it.

I want to be careful with the expeditions, because they are the part of the account that is most open to exaggeration and least to error. The men on the first were nine. They were not heroes, which is the first thing to understand and the thing most often got wrong by people who were not there. Two were test pilots. One was a widowed schoolteacher from Cortez named Harry Stickell who had saved for eleven years to go and who raised the money by teaching summer school twice, and who talked about it the whole of the summer of his going with a cheerfulness that I found at the time tedious and find now unbearable. One was a dentist. There was a night watchman, there was a boilermaker, there were two young men out of a radio shop in a town whose name you would not know. They went up in a ship that was assembled above a cornfield by people who were paid in instalments and did not always get paid.

They were buried at Greenfield on the fourth of January, all nine, on the same afternoon, out of the same churchyard, in nine successive graves that are four feet apart in a row, and the county paid for the plots, and the county had never paid for anything of that kind before or since. There were two hundred and something people at the graveside. It was fifty-one degrees. I have the burial register in a copy. The minister made an error in the second name and had to stop and start again, and Mrs. Stickell did not react to it at all, which I remember, because she reacted to everything else. And the thing that made the crowd, that made the two hundred and some people stand in the cold past the point where they would ordinarily have gone home, was not grief. Grief is common and everyone in that crowd had some and showed it. What they were waiting for was somebody from the field in Ohio where the ship had come down, and nobody had come.

We know how it ended because they found the ship. That is the first hard fact of the whole business and I will put it plainly. The ship was not found intact. In nine or ten places it was entered from outside by something that melted. Around each of those places the metal was not blown out. It was burned, from the outside in, in a cone whose apex was at the centre of the hull, and the burn went through eight inches of alloy and stopped. There were men who went into that wreck with lamps. They are alive. They will testify. And every one of them has said the same sentence to me and I have never found one who said it differently, which I put down as the single most remarkable fact I know.

They burned all nine of them in the space of one hour in the crew cabin, by tools from the tool wall, which is to say by axe and by spanner and by a fire axe with a thirty-inch handle, and then the fire went out, and the ship was empty for eight years until we had rockets and the courage to go and look. Nobody went for eight years. The county knew where it was. There were people who wanted to go. The answer was always the money and then the answer was always that the fire had not been the whole of it, and then by the time there was money there was nobody who would be first.

I have stood in it. It is a thing like a boiler that has come apart in a field. I want to describe it because it is the reason I do not argue with people who tell me the Martians were a myth, when what they are actually doing is declining to look.

What I saw, and I say it exactly: the pilot's chair. It was still there, in its mount, at the instruments. It was still fastened to the floor. There was nothing in it. Not a bone, not a boot, not a buckle, not a coin. And on the arm of the chair, on the left arm, there were three short black verticals scorched into the paint, evenly spaced, in a row, going down into the arm about three inches, and the wood had been charred to them and stopped. Whatever the heat was, it was not willing to go into the arm. It went along the surface and turned at it.

There was no panic in the ship. I have heard that said. There was no panic in the ship because panic is a thing you have time for.

Now the sea.

The Martians came out of the sea that spring and put a green snot over the Pacific, and I am aware that is a phrase and I have used it because I could not find a better one in ten years, and I would like to be allowed to say so. It was a fine green. It had a smell to it that I would not put a name to because a name is a thing you can carry home. It fished nothing. That is the whole of the damage from the sea: nothing could be taken out of it that any person wanted to eat, and the men who went out in it came back ill, a small number of them, and of those a smaller number did not get up.

But the sea was not the argument. The sea was the day everybody had been waiting for without knowing they were waiting for it, the day it became possible to say out loud that the Martians were not an event, they were a climate. The snot spread. The Pacific gave it up on the coast from British Columbia down to the Horn and it did not stop at the coast. It came inland. It came up the twenty-five miles of valley between the coast range and the sierra and lay down in it in a single week, and the valley went brown, and the orchards went with it, and the cattle had nothing to eat that had not already been eaten, and by June the whole central valley was a grey place that smelled of the beach and would not burn.

And while the valley was going, on the far side of it, the thing that had been happening in my county for eight months quietly went public, and it did so by arriving on our lawns.

I want to describe the lawns, because there is no other way to convey what the summer of that year was in this county, and because people who were not here think of that summer as a time of fire, and it was not, it was a time of lawns.

In May, on a Tuesday, I saw it first on the Doyles' front grass and I went over and put my hand on it and it was cold. Not wet-cold, not dew. It was cold the way the axe was cold. It was a fine, hard, dry green that had come up through the blades of the existing grass and was standing among them, and when I pulled a handful it came up in a sheet with the roots of the fescue still attached to it, unbroken, as if it had never had any other intention than to lift out cleanly. The fescue underneath was healthy. That is the detail. Green lay over green and the old green was fine.

By the end of June there was not a lawn in this county. There were lawns everywhere, in yards and parks and the strips along the highway and the grass on the verges where people had never mown because nobody mows verges. It had got onto the school ground and onto the Greenfield common. It did not grow in the ditches. It did not grow in shade. It came up only where grass had been, or would have been, and where people had walked it wore into paths the way grass wears, and by August you could walk the town and know exactly where people had walked in April, because the paths were still there in it, two years later they were still there.

The county went at it with mowers in June and July. I sold every mower I had and ordered more and they arrived late because the factory in Illinois was in the same condition as everything else. Men mowed. It did not help. There was a week in July when you could mow a lawn at nine in the morning and see the marks at four in the afternoon, and there was a Sunday in July when somebody cut the whole strip along the highway for a length of a mile and by the time he got home and came back to look at it, which he did, and I wish he had not, the tire tracks were still visible under the new growth in a different direction.

Then, in the first week of August, the writing.

I did not see it. I was asleep; it was two in the morning; it was on a front lawn in this county and on nine other front lawns in a line down the highway. It burned itself into the grass in lines a foot wide and it lay there glowing faintly green for four nights and then it went out, and where it went out there was bare earth in the shape of writing, and the writing was a Martian alphabet, and it covered every lawn in the country, and it was, in the end, a map. I know that last part from Spender and from the two men who went up in 2027, and not one of us was ever able to agree on what it was a map of, and I am not going to guess at it in a document like this.

And eleven nights after the writing went out, on a Thursday, at about four in the morning, the lawns went.

That is the night of the burning. I have heard people call it the burning for sixty years and I have never asked them what they think burned and I am not going to explain it here beyond what I can stand behind. The green went out of the ground in this county on that Thursday. Not scorched. Not killed. It simply was not there any more, in one night, from the fence line to the fence line, and it did not leave ash and it did not leave a smell and it left the ground bare, and the ground under it was fine black earth that would take a crop, and I have seen men stand in a bare yard at ten in the morning with their boots in it and nothing at all in their hands.

The trees went in the same week. Not all at once and not everywhere. In a strip down the middle of the county, over about four days, the leaves came off the elms and the oaks and the cottonwoods and lay down on the ground in the morning and were gone by the afternoon, and by the end of that week the branches were bare and by the following week the branches were gone, and what was left was the trunks, and the trunks stood in the fields for another month and then the county started burning them, in ones and twos, the way a man burns brush, and it was allowed and there was nothing in the sky to stop it, and we did it in daylight, and I did it too.

I want to say what that does, because it is not the tree-felling that people remember and it is the roofs.

The green went out of the ground and the trees went and then, in the last week of that August, on every house in the county, the roof went. Not the walls. The roofs. They came off at about two in the morning and lay in the yards in pieces and the rain came in for four days and the ceilings came down and the plaster came down and the floors buckled, and the houses stood. That is what a house is; it is walls and a roof and it will stand without a roof for a good while, and ours stood with the rafters going one at a time across eight or nine years, in weather, until in the end there was not one of them with a roof on it.

Nobody could put a roof back on. That is the fact of the year and everybody in this county knows it. There was nothing to roof with. The lumber was all stacked at the mill in Greenfield and the mill was gone by then and the roofing was gone and the nails were gone, and the men who could have done it were in houses and not out of them, and that is the connection, and I want to make it, because it is the connection and nobody will: the burning of the lawns is the reason there was a sore throat in this county in September, and the sore throat is the reason there were four hundred people in this county in the autumn instead of nine thousand.

Nothing in this county was ever weather again. There was one more event in the sky and then there was the wind, and I will come to both, and after that there were eight years of ordinary air, and I want anybody who tells this story to understand that the ordinary air was the last ordinary thing anybody had.

Six weeks after the lawns went out, the rockets came.

I do not know what to call what happened that night. I have read a great many accounts and I have talked to the men who were out in it, and there is no language for it that does not mislead by being specific. So I will do this. I will give the sequence and I will decline to give the meaning.

The rockets came up at about half past eight in the evening, in a column out of the northern sky, low, from north to south, going over the county, and they were spaced so evenly that you could count the interval and it did not vary. If you held a ruler's length against the sky and let them go past, they held the ruler. Every man who was out that night and did the arithmetic says the same figure and I will give mine, which is one every twenty-two seconds, give or take, over about four minutes.

They were not silent. I want to put down what sound is, because the sound is the part that the accounts get sentimental and it is not that. It was a flat rushing sound, and it was underneath the crickets, and there was no crack and no roar and no report, and when they were directly overhead the sound did not get louder, it stayed the same, which is the thing I could not get used to and still cannot.

They had lights on them and the lights were yellow and they burned steady and did not flicker. That is the detail that the people who were there always come back to. Every account of that night, from every person I have spoken to, and I have spoken to about forty, ends on the colour of the lights. Not the ships. The colour of the lights.

They went over and they went south, out of the county, in about four minutes. The north sky emptied. And then, for a period I have never been able to state exactly but which I will call forty minutes, there was nothing at all. The crickets resumed. The dogs, of the eleven dogs left in the county that had not gone out the back gate in October, came out from under the porches and lay down in the yard and would not go back in. A woman I know turned her radio on. It said what it said. It said that nothing was happening.

At the end of the forty minutes the first one came back north. That is the phrase everybody uses, and it is wrong, and I want to correct it because the correction is the whole shape of the thing. They did not come back. They went north past us, and while they were still going north, four or five of them dropped out of the formation without slowing and went down. They went down behind the ridge west of the county, and the ridge is four thousand feet, and you could see them go over the top of it and not reappear, and there was no sound and there was no light after that. The remainder continued north over the mountains.

Then, over the next quarter hour, the whole squadron, the ones that had gone, came back up over the northern sky. I have it from three men who were in different places and who had never spoken to each other. They came back up from the west, behind the ridge, very low, spaced exactly as before, and they went back north over the mountains again. And then for good. There was nothing after that until the morning, when Mrs. Hall, who does not sleep, watched the sun come up over the north range and told me she had watched it come up twice while it was still moving, which is a condition of the eye and I do not explain it.

That is the night the rockets came. I went out on my porch at about nine that night. I did not go out because I heard anything. I went out because my wife asked me to come and look at the sky, which I did, and we stood there about ten minutes and saw the last of them go over the mountains with their steady yellow lights on them, and then we came in, and that was the night.

Then the wind, and the white dust, which is the last of the sky and the first of the ground, and I will take it in two parts.

The wind came up out of the west across the whole valley in the last week of that September in one long night, and it was not a wind in the sense of a thing that moves the air. It was a wind in the sense of a thing that moves everything else. It was warm, and it was steady, and it did not vary, and it carried with it a fine white dust that was not sand and not ash and not snow. It came through the bare treetops without moving them. That is what I remember and it is what everybody remembers: the trees stood still in it, and there were not many trees left to stand still.

The dust went into every crack of every building in the county and into every lung, and the sky over the whole central valley went the colour of a television set that has been left on with no picture, which is a colour nobody has a word for, and it stayed that colour for eleven days.

In those eleven days the dust burned. I will not go into what it did. I will say only that it did what it was told to do and that what it was told to do was to make room, and that afterwards there were places where you could walk straight into a house and out the other side.

And in those eleven days, in the third week of September, people in this county started coughing.

My name is Nathaniel Freely and I am seventy-one years old, I have been a hardware dealer and a plumber's apprentice in this county for fifty years, I have never in my life joined anything or believed in anything that asked me to, and the only claim I am able to make on anybody's attention is that I was here, and that I looked, and that what I have set down is what I saw.

They called it the sore throat disease. That was the name the county gave it in the two or three weeks before the cough, and I have found no authority for the phrase outside this county, and I have looked. The Martians did not call it anything. They have never called it anything. That is worth a paragraph of its own and I will come back to it at the end, because it is, in the end, the whole of the argument I have to make.

I heard the first coughing behind the Doyles' washhouse in the last week of September and I want to give the weather, because I have read accounts of that morning and none of them mention the weather and the weather is the reason I went out to look.

It was still hot. That is what nobody can get used to and it is the thing I would put at the front. September of that year was eighty-four, eighty-five, in the afternoon, in the shade, for a week. I went out behind my own house about four o'clock in the afternoon to see what Mrs. Doyle was doing about her beans, since her beans were the only green thing left in that block, and I found her on the ground beside the washtub, sitting up with her back against the wall, and she was coughing.

I am going to be careful here, because I have been told that I have exaggerated and I have been shown the hospital records and I will not be shown to be wrong. What she said was: something has got in. She was not a woman who used expressions. Her sons were in the war and she had buried one of them and she had a way of saying a thing once. She said, something has got in, and I have not got it out.

I got her inside. I put water on the stove, which in September is an absurdity, and I sat with her. She coughed for about twenty minutes and it was a wet, deep, tearing cough that came up out of her and brought nothing up for a long time and then brought a great deal of grey foam. She was fifty-nine years old and she had not been ill in her life. I went up the hill at a run to the Doyles' house and her husband was in the field and I got him, and by the time we had her indoors on the porch with a blanket she was coughing blood in a fine red mist into the blanket, so fine that you could not see it, you could only see the spots on the blanket darken.

She was gone in eleven days. So was her husband. So were her two next-door neighbours on the east side.

That is the sequence. I want it flat. Mrs. Doyle coughs. She is gone in eleven days. Everyone she was in contact with is gone in between two and three weeks. That is all there is. That is the whole of the mechanism and I have never heard a better description of it from any person in any county.

The sore throat disease went through the town between the eighth of September and the third of October. It was in the east end of Greenfield first, and it worked west along the main street in a rough front, house to house, and behind it there were empty streets. I could walk the length of the main street in the second week of October and you could see it as a line: on one side of the street people were still up and about, moving, and on the other side there was nothing to talk to. The Wells family on the east side had nine people in one house and six of them were down, and Mrs. Wells came out on the porch every morning at eight and swept the step, because she had swept that step every morning since 1921, and she swept it for eleven days while six of her family lay in the front room.

That is not meant to be touching. I want to be exact about what I am saying. She swept because she could not think of anything else to do with the part of her that had a job in it, and because if she went inside she would have to be in the room with them, and she was not going to do that while there was still sweeping.

Then the yellow spots.

Somebody who worked for the state health department in those years told me about it. He said the yellow spots were the last phase and that they came in about a fifth of the cases and that if they came up out of the skin, generally on the trunk, generally on the back, that was it, that was the end, and that it would be from the fourth to the sixth day after the coughing started.

His name was Rupert Molina and he drove a state health car and he had gone into about forty houses and he did not take it, and he never could work out why, and he spent the rest of his life with it. He said to me: the ones who went, went of it in their blood. The blood went bad. That is what he said. He said the Martians had done something to the blood so that the blood would not take up oxygen the way blood is supposed to, and he said that he had read that in a paper in 1950 and that the paper had been taken out of the library in 1956.

And the doctors, in their own words, in the winter of that year, in the hospital at Fort Crawford, which is not in this county but is the one all of them went to: the doctors said it was a lung disease, and they said the lung was full of something that was not water and not phlegm and that when they opened a chest in the first week of October there was nothing in it that any of them had seen before. And one of them, a man named Stow who was forty-one and who did the first of those on the west side, said out loud, in the autopsy room, to the other doctor, and I have this from the other doctor and not from Stow, who is gone: he said, well, we have never seen this before.

He said the only way he could describe the lung was that it looked as if it had been scooped out and replaced with a very fine dry powder, and that the powder was not sand and not ash and not flour and not any substance he had in that laboratory, and that when he put a swab in and looked at it under the glass the field was not blue.

I am aware that this is the point in the account where people who have read more than I have say: but the Martians themselves did not have the sore throat. That is true. They were there for eight years and they did not have it and they did not have the coughing and they did not have the yellow spots and they were not immune. They were simply not the kind of thing it worked on, and they never said so out loud in so many words.

We asked. That is what I want to say about that autumn. We asked and we asked and we asked, and it is the single strangest fact of the whole business that for eight years nobody from Mars would put a name to the thing that was taking us.

They would stand in the road and they would tell you the rule about the number of people in a room. They would tell you to take the buckets out of the wells, and everybody who took their buckets out of their wells in that first October lived. They would tell you that. They would tell you about the buckets and they would tell you about the cough and they would tell you about the lawns and they would tell you the names of the plants they were putting in the ground.

They would not say the name of the disease. And Molina, who asked, and who is one of the two or three people in the world who spent his life on this, said to me the last time I talked to him, which was in November, a year before he went: you know what the worst of it is, Freely? The worst of it is that they were polite about it. They were polite. They would have told us the name. Any decent thing would have told us the name. They told us everything else on the schedule. They told us about the bucket. They never told us about the cough.

The green dust did not stop with the wells. That is where the county learned to stop calling it weather.

The old man La Vache kept a shop on the main street, a narrow place with four rooms going back, and in those rooms, on shelves he had built himself out of oak that is now ash, were something over forty thousand books, the oldest of them printed in 1490. He had them under glass in the front window in a display he changed every week of his life. His name was La Vache and he was seventy-eight and he had never married and he had lived over that shop since he was nineteen years old. He was also, by every account I have of him, unbothered by people, and he told me in June that he could not imagine a more agreeable arrangement for a man's old age than to be surrounded by forty thousand minds that could not answer back.

I give you that detail because of what he said afterward, and because it is the only explanation that works.

On the sixth of September, in the middle of the dust, on a night when the sky over the valley was still that television colour and the air was still full of the fine white powder, a man in the street above my shop was doing what half the county was doing, which is burning his ground. He had pulled up what was left of his lawn and made a pile in his front yard and set it alight at ten in the evening, and the smoke went up in one thick column you could see from four miles, and it was not a cheerful act exactly but it was not meant to be a gloomy one either. It was a housekeeping operation. There were probably two hundred people standing in the road and a good many of them laughing. La Vache was out on his step in his shirtsleeves with his arms crossed, watching it the way you watch anything you have no opinion about.

Then the wind came around the corner of the building and went up the four inches between the street and the side wall of La Vache's shop, and where it went in under the back of the window frame it made a sound, and it brought the dust in.

I was three doors down. I was not in the shop. What I am giving you is what I was told by four separate people who were, and I will not improve on their words to make it a better story than it was, because I have spent six years listening to people improve it and it is always in the same direction, which is toward a thing that could not have happened.

The dust came in on the night breeze and it hit the shelves in the front room and it went along the shelf as a wave. It was visible. That is the part I will not soften. There was a shelf of books in the window of the front room of La Vache's shop in the town of Greenfield in this county in September, and a green dust came in at the window, and a man in the street with a scythe was standing not thirty feet away shouting and laughing, and the books went off that shelf one after another in a wave you could see, and each one took its time.

He said you could count them. He said a book would last about two seconds, and then it would be green powder on the shelf, and the shelf would go down a foot, and then the next one would start, and so on across the whole length of the shelf, four feet at a time. And he said the sound of it was like rain on a tin roof, and he said he knew that sound for forty years because he had heard it every summer when he was a boy in Iowa with the wind coming up off the corn.

The dust went through four hundred volumes on that shelf. Then it went to the end of the shelf and turned down the aisle. He said he heard it start again in the second room and he said he stood in the doorway and did not go in, and he said he understood at that point that it was not a thing that a person could stop by putting their hand on the books.

The window display went. That was the part he could not put down afterward. Not the forty thousand. The display. He had done that window every Friday for sixty years, and there was a woodblock of a hare and hounds and a case of a book on the Nile and an abacus and a stereoscope card, and he said that on that night the whole of it went off the sill in about nine seconds, the woodblock included, and that he had put the woodblock in in 1911.

He never came to tell me. I went to him. I want that clear, because there is a version of this that makes him into a man who died telling it, and he did not. He lived for four more years and he never once in those four years mentioned it to me except the once, and I want to set down that once because it is the whole of what I have from him.

He came out of the shop one afternoon in December and he had a case of dust on him, a small wooden box the size of a breadboard with a handle, and it had been the bottom shelf of case number eleven in the stockroom, and it was dust to the depth of two fingers. He set it on the counter and he looked at it for a while and he said: I was wrong. Then he said: it is not a book. Then he said: they have all been dead a long time and you can say it. Then he went back into the shop and shut the door.

That is the whole of what La Vache said to me on the subject and I have never had it out of anybody else either.

It is worth saying what the dust did to the rest of us, because the books were only the part that showed it working. It ate out wallpaper. It took the glue out of picture frames and the frames came apart and fell over with the pictures still in them. It got into the engines of parked automobiles and lifted the paint off in flakes. It got into food. It got into wells.

Across the street from La Vache was Norbert Fanshaw, who painted houses, and who has been for fifty years the closest thing this county has to a man who will say anything to your face. He came out that same night with a brush and a can of turpentine and mopped the green off his front wall by the light of La Vache's window, which was the only light on the street. And he mopped for a long time. And La Vache came out and stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and watched him work, and the two of them had the following conversation, and I have it from La Vache and from Fanshaw both, separately, and it agrees word for word.

La Vache said: they cannot help it.

Fanshaw said: they could have stopped it.

La Vache said: it is a spore. It is their way of breathing on the place.

Fanshaw said: it has eaten every book on that street and it ate four hundred books in ninety seconds in front of me, and I would like to know what their way of breathing on the place is when it is eating a man's whole occupation in front of him at ten o'clock at night, and if the answer is that they are not in the room, I would like somebody to explain the politeness of that.

Then Fanshaw finished the wall, which took him about forty minutes, and went in, and did not speak to him again for six weeks.

Then the injunction came, which is the point at which this county stopped being a place where people lived and became a place people were held.

The Martians put the injunction up on every screen and every radio in the country at the same hour, and the county heard it in a voice like a man reading out a bus timetable. It said that no human being was to assemble in groups of more than fifteen. That was all it said. Fifteen. Not a hundred and fifty. Not a thousand. And then, after it had been read, a form of words came across that I can only give as a translation, and the translator was one of my neighbours, a retired schoolteacher named Valla, who had done Italian in the war, and who said it was approximately this: the people of Earth are kind to each other, and the gathering of many is a way of being kind, and there is a thing going about among them now which is a way of being unkind, and it passes from one to another in the breathing, and it will find them out, and it will take them, and there is nothing we can do about it but keep them apart.

Fifteen.

I have never been able to get past that number. Not because it is small. Because it is exact. Somebody worked it out. Somebody on Mars, with whatever passes for arithmetic over there, put a number on it, and the number was right, and in a county where four hundred people had already gone into houses and not come out, the number was right and it worked.

So the county went quiet for real. That is the word. Before, it had been settling. From the middle of September it was quiet the way a body is quiet.

The tents went up on the school ground in October, and in them were put everybody in Greenfield who had been in the town since the sixth, which by then was about six hundred people out of nine thousand. They went voluntarily. That is important and everybody insists on it now, and it is true in the narrow sense that nobody was marched. They walked. They carried what they could. They went into the tent city and they stayed a year.

And the tent city was run by two Martians, which I did not know until 2006, and which I only learned about from a man who was in it and who never told an official anything for thirty years and then told me in a kitchen over a bottle of something.

Inside the tent city it worked. It was orderly. It was quiet. Nobody went of the coughing in the tent city. That is the fact, and it is the fact nobody wanted, because the alternative is a story where the whole thing was random, and it was not random. It was a rule. Somebody knew the rule and told us the rule and we had a year to be indoors with other people in small numbers and we took it.

It was the rest of us who went out wrong.

There was a night in that first winter, the fourteenth of December I think, when a man about sixty years old climbed to the top of the water tower at the north end of the main street with a rifle and looked down over the town and fired until the magazine was empty, and then walked off the top of it. What he thought he was doing I have never established. I have heard six explanations and I have found none of them. He was a man who had been through a divorce in 1941 and a bad winter in 1943 and who had a brother go into the war and not come back, and he had been the one to talk to the first expedition, and the last person who spoke to him in November had been the bank teller, who told me he was a very ordinary man, very polite, would hold a door.

The county put him in the ground on the eighteenth without a service. The Martians gave us the ground to do it in. They gave us the ground for eight years, and I want somebody to sit with that sentence for a minute, because it is the shape of the whole arrangement and I do not think we ever understood it: they ended the world and they gave us the ground to bury our own people in, and they did it free, and they were not thanked, because thanking them would have meant admitting there was somebody to thank.

Then the winter. Then the road. Then the eight years.

That is the part where it stops being a county and becomes a story that people tell, and I want to say clearly that I do not know when it stopped being a county. There was no day. That is the thing I said at the beginning of this and I am going to say again at the end, and it is the whole of what I can tell anybody who asks me what it was like. There was no day. In October of that year we had a town. In the spring of the next year we had a set of buildings where a town had been, and a road that came from somewhere and went to somewhere, and the somewhere on the north end was, as far as anybody could tell, simply the place where the road stopped being a road.

And every so often for eight years somebody would come up that road, and it would always be a Martian, and he would stand at the north end of the main street and not come down it, and wait, the way a man waits who has understood that he is a guest.

They called it the sore throat disease. That was the name the county gave it in the two or three weeks before the cough, and I have found no authority for the phrase outside this county, and I have looked. The Martians did not call it anything. They have never called it anything. That is worth a paragraph of its own and I will come back to it at the end, because it is, in the end, the whole of the argument I have to make.

I heard the first coughing behind the Doyles' washhouse in the first week of September and I want to give the weather, because I have read accounts of that morning and none of them mention the weather and the weather is the reason I went out to look.

It was still hot. That is what nobody can get used to and it is the thing I would put at the front. September of that year was eighty-four, eighty-five, in the afternoon, in the shade, for a week. The trees were still heavy. I went out behind the house about four o'clock in the afternoon to see what Mrs. Doyle was doing about her beans and I found her on the ground beside the washtub, sitting up with her back against the wall, and she was coughing.

I am going to be careful here, because I have been told that I have exaggerated and I have been shown the hospital records and I will not be shown to be wrong. What she said was: something has got in. She was not a woman who used expressions. Her sons were in the war and she had buried one of them and she had a way of saying a thing once. She said, something has got in, and I have not got it out.

I got her inside. I put water on the stove, which in September is an absurdity, and I sat with her. She coughed for about twenty minutes and it was a wet, deep, tearing cough that came up out of her and brought nothing up for a long time and then brought a great deal of grey foam. She was fifty-nine years old and she had not been ill in her life. I went up the hill at a run to the Doyles' house and her husband was in the field and I got him, and by the time we had her indoors on the porch with a blanket she was coughing blood in a fine red mist into the blanket, so fine that you could not see it, you could only see the freckles on the blanket darken.

She was dead in eleven days. So was her husband. So were her two next-door neighbours on the east side.

That is the sequence. I want it flat. Mrs. Doyle coughs. She is dead in eleven days. Everyone she was in contact with is dead in between two and three weeks. That is all there is. That is the whole of the mechanism and I have never heard a better description of it from any person in any county.

The sore throat disease went through the town between the eighth of September and the third of October. It was in the East Ward, the west end of Greenfield, first, and it worked east along the main street in a rough front, house to house, and behind it there were dead streets. I could walk the length of the main street in the second week of October and you could see it as a line: on one side of the street people were still up and about, moving, and on the other side there was nothing to talk to. The Wells family on the east side had nine people in one house and six of them were down, and Mrs. Wells came out on the porch every morning at eight and swept the step, because she had swept that step every morning since 1921, and she swept it for eleven days while six of her family lay in the front room.

That is not meant to be touching. I want to be exact about what I am saying. She swept because she could not think of anything else to do with the part of her that had a job in it, and because if she went inside she would have to be in the room with them, and she was not going to do that while there was still sweeping.

Then the yellow spots.

Somebody who worked for the state health department in those years told me about it. He said the yellow spots were the last phase and that they came in about a fifth of the cases and that if the yellow spots came up out of the skin, generally on the trunk, generally on the back, that was it, that was the end, and that it would be from the fourth to the sixth day after the cough started.

His name was Rupert Molina and he drove a state health car and he had gone into about forty houses and he did not contract it, and he never could work out why, and he spent the rest of his life with it. He said to me: the ones who died, died of it in their blood. The blood went bad. That is what he said. He said the Martians had done something to the blood so that the blood would not take up oxygen the way blood is supposed to, and he said that he had read that in a paper in 1950 and that the paper had been taken out of the library in 1956.

And the doctors, in their own words, in the winter of that year, in the hospital at Fort Crawford, which is not in this county but is the one all of them went to: the doctors said it was a lung disease, and they said the lung was full of something that was not water and not phlegm and that when they opened a chest in the first week of October there was nothing in it that any of them had seen before, and one of them, a man named Stow who was forty-one and who did the first autopsies on the west side, said out loud, in the autopsy room, to the other doctor, and I have this from the other doctor and not from Stow, who is dead: he said, "Well, we have never seen this before."

And Stow said that the only way he could describe the lung was that it looked like it had been scooped out and replaced with a very fine dry powder, and that the powder was not sand and not ash and not flour and not any substance he had in the laboratory, and that when he put a swab in and looked at it under the glass the field was not blue.

That was 1997, when we went back with the ship, and I have not read it anywhere else, and it is in a letter in the Fort Crawford hospital archive that I myself copied out with my own hand on the ninth of June.

I am aware that this is the point in the account where people who have read more than I have say: but the Martians themselves did not have the sore throat. That is true. They were there for eight years and they did not have it and they did not have the coughing and they did not have the yellow spots and they were not immune, they were simply not the kind of thing it worked on, and they never said so out loud in so many words.

We asked. That is what I want to say about that winter. We asked and we asked and we asked, and it is the single strangest fact of the whole business that for eight years nobody from Mars would put a name to the thing that was killing us.

They would stand in the road and they would tell you the rule about the number of people in a room. They would tell you to take the wells out of the ground and the buckets out of the wells, and everybody who took their buckets out of their wells in that first October lived. They would tell you that. They would tell you about the wells and they would tell you about the cough and they would tell you about the buckets and they would tell you about the lawns and they would tell you the name of the plants they were planting.

They would not say the name of the disease. And Molina, who asked, and who is one of the two or three people in the world who spent their life on this, said to me the last time I talked to him, which was in November, a year before he died: "You know what the worst of it is, Freely? The worst of it is that they were polite about it. They were polite. They would have told us the name. Any decent thing would have told us the name. They told us everything else on the schedule. They told us about the bucket. They never told us about the cough."

Then the eighth year, and the house by the river, and Spender.

His name was Spender. He came up that road in the spring of the seventh year and stood at the north end of the main street, the way they all did, and waited, and I was the one who went down to him, because by then it was my job in the way that being the hardware dealer was my job, and nobody objected.

He had been waiting two hours. He was about five feet ten and thin in a way that suggested not deprivation but design, and he was wearing a brown coverall suit with no mark on it anywhere, and he did not have a weapon of any kind, which by the seventh year had become the first thing you checked.

I did most of the talking and he did some. He told me what they were doing. Not in the way of a report. He talked about the weather, because he wanted me to understand that the weather was theirs, that they had made the weather, that they had been working on the weather for a hundred thousand years on Mars and it was a thing they knew and it was not a thing they were proud of. He said the weather was a kind of gardening. He said it was like the way a man makes a garden out of a piece of ground.

He said: "We have been in touch with your planet for a hundred thousand years. Do you know how we knew about you? We did not come here. We have been coming here. We knew about you from your own air. Your own atmosphere broadcasts. All you had to do was to stand still and we would know you, because the air would tell us that somebody was here."

I did not believe him and I still do not believe him and I would not change that sentence if I had it to write again. It sounded to me in the road that day like a thing a man says to be impressive, and I have never found anything to show that it was a lie, and I have never found anything to show that it was true, and I have gone over it a great many times with the two men who were on Mars at the end and both of them told me that the way he put it was the way Martians put things, that they do not distinguish between knowing and arriving, and that from their side the difference does not exist.

I want to put down what I actually felt, because I do not want this to read as a man converted.

He was very tired. That is what I remember. He talked for two hours and he got more tired as he went, not weaker but tired in a way that had a shape to it, the way a man is tired near the end of a day of work he cares about, and at some point in the middle of it he stopped and put his fingers on the surface of the hood of his truck the way a man puts his hand on a table, and I remember because I have put my hand on a lot of truck hoods in this county and it is a thing you do to be sure of where you are.

And then he said the thing I have thought about every day for two years.

He said: "I have burned your paper cities. There are five hundred of your cities on the coasts and we have burned them all. Do you know how much time it takes to burn a city?"

And I said no.

And he said: "Three weeks. Do you know what it costs to be a gardener?"

I did not have an answer. I want that on the record, because a great many people who have written about this have supplied an answer. I did not have one. He did not wait for one. He said: "Look at me. Look at what I am. You will go home and not believe me. But I have been fighting for a hundred thousand years and my heart is tired and my legs are tired and I am a very small thing compared to the warriors who will follow me. And this whole planet and everything on it — every city, every lake, every forest — I have had to fight for it and I will not be able to keep it for long. Do you understand?"

And then, about four hundred words later, the thing that broke the argument, because it was not said as an argument and I have never been able to get it back into the category of things Martians say to be impressive.

He said: "You have no reason to be unhappy. Your planet is as good as ours. When we came here we brought nothing. We took nothing that we could not replace. We are only here because there was a great deal of water and the air was sweet. And we are going to leave, and when we have gone, your world will be as it was before."

He looked up the road at the grey places where the town had been.

He said: "You will be alone, but you will not be lonely. You will be the only ones left. That is the whole of it. You will be alone and you will not be lonely, and there will be nothing left to do but to tend the garden, and you will be very tired, and there will be a great many of you, and I am sorry for that, because I know what it will be like."

I have talked to Spender three times. Two of those were on the Mars ship with the two men who were there at the end, and one of them he gave me the story of Ylla, and I did not understand it then and I do not understand it now, and I have never had it explained to me by anybody including him, and I am going to set it down and let it stand where it stands.

It was this. He said there were five Martians in a party, that they came from a settlement called Hellas, and that one of the five was a woman called Ylla who was the biologist, and that the other four were Yaro, and Feen, and M-mann, and one whose name he could not give me.

I am not going to invent a name for the one he could not say. He tried three times and each time he stopped in the middle of it and started again, and the third time he put his hand flat on the hood the way he had done before, and said: "It will not come. I have said it four hundred thousand times and it will not come out of this mouth in your language. Write it the way it sounds and you will have it wrong."

I have looked for the name in the recordings that were made of the trip. The recordings have it as he said it, which is a sound with no shape. Two people have told me they can say it and both of them have been asked, separately, by somebody who wanted to verify it, and both of them have declined, and I do not press people on that.

What Ylla did, according to Spender, was this. They put down on Mars and they went blind. All five of them. That is the fact of the story and it is what makes it a story and not a complaint. They put down on Mars and their sight went, because the light there is different, because the sun is closer and it is a different colour, and after a while the eyes stop and give you back nothing at all.

And they were not disturbed by it. That is what Spender meant and it took me years to see it. Ylla, blind, was the one who kept the seedlings alive. She could feel them. She could feel when a thing was too dry and when a thing had gone over. She held her hands a foot above the sand and she could tell, and they all learned to work off her hands, and they planted the whole length of that valley by touch, in the dark, and they got most of it in.

And the four men watched her do it for nine years. And every one of them said, in his own time, and not to her, and in the way of men talking about a thing that shamed them, that seeing had made them useless and blindness had made her necessary.

There is no moral to it and Spender did not put one and I will not put one. That is the thing I want to be careful about, because everything else in this account of mine has a moral of some kind attached to it by me, and this one does not.

I want to say one more thing about that afternoon and then I will let the eight years go by.

He told me the name of the plants they had been planting for seven years on this planet, and he said it and I wrote it down in my notebook and I did not know what it was then and I know now what it is. I looked it up when the Martians left. It is a very ordinary thing. It is the blue field grass that came up in my lawn in May of that year, the cold fine hard green that came up through the fescue in a clean sheet.

They were not building a world for themselves. I want to say that once and clearly and then let it stand. They had the whole planet worked out and named and divided and they were planting it to leave behind, and every blade of it was for us, and they said so, in the road, in the seventh year, to a hardware dealer.

They said: "We are only the sorrel. We are only a strange root in a field. But look at us. Grow us and the world will be green again."

I did not believe that and neither does anybody else, I expect, but I have a lawn. It is green. It is the only lawn for a hundred miles that has grass in it at all, and it came up cold in May of that year and nobody put it there, and I mow it, and I do not feel that anything was given to me. I feel that I am the last man in a county sitting on the only thing anybody left, and I do not know what to call that and I have had two years to call it something.

Then the eight years went by, and I want to give them in the only form I can stand to give them in.

The wind stopped in the first week of August and it did not come back that year or the next. The dust stopped with it. There was one more week of the sky the colour of a set with no picture and then the sky was the sky, blue, the ordinary blue, from the top of it to the bottom of it, and I want to say what that did to people in this county, because it is not the thing you would expect.

They were glad.

I have never been able to get used to that and I have stopped trying to. In the spring and summer of the seventh year the sky went blue and the sun came down on this county like a hand on the back of the neck and people came out of the buildings they had been in for a year and a half and stood in the road and looked up, and they were glad, and some of them were crying, and I did not understand it for a long time and then I did. They were not glad that it was over. They were glad that they could see the sky and it did not appear to them that the sky had done anything. That is the human arrangement. You do not have to hold the sky responsible. You just have to be able to look at it.

There was no grass in the county at all for the three summers. Everything was the grey fine dust. Every year in May a Marsman would stand at the north end of the main street, the way they all did, and say — and I will give the schedule because it is the most civilised document any of us has ever been handed and I have it framed in my shop — he would say: plant the seeds. Plant the seeds, do not ask questions, you have the paper, put them in the ground and water them and then go away and leave them. And people did that. Thousands of people in this county and thousands more in every county in the state put a paper of grass seed into the ground in May of the seventh year and went away and left it.

The seeds did not come up for two years. That was known. That was on the schedule. Two years.

And then in the second spring it came up, and the way it came up was this: it came up everywhere at once and not in the places you had sown it. That is a fact I have never been able to reconcile with anything and I have asked every Martian who would stand still for me, and they gave me the same answer, which is that they seeded the whole of it and not your ground, only yours, and I said that is not an answer and he said it is the whole of the answer and I did not have a second question.

The wind came back that May too. A very light wind, warm, from the west, and it went through the new grass and the new grass lay over in it and came up, and lay over and came up, all day, the way a field does. And nobody in this county could stand to watch it. There were people who could not come outside. There were people who shut the door and sat in the kitchen with the radio off. And I want to say that I was one of them, and that I have never told anybody that until now, and that there is a great deal of lying in the accounts of that period written by people who were not in this county.

Here is what the wind did to the town, and I will do it plainly, because it took a year and there is no hurry any more.

The Martians came back that August, the last time. They came into the streets with the cutting things and they went along the road and they cut the grass down in strips, and where they cut it down it did not grow again. It stayed cut. The strips stayed the height of a mown lawn for eight years, and the whole of the county floor was in strips, in bands, like a field that had been cut by somebody who knew exactly what they were doing.

They took the trees out. Not all of them. There were four hundred trees left, along the main street, in a row, the elms, and they took out everything else in the county, every oak and every cottonwood and every sycamore along the river, and they cut them at about four feet and left the stumps, and the stumps stayed four feet, and I still have one by the back fence and it is four feet and it is forty-four years old and it has not put out a bud and I do not believe it ever will.

They took the buildings apart. That is the word I will use and it is not fancy. They unbuilt them. They took them down board by board and stacked the boards, sorted by size, along the road. There are places in this county where you can walk out into what was Main Street and see rows of lumber like a lumberyard, four feet high, in stacks, the whole length of it. Some of it rotted. Most of it did not. In the eighth year a great many people took the lumber and built houses on the north hill and on the south road, on higher ground, and I built mine out of four different buildings and I could tell you which one was which wall by looking at the nails, and there are sixty nails in my kitchen wall that came out of a house that had belonged to a woman named La Vache, whose name I will come back to, and I have never been able to make myself take them out.

And I want to say what I think they were for, because nobody will say it and I have been two years with this and I am going to.

They were not leaving a museum and they were not being tidy. They were being a species that had taken over a garden. When you take over a garden you do not leave the previous gardener's trellis lying about. You take it down and stack it and you plant. That is what they did. There is no evidence that any human being was meant to use that lumber and there is strong evidence that it was not what they had in mind at all. They stacked it because it made a street.

The trouble with the last eight years of this county is that the roads healed.
