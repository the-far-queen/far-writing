Consider the film, because the page puts it there deliberately. Seven Years in Tibet was published in 1954 by Heinrich Harrer, an Austrian mountaineer, and made into a film by Jean-Jacques Annaud in 1997 with Brad Pitt playing Harrer and David Thewlis playing the Fourteenth Dalai Lama. The Dalai Lama has said publicly and more than once that the book introduced hundreds of thousands of people to his country, and that for this he was grateful. The Dalai Lama is entitled to his opinion about his own life, and the opinion is generous, and it is also the opinion of a man whose institution depends on not litigating the matter. The historical record is another document.

Harrer entered Tibet in December 1945 and was in Lhasa, with intermittent absences, from early 1946 until his departure in April 1950. That is roughly four years. The title says seven. The first six months of the seven are spent reaching the plateau at all, on foot, after escaping a British internment camp in India, and the seven-year figure appears to derive from a misreading of an earlier German edition's framing rather than from a lie. Harrer was a member of the Nazi Party, a signatory of the party programme, and spent the war in the SS; the first English edition of his book concealed this, and it became public in the 1990s through his own accounts, the testimony of survivors of his mountain expeditions, and his own written statement. He was not the tutor of a five-year-old boy. The Fourteenth Dalai Lama was born on 6 July 1935 and was therefore fourteen or fifteen during Harrer's years in Lhasa, a youth who had been recognised as the tulku of the Thirteenth in 1937, publicly proclaimed in October 1939, walked to Lhasa from Amdo across three months in 1939, and enthroned at the Potala on 22 February 1940, and who had already taken full temporal and monastic responsibilities in 1944 when the regent died. The film gives you a five-year-old child and an adult European in a relationship of tutelage. That relationship is a construction of the Western imagination about how influence is transmitted, and it is worth noticing that it is the version that most Westerners bring to the plateau in their heads.

None of this makes the book worthless. Harrer was an extremely good mountaineer and a decent photographer, and his account of the Potala interiors, of the ritual, of the cold, of the daily texture of a monastic city is affectionate and often precise. But it is the account of an outsider who was given a great deal of access, and who is candid about his own liking for the access, and who was there because he was a political refugee from his own country. He fled a Europe he had helped construct. That is worth holding onto. A tradition that gets to tell its story mainly through guests is a tradition being read from the outside.

Hold the two facts together: this is a place with an extraordinarily well-documented history of conquest, famine, cultural destruction and political imprisonment; and it is a place that the West has been consuming as a spiritual consumable since at least the middle of the twentieth century. Both are true at the same time, in the same building, on the same afternoon, and the tension between them is not a paradox to be resolved. It is the situation.

The map. Before you go anywhere, understand that this is not one level. The first thing to understand about Tibet, as a level, is that it is not a single level. It is four, or five, depending on whether you count Bon, and the four are not denominations of one church. They are separate institutions with separate founder texts, separate canonical collections, separate lineages of transmission, separate systems of monastic law, and in several cases separate political trajectories through the twentieth century. A Gelug monk and a Nyingma tertön are not two clergy of the same confession. They are more distant from each other than a Lutheran is from a Methodist, and in one important respect more distant, because Gelug scholasticism treats Nyingma antinomianism as an error to be refuted rather than a difference to be tolerated.

You can stand in Sera monastery, which the page lists with the Gelug, and in the main assembly hall hear four debates a day between monks, and understand that what you are watching is an examination system. This is not a meditation centre. It is a university that also happens to chant, and it has been running a formal curriculum with examinations for around four hundred years, and before that, in a predecessor form, considerably longer. The schedule is published. The morning debate runs roughly seven to ten, the noon debate eleven to one, the afternoon two to four, and the night debate half past eight to half past nine, with seasonal adjustment. Two monks argue. One defends a thesis, and the other refutes it, and the refutation is the point. The defending monk may not raise his voice, may not gesture. The attacking monk wears a mask over his mouth so that his breath cannot be heard. He wears a grey hat with a mirror on it, so that the defender cannot see his expression. He calls the defender's name. He asks, in a raised hand-clap sequence so that each syllable is a physical stroke, for the refuting of the four extremes.

You will not understand the content for months. You will understand the room within an hour. You will understand, sitting on a bench with a thermos of butter tea while a boy of fifteen dismantles the philosophical position of a boy of sixty, that this is a machine for finding out whether a person knows what they think they know. It is an oral machine. It has almost nothing to do with writing, and the level page is about to make a great deal of fuss about writing, and both are correct, and the tension between them is the actual subject of this level.

The path choice. The game gives you a path choice at the start, one of six destinations, and if you chose Tibet you chose a game master who is a Tibetan Buddhist lama in the Shaolin lineage, and the level's own promise is that you will meet him and not recognise him, and that he will play the fool, and possibly get drunk, and that you will reject him, and that he will also see that you are not ready. That is the scenario as written, and it is a fair piece of dramatic construction. It is also, without meaning to be, an accurate description of how this material is usually transmitted to Westerners, and a fairly inaccurate description of how it is transmitted to Tibetans. Both are worth following.

Here is the choice as it actually presents itself, once you are on the ground.

You can take the path of the collector.

This is the path of the museum, the bookshelf and the retreat-centre brochure. You buy the paintings. You take the tsatsa prints, the small gilded clay ones, in their thousands, assembled from moulds, sometimes by the same monastery that supplies the mould and sometimes not. You buy the singing bowls, which are mostly not old and never were singing bowls in any functional sense until about thirty-five years ago, and the singing bowl industry in Nepal is a cottage industry with a tourist interface. You buy the singing bowl and the singing-bowl cushion and the malas and the singing-bowl-strike-mallet and the felted ring cushion that keeps the bowl from sliding, and after a few years of this you have more stuff than the monastery you came from, and when the temple asks whether you can help with the roof you have to find a way to explain that your money is in a different currency.

The collector's path is not dishonest. Some of the best scholarship on Tibetan material culture, and a great deal of the best photography of Tibetan monastic life, has been done by collectors. The field was built out of collections. But the collector's path has a characteristic failure, which is that the object separates from the use. A tsatsa is a moulded relief of a specific deity, made by a specific mould, and the mould belongs to a lineage, and the lineage has a transmission, and the transmission has a living holder. Once the tsatsa is on your wall in Los Angeles it is inert. It is not connected to anything. It may as well be a plaque. And the collecting impulse, which is a desire to hold a piece of the thing you cannot yet enter, is the direct engine of the market that moves those objects out of the villages they were made in.

There is a version of the collector's path that is not interested in Tibet at all, and the page has already given you the name for it in its list of magic objects: the Golden Urn, the relic. Bronze figurines of lamas in matched sets. Calligraphy sets with the four dignities printed on the box. And, documented in a Los Angeles Times dispatch from January 1989, photographs of the Dalai Lama that had been illegal to own were being sold openly from stalls in front of the Jokhang, the holiest building in Tibetan Buddhism, while monks and herders asked foreigners for money to buy them. The report noted what that meant: a people who had gone from begging tourists for a picture of their exiled leader to selling the picture themselves, in the place where the leader's office once stood. This exists, it is documented, and it is the clearest available demonstration that a political occupation does not merely suppress a culture. It moves it into the market.

You can take the path of the scholar.

This is the honest path and the slow one. It looks like the library. It looks like the National Library of Australia in Canberra, which holds one of the finest collections of Tibetan material outside Asia. It looks like the Buddhist Digital Resource Center, founded in 1999 in Bellingham, Washington by E. Gene Smith after he left the Library of Congress, which now hosts the largest collection of digitized Tibetan texts in the world and lets anyone consult Tibetan architectural drawings and polychrome manuscripts online without a password. It looks like the 84000 project, which began releasing freely the entire Tibetan Buddhist canon, roughly 230,000 pages, with parallel translations, because in the late 1990s someone worked out that only about five per cent of it existed in any modern translation.

The scholar's path has one classic failure, which is to arrive at the perfect sentence and stop. You will produce work that is accurate, cited, unobtainable and unread. You will learn Tibetan properly. You will do field years in eastern Tibet, in Amdo, where the dialect is different, where the local monasteries are the Nyingma, where the reorganisation of the Gelug has never fully arrived. You will write an article that four hundred people read and none of them can act on, and the distance between the article and the field will be exactly the distance between your field site and the people in your field site who are not also your field site.

The scholar's other failure is the mirror of the collector's. You can end up with a view of Tibetan Buddhism from the outside that is complete, sourced and uninhabited. Tibet becomes an object of analysis. That is a real possibility and it is not the same as the tradition.

You can take the path of the practitioner.

This is the path the page is built for, and the hardest one, and the one with the highest dropout rate. Practising means accepting an obligation you cannot check. It means a teacher who will tell you that you are wrong and that your objection to being told you are wrong is itself the problem. It means a curriculum measured in decades, in a system where the entry credential is roughly a bachelor's degree of formal study and the next credential is another nine or ten. It means giving up the right to the version of the tradition that confirms you, which is the version you were brought here looking for.

There is a particular hazard on this path that belongs in a level about transmission, and it is this. The teacher's job, in almost every lineage on the page, is partly to prevent you from getting what you came for. If you came for the Tibet of the photograph, the teacher is obliged to be a disappointment to you, and the relationship survives on the day you do not require the teacher to be what you came for.

The three paths are not exclusive and most real lives drift between them. What matters for this level is that you know which one you are on, because the level is built so that you can play it wrong for a long time before anything tells you that you have.

There is a fourth possibility, and the game does not build it, which is that you come here to pay attention to what is being done to a people. That is not a path in the game's vocabulary. It is a reason to be on the map, and in the current condition of the plateau it is the most urgent reason.

The teaching machine. The page gives you four lines under the photograph. Snowy Mountains. Tipikata. Tenjur. Kanjur. Two of those are canon and two of those are a mountain and a place, and the page does not distinguish between them, which is the first thing worth noticing about how this material is normally presented to a newcomer. The Kangyur is the words of the Buddha. The Tengyur is the commentary. Between them, depending on the edition you pick, you have somewhere around 101 to 120 volumes of sutra and 220 to 250 volumes of commentary; the Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia's own figures are 1,169 texts and about 70,000 pages for the Kangyur, and 4,093 texts and about 161,800 pages for the Tengyur. That is a quarter of a million pages. It is the largest printed body of Buddhist writing in any language and one of the largest bodies of religious writing in the world, and essentially none of it was available in English translation until about the turn of the millennium.

Hold that number for a moment, because it is the whole level. A quarter of a million pages of systematic teaching on how to train a mind, and at the time the West was reading it, the monks who had been trained in it had never seen a printing press that could set it in their own script. That is not a metaphor. Tibetan writing before the twentieth century was almost entirely done in manuscript, on paper made from bark, and the canonical complex was preserved the way other people preserve a cathedral: in buildings, in rooms, in monastic hands.

Here is how the text got here in the first place, because the mechanics matter more than the mystical framing usually given to them.

Buddhism came to Tibet from India through a translation movement that ran from the seventh century to the ninth. Lotsawa, the translator, was a specific monastic office with a specific training. The translator took a Sanskrit or Indic text and rendered it into Tibetan, and then, critically, a second person who had not seen the Sanskrit went back and checked the Tibetan against the Indic, and where the Tibetan was wrong or thin, a third person supplied. The Tibetan term for the whole apparatus is muchan, the two translators, and later the system broadened into a panel of specialists who could handle the technical vocabulary of tantra. Sanskrit and Tibetan are not related languages. They are related by a translation tradition eight hundred years long, and when you read a Tibetan tantra in translation, you are reading something that passed through at least two languages and three minds before it got to you.

King Songtsen Gampo, who ruled from about 627 to 650, is credited with the first Tibetan script itself. Not the alphabet, which probably came from a Brahmi model, but the conventions for using it, and with it the imperial decision to make Tibetan a written administrative language. The same king is credited with the first monastery in Tibet, Samye, modelled on the Buddhist monastery at Nalanda in India and built in such a way that the Indian parts face south and the Tibetan parts face north, so that the whole building is an argument about where Buddhist authority actually sits. Around 792 to 794, according to the Tibetan accounts and not to any independent witness, a debate was held at Samye between Indian and Chinese masters over whether enlightenment comes gradually through practice or suddenly through insight. The Chinese Chan side lost. One version says the defeated masters were sent back to China and had their legs crippled so they could not preach; that detail is almost certainly a Tibetan anti-Chinese narrative device from a later period, and it has been repeated for a thousand two hundred years in a form that reads as history. It is worth knowing that the founding story of Tibetan Buddhism contains a propaganda layer, because the page's framing of Tibet as the place that preserved the authentic teaching while China lost it is itself a piece of that story.

And here is the first great irony the level has in store, before we have even left the eighth century. The tradition that defined itself in Tibet by defeating China became, two centuries later, an imperial instrument of China. In 1042, the Songtsen Gampo dynasty having ended, the Mongol leader Altan Khan of the Western Xia engaged Songtsen Gampo's great-grandson Gyatse Trizin, founder of the Sakya school, in a dispute. Gyatse lost. His nephew, Sakya Pandita, addressed the letter that converts a defeated family into a ruling one, advising the Khans that Tibetan Buddhism was the tool by which they would rule Tibet. From that point the Sakya hierarchs served the Yuan court, and Sakya Pandita's brother, Drogön Chögyal Phagpa, was given the title of Imperial Preceptor and taught at the Yuan court in the 1260s. In 1279 the Yuan completed the conquest of the Song dynasty. The Tibetan lamas went to Dadu, the Yuan capital in what is now Beijing, to advise emperors on matters of state.

A Western reader who has absorbed the Samye narrative, in which the Chinese lost the debate and Buddhism retreated to Tibet for safekeeping, will find this genuinely difficult to absorb. But the two stories are the same story, and the resolution is not that one of them is a lie. The Tibetan lamas went to the Yuan court because they had lost a war, and they were good at what they did, and the dynasty needed administration more than it needed purity. Meanwhile the Tibetan state itself had collapsed and been absorbed by Mongols. What looks from outside like preservation of a pure tradition was, in part, one aristocratic family allying itself with an empire in order to keep governing a territory it had just lost.

The teaching machine has three layers and it is worth being exact about each.

The first is the text. Sanskrit, Indic and Tibetan originals, preserved in a limited number of physical editions. On the page these are called the Tipikata, the Kanjur, the Tenjur. Under the gelug school they are exactly what they sound like: a three-tiered structure of scripture, commentary, and the logical-philosophical treatises that sit underneath both. The Tibetan word that the page renders as Tripitaka, the word that refers to the Pali Canon of the Theravada school in Thailand and Sri Lanka, is used on this page to mean something else entirely, the Tibetan Buddhist canon, and the page lists it under Tibetan Buddhist Schools without marking the switch. That confusion is endemic and worth learning to detect.

The second layer is the curriculum. In Gelug, this is the Lamrim, the graduated stages of the path, and Tsongkhapa's Lamrim Chenmo, the Great Treatise, is the foundational text of the whole system. The stage structure is roughly as follows. First the small scope, that you must strive to gain a human rebirth, because the alternative is rebirth in one of the six realms, three of them horrible, and you should understand this before you try to be spiritual. Second the intermediate scope, that you must strive for the higher rebirths of a god or a bodhisattva. Third the great scope, that you must strive for full Buddhahood, for the sake of all beings, not only yourself. The Lamrim Chenmo is several thousand folios. Students study it in sections, memorise it, debate it, and are examined on it. The Mengshe examination system, the bodhisattva examination, takes about two years and is earned before a large assembly.

The third layer is the personal transmission. This is the part that is genuinely oral, genuinely embodied, and genuinely fragile, and it is the reason you cannot buy your way in with a book.

The mechanics of transmission in Tibetan Buddhism are not secret and they are not mystical. They are a system of four or five kinds of transmission, and every teacher you sit in front of holds all of them.

The scriptural transmission passes the words of a teacher to a student, who receives them, memorises them, recites them back. It is oral, and it is also the route by which texts travel from one language to another, since the teacher can be a person who only has the text in Sanskrit and the student only has Tibetan.

The oral transmission passes an explanation. The teacher explains, the student questions, the student explains back, and the teacher corrects. This is what you are hearing in a debate courtyard, and it is the foundational pedagogy of the entire system.

The mind transmission passes an experience. This is the one Western students ask about constantly and understand least. It is not a feeling and it is not a mood and it is not a transmission of energy. In its most developed form, mind-to-mind transmission is the direct passing of a state of consciousness from one mind to another without an intervening sensory medium, without a word, without a symbol. It is reserved for the very highest level of teacher-student bond, and in the traditional description it is established over a very long period of close association and mutual understanding. It cannot be faked and it cannot be rushed. And, crucially, the teacher cannot actually do it on demand, because it depends on conditions internal to both minds that neither of them controls. A teacher in the Tibetan system is thought to be able to transmit what the teacher has actually realised, and only that. A teacher who has read widely and realised nothing has texts to give you and nothing else, and one of the great implicit judgments in the tradition is that this difference is visible.

The secret transmission passes an esoteric practice, given under seal, to be practised under instruction and not disclosed. This is the tantric layer, and it is where the page's Chalice, the Vajrayana, the mandala creation, the tulkus and the terma all live.

And there is a fifth category, the lineage transmission, which is not about a text or a state of mind at all. It is about authorisation. The lineage is a chain of teaching holders going back to the historical Buddha. To be a holder of the lineage is to be authorised to teach at a particular level. This is the transmission that survives the death of any individual, and it is the answer to the question the level keeps asking.

Why can a written practice outlive the person who practised it? Look at the Lamrim Chenmo. It is still taught, still debated, still examined, verbatim, in the same order, in monasteries in Dharamsala, in Mundgod in South India, in Bylakuppe, in Sera outside Kathmandu, in a classroom in Portland, Oregon, in a study group in a suburb of Melbourne. Tsongkhapa died in 1419. The practice did not stop. It is still running. That is the answer, and it is unsentimental: the practice is embodied in the text, and the text is embodied in a curriculum, and the curriculum is embodied in an examination with a structure that does not depend on the personality of the person administering it. The system is designed to be robust to the death of any single human being, and to be dependent on no human being at all. That is what makes it a tradition in the technical sense rather than a cult.

And the same design is what makes it fragile in a different way, which is the subject of the next level sub-chapter, and which is the reason the diaspora question is not a sentimental one.

The lineage, and what happened to it. A lineage is a chain. Every link in the chain is a person, and the chain is only as strong as its shortest link, and the chain is also only as long as the territory in which it can be physically continued.

The clearest case is the Karmapa. The First Karmapa, Düsum Khyenpa, was a disciple of Tsongkhapa's teacher and established the Karma Kagyu school in the twelfth century. The lineage runs continuously for over eight hundred years and is the oldest tulku lineage in Tibetan Buddhism, predating the Dalai Lama lineage by more than two centuries. The Sixteenth Karmapa, Rangjung Rigpe Dorje, was born on 14 August 1924, left Tibet in 1959, and reestablished his seat at Rumtek in Sikkim. He died on 5 November 1981. He left a letter naming his successor, a letter that was itself a piece of esoteric technique, a prophetic verse that his students had to find before they could find the boy. The letter was found. In 1992 a search party, working through a documented sequence involving a tai-tse or elephant-flag oracle, identified a child among the nomads of eastern Tibet, and in January 2000 that boy was brought out of China. He lives in Dharamsala. He is the Seventeenth Karmapa.

The complication is that in 1992 a second search, running in parallel, found a second boy in Lhasa, and the Chinese authorities enthroned him at Tsurphu that September with state approval, the first high reincarnation they had approved since 1951. That boy is Ngawang Wangchuk Norbu, and he is referred to in the international press as the Beijing Karmapa. Both claimants are called the Seventeenth Karmapa. Both have followers. The Kagyu school has been divided between them for more than thirty years. This is not a doctrinal dispute that could be settled by reading the texts. It is a dispute about who held which transmission, and it has been settled, if at all, by the relative institutional power of the two claimants. The one recognised by the government in Lhasa receives state support. The one recognised by the community in exile lives in India under Indian protection. The Dharma in this case is a function of the distribution of power, and any honest account of the lineage has to say so.

The Second case is the Panchen Lama, and this one is a different kind of rupture. The Panchen Lamas are heads of the Tashilhunpo monastery in Shigatse, the second most important position in Tibetan Buddhism. The lineage has run without interruption since the fifteenth century. In May 1995 the Dalai Lama recognised a six-year-old boy, Gedhun Choekyi Nyima, from Lhari in Nagchu in northeastern Tibet, as the Eleventh Panchen Lama. On 17 May 1995, three days later, the boy and his family were taken away by Chinese authorities. He has not been seen in public since. He is the youngest political prisoner in the world according to the United States Commission on International Religious Freedom, which has listed him since 1995. He was six. It is now over thirty years since anyone has seen him. The Chinese state rejected the Dalai Lama's choice and appointed another boy, Gyaincain Norbu, through the Golden Urn lottery process, and Gyaincain Norbu is now a state official with state authority over Tibetan Buddhism and a public profile in Beijing. The Panchen Lama's disappearance is not an isolated cruelty. It is the deliberate destruction of a line of succession, because a living Panchen Lama who could be found, educated, and enthroned would be a figure of independent authority inside Tibet, and the state's entire structure for controlling Tibetan Buddhism depends on there not being one.

And here you have to go back to the Qianlong emperor, because the Golden Urn that China used to appoint Gyaincain Norbu is a real object with a real history, and it is the single most instructive artefact in this whole question.

In 1792, after a war with Nepal, the Qianlong emperor issued the Twenty-nine Regulations for Reorganising Tibet. Among them was a reform of the system of recognising reincarnated lamas. The emperor, it is recorded, presented the Tibetan religious establishment with a golden urn, and decreed that in future, whenever a high tulku died and needed to be reincarnated, the search party should identify several candidates, write their names on slips, and draw from the urn to determine which was legitimate. The stated motivation was to reduce corruption and factional fighting in the search process, and to strengthen the throne's authority. And it did both. In 1883 the Ninth Panchen Lama, the Tenth, the Eleventh, the Thirteenth Dalai Lama in 1893, and many others were all selected by the urn procedure, under Chinese supervision. The system functioned. It functioned for a hundred and fifty years. And in 1995 the state used that same procedure to install its own Panchen Lama, over the Dalai Lama's explicit objection.

This is the pattern that runs through everything, and it deserves to be stated plainly because it is the real mechanism of the last century and not a marginal one. The Chinese state's position on Tibetan Buddhism is not that it despises it. It is that it regards it as a genuine religion that must be administered. It funds temple restoration on a large scale. It employs lamas. It gives awards. What it will not tolerate is an independent stream of authority, because an independent stream of authority is the one thing a religious tradition produces that a state cannot tax. So the strategy is consistently the same: preserve the form, control the appointment. Rebuild the monastery. Appoint the lama. Regulate the curriculum. And when a succession produces a candidate the state does not control, take the child.

You can see the same mechanism in a second case, and this one turns on a piece of physical evidence. The 16th Karmapa died on 5 November 1981 at Rumtek, and in his cremation pyre his heart was found not to have burned. His students took this as confirmation of the letter he had left, a prophetic verse naming his successor that had to be found before the boy could be found. In March 1992 Situ Rinpoche announced that he had discovered the letter and that it pointed to a boy in eastern Tibet. In 1992 two different searches, running for about two months and finding two different boys, converged on the same answer. One party identified a nomad's son and took him to Lhasa on 13 June; he was enthroned at Tsurphu on 27 September in the presence of Chinese officials, the first high reincarnation approved by Beijing since 1951, and he has been recognised by the state ever since, referred to as the Beijing Karmapa. Another party produced a rival candidate in 1994, after the Shamar Rinpoche publicly rejected the authenticity of Situ's letter. That boy came out of China in January 2000 and is referred to as the Delhi Karmapa. Both are in their forties. Both have followers, both have a lineage, both claim the same transmission, and the two branches of the Kagyu school have been divided for three decades. The state did not consult the lineage and did not need to. It had the Golden Urn, a hundred and fifty years of imperial precedent for using it, and the entire apparatus of a state to enforce its answer. Which Karmapa holds the transmission is, in practice, settled by who controls the territory on which the Karma Kagyu seat was historically located, and that has been the situation since the eighteenth century.

This is what happened to the lineages. It is not mystical and it is not a conspiracy. It is the ordinary operation of an imperial power confronting a religious institution that had been, until recently, also a national government.

The archive. If you want to know what actually happened to Tibetan Buddhism, do not read a book about religion. Go and count.

The count begins with the numbers. Before 1959, Tibet had approximately 2,464 monasteries, according to Chinese government figures, and roughly 110,000 monks and nuns, which is about one fifth of the entire pre-1959 Tibetan population, a population that was somewhere between 1.2 and 1.3 million. After what the Chinese call the democratic reform campaign, the number of monasteries was reduced to ten and the number of monks to about two thousand, according to a Chinese official quoted by the New York Times in July 1979. The same source described what remained as "the Lamaist theocracy," in the reporter's phrase, and a substantial part of the population did not yet know what had happened to it.

Those are the poles. The middle is where the real work of destruction happened, and it happened in the 1960s, and it happened to objects.

The Jokhang temple, the most sacred building in Tibetan Buddhism, was entered by Red Guards in August 1966 and ransacked. Its contents were destroyed and its sacred images were desecrated. Ribur Rinpoche, a lama who had been jailed in Lhasa for twenty years beginning in 1959, was released in 1979 under the post-Mao liberalisation policies and was given a position in the Tibet Office of Religious Affairs, and one of the things he did with that position was oversee the restoration of the Jowo Mikyo Dorje, the principal image of the Jokhang, one of the most venerated objects in Tibetan Buddhism, which is said to have been cast by a king more than a thousand years ago and which is described in the tradition as being periodically laundered. The restoration was possible only because a long-imprisoned monk with no resources and no international standing obtained the cooperation of the Chinese state. That is the actual texture of what happened to Tibetan Buddhism. It is not a clean story of destruction. It is a story of destruction, and then of a very long fight over scraps conducted at the sufferance of the state, by people the state had imprisoned for decades.

The printed canon tells its own story if you know how to read it. The Tibetan canon was carved in woodblocks, and the Derge printing house, the parkhang at Derge in eastern Kham under the ruler Tenpa Tsering, was established in 1729 and produced the edition that became canonical. Scholars and craftspeople there edited and then cut the blocks for the Kangyur between 1729 and 1733, the work directed by Situ Panchen Chökyi Jungné, and in the following decades they completed the far larger 224-volume Tengyur. The parkhang is a working institution and holds the greatest number of Tibetan woodblocks in the world, with a corpus that had reached close to a hundred thousand blocks by 1738. A considerable quantity of the Derge and Narthang woodblocks were later carried out of Tibet and are now in libraries in the West, which means that a substantial part of the Tibetan canon now exists outside Tibet in two forms, the woodblock edition and the digital edition, and the story of how it got there is itself a story about geopolitics.

Consider the case of Sakya monastery. The Sakya hierarchs governed Tibet during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries under Yuan overlordship. The Sakya seat held, until 1959, a library of some eighty thousand volumes of texts, which the Chinese state relocated during the Cultural Revolution and which was the subject of a documented relocation in the 2000s with handlers signing logs. The Forty-second Sakya Trizin, who has held the Sakya throne since 2017, is the first Sakya hierarch in the modern period to be based outside Tibet. The Thirty-Ninth Sakya Trizin, Chögyal Phagpa, had been the last to be enthroned in Sakya itself. The seat of one of the four great schools of Tibetan Buddhism has not been on its own territory since the 1950s.

And the archive has been growing in the West in a way that has no precedent in the history of the tradition. The Buddhist Digital Resource Center, founded 1999, now hosts the largest collection of digitised Tibetan texts in the world, including the Tibetan Buddhist Resource Center's polychrome manuscript collection and the architectural drawings from Tibet, all freely available. The 84000 project is working on translating and publishing the entire canon. And the actual physical books, the ones that were carried out by monks on the retreat, are now in university libraries in Canberra, in Cambridge, in Berlin, in the Bodleian, in the Harvard-Yenching, in the University of Leiden, and in a growing number of smaller collections.

None of this is a restoration. It is a relocation, and the difference matters. A text removed from a functioning ecosystem and put in an archive is preserved as an artefact but it has been removed from the system that made it a teaching device. A Debating text in a debate courtyard in Sera is not the same object as the same text in a box in a climate-controlled vault in Canberra, even if the Tibetan is character-perfect, even if the polychrome and the woodblock illustrations are photographed at full resolution. The archive preserves the words. It does not preserve the transmission, because the transmission is not in the words.

The record. The level is called Tibet and the page puts Brad Pitt at the top of it, and if you play the level as written you will spend most of your time on the roof of a bus with a prayer wheel in one hand and a view of Kailash in the other. So put the historical record first, in the front of the level, where it belongs, and then go back to the roof of the bus.

The conquest has a sequence and the sequence is documented in unusually good detail for the period.

In 1950 the Chinese People's Liberation Army entered Chamdo, the largest town in eastern Tibet, and it did so largely through an operation undertaken by a small number of troops who walked in disguised, in the last weeks of 1950, because Chamdo's garrison and its local militia were not strong enough to be worth a battle and the commanders had calculated the risk of a real fight against the risk of simply entering. Once they were there they were not dislodged. Chamdo fell in December 1950, which ended the military position of the Tibetan government east of the Tsarong, and the Dalai Lama's government lost its eastern half.

In late April 1951 a Tibetan delegation arrived in Beijing to negotiate. On 23 May 1951 they signed the Seventeen Point Agreement, the Agreement on Measures for the Peaceful Liberation of Tibet. It was signed at Zhongnanhai in Beijing. On the Chinese side the chief delegate was Ngabo Ngawang Jigme, the Tibetan official from Chamdo, and the delegation included people appointed by the Kashag, the Tibetan cabinet, and one of them, Ngawang Jigme, a monk, went to Beijing carrying a letter from the Dalai Lama. On the Chinese side of the table sat a delegation including senior figures of the Party and the government. The agreement has seventeen numbered points. Its language is the language of 1951 China, in which everything is a liberation and the people being liberated are lucky, and reading it is a useful exercise precisely because it is so naked. It pledges that religious belief and customs will be respected and protected, that the Tibetan lama system will not be altered, that monasteries will not be interfered with, that the Tibetan language and script will be respected in government and education. It provides for a Tibetan area of self-government under a high degree of autonomy under the central government, and it pledges the central government to take no steps to change the social system or the religious beliefs of the Tibetans.

Read it as a contract and it is a document in which a monastic government and a continental empire agree to differ on sovereignty while sharing administrative reality. Read it as a Chinese Communist Party document and it is a document in which the Party promises to leave religion alone in exchange for recognition of Chinese sovereignty, with the sovereign status of a foreign state written out as something the Tibetans had falsely believed themselves to possess.

It did not work as a contract. The phrase democratic reform entered Chinese policy in 1956, and between 1956 and 1959 a campaign against the Tibetan nobility and the monasteries produced the largest refugee exodus in modern Tibetan history. The Tibetan government gave up the agreement formally in 1959 and the Dalai Lama left.

The uprising in Lhasa ran from 10 to 23 March 1959, over two weeks, with a second wave of fighting in April. It began with what sources describe as a confrontation in which the uprising's organisers attempted to force the PLA garrison in Lhasa to leave the city, and it became a broader rising involving Khampa fighters who had come down from eastern Tibet and were, in several documented engagements, better armed and better led than the PLA units they fought. The PLA eventually put Lhasa under martial law on 28 March. Chinese sources place the number of PLA personnel in the region by the mid-1960s at close to 200,000. Some eighty thousand Tibetans died in the suppression of the uprising according to exile-government figures, and there is no reliable independent count, and the range of estimates is very wide.

What is not in dispute is the sequence that follows. On the night of 17 March 1959 the Dalai Lama, aged 23, left Potala Palace disguised as a monk and began a four-day journey on foot across the Tibetan plateau. He crossed into India on 30 March 1959 and has not been in Tibet since. About 80,000 Tibetans followed him into exile in 1959 and the years immediately after. Jawaharlal Nehru gave him refuge and made arrangements for him to stay at Mussoorie and then Dharamsala, and in 1960 the Tibetan government was reconstituted in exile as the Central Tibetan Administration. In 1961 the Indian Parliament took a formal decision on Tibetan refugees. The Indian constitution does not have a refugee category and India did not give them formal refugee status; the legal framework that resulted was administrative and discretionary.

The population consequences of that decade are the hardest part of the record, and here the discipline matters more than anywhere else in this level. The Great Chinese Famine of 1959 to 1961 killed somewhere between fifteen and forty-five million people across China depending on whose demography you accept; the most common modern academic estimate is in the thirty-million range, and the range is wide because the Chinese state's own records were destroyed and the demographic method used changes the answer. Tibet was inside that famine and in some regions the mortality was higher than the national average, and this is not disputed by serious demographers including those working inside the Chinese state. What is contested is the specific Tibetan figure. Zheng Zhenman and Wang Daiyu's demographic analysis, published in the early 1980s and the most cited Western-language estimate, put excess deaths in Tibet in 1959 to 1961 at around 434,000, which is roughly a sixth of the population. Contemporary Chinese-language scholarship disputes this figure and argues for a substantially lower one, and the dispute is conducted in journals rather than in pamphlets. What can be said without taking sides is that the Tibetan population fell sharply in absolute terms through the late 1950s and early 1960s and rose steadily afterwards, and that the fall is not explicable by emigration, because the emigrants left rather than died.

Now the Cultural Revolution, 1966 to 1976. The Red Guards entered Lhasa in 1966. Monasteries were closed, monks and nuns were sent to labour, and sacred objects were destroyed on a scale that the Central Cultural Group's own 1971 document later described in terms of religious damage. The Jokhang was ransacked. The Great Prayer Festival, the Monlam Chenmo, which had been held in Lhasa continuously since it was founded in 1409 by Tsongkhapa, was cancelled, revived briefly in 1985, and prohibited again in 1990. The number of monasteries operating in Tibet fell from 2,464 to around ten by the late 1970s, with about 2,000 monks and nuns remaining. Nearly every senior lama in Tibet was imprisoned. Among them was the Drikhung Kyabgon Chungtsang Rinpoche, head of the Drikhung Kagyu lineage, who was jailed in December 1959 and remained imprisoned for twenty-three years, until 1982, undergoing struggle sessions and forced labour throughout the Cultural Revolution.

The years after 1979 brought the enforcement of a different kind of violence, and this is the one most Western accounts underreport. Monasteries were reopened and re-educated, which means the monks were put through political instruction and the religion was reshaped into a form the state could administer. Many monks and nuns were required to live outside the monasteries. Beginning in the 1990s and accelerating sharply in the 2000s the state's programme shifted from suppression to Sinicisation, and Sinicisation is best understood not as an attack on belief but as an attempt to replace Tibetan Buddhism as a living religious tradition with Tibetan Buddhism as an administrative category. The regulations governing reincarnate lamas require state approval. Chinese cadres sit on the management committees of Tibetan monasteries. Monasteries are required to keep detailed records of the religious activity of their monks and nuns, and those records are transmitted upward.

The clearest single event in the modern period is a number and it is not in dispute. According to figures compiled by the Tibetan Centre for Human Rights and Democracy and other monitoring organisations, at least 157 Tibetans have set themselves on fire in protest since 2009, of whom the great majority have died. The first was Tapey, a monk from Kirti Monastery, who burned himself in a marketplace in Ngawa in Sichuan on 27 February 2009. Most of the recorded immolations occurred between 2011 and 2013. The Tibetan government-in-exile and international organisations maintain that the self-immolations are a response to Chinese policy, to the presence of armed police, and to the imprisonment and disappearance of Tibetans, and China maintains that they are separatist acts. Both things can be true of a person, which is the difficulty with interpreting an act by a person who is also a political subject.

You will also hear that Tibet under Chinese rule has seen health and education transformed, and this is not propaganda. Life expectancy in the Tibet Autonomous Region rose from roughly 35 years in 1959 to roughly 68 by the late 2010s according to Chinese official statistics. Literacy is near universal. The 2020 national census recorded a Tibetan Autonomous Region population of about 3.65 million, of whom about 3.14 million were recorded as Tibetan and about 66,800 as other ethnic minorities, which leaves a Han Chinese population of roughly 430,000, about 12 per cent of the total. The region's growth rate has been among the fastest in China. There is a real, documented, self-reported pattern of rural poverty alleviation and infrastructure spending. None of that is a lie and none of it answers the question the census cannot ask, which is the question of a language and a lineage and a succession.

Set the two columns side by side. On one side: a population that has multiplied, life expectancy that has doubled, literacy, electrification, a health system, monasteries rebuilt at state expense with the state's lamas in charge. On the other side: a religion whose highest independent figures are either in exile or under state control, a succession line with a missing child, a language that is nominally but not actually the language of instruction in much of the school system, a registered urban unemployment rate that Chinese statistics hold under 5 per cent and that nonetheless measures a state-defined category rather than the actual availability of work to a Tibetan graduate, and 157 people who have burned themselves. The columns are not reconcilable into a single sentence and you should refuse any account that offers one. Both are real. The gap between them is the policy.

The language. Transmission requires a language and the language is the first thing that goes.

Tibetan is a Tibetic language in the Sino-Tibetan family and it is not mutually intelligible with any Chinese language, any more than English is with Russian. It has its own script, which is an abugida descended from a Brahmi model and which encodes the historical phonology of the language in a way that makes it one of the few writing systems anywhere that lets you reconstruct what a text sounded like when it was written. Written Tibetan is extraordinarily conservative. A document from the eighth century is largely legible to a reader today, which is why the old translations are still teachable and why the Tibetan commentarial tradition has been able to read its own foundation without a Rosetta Stone.

The spoken language is a different matter. Central Tibetan, the Lhasa dialect, is one variety of a cluster. Tibetan as spoken across the plateau includes three major regional groups, Ü-Tsang in central Tibet, Khams in the east, and Amdo in the northeast. Within those there are multiple varieties and no small number of local tongues. Ethnologue and Glottolog register the Tibetan languages as a family with substantial internal diversity, and a field linguist working in the region will describe dialects that are as different from each other as Portuguese is from Spanish.

This matters for transmission in a way that has nothing to do with linguistics. The Gelug scholastic curriculum was built around a specific liturgical and scholastic register of Tibetan, a classical written Tibetan that is essentially a scholarly lingua franca for the whole tradition, and that register is taught across the plateau. But the ordinary speech of a teacher in eastern Kham and the ordinary speech of a teacher in Lhasa are not the same, and a student who learned to debate in one does not automatically understand a debate in the other. The teaching transmission crossed those boundaries for a thousand years and it did so because the monks travelled and because the classical register travelled with them.

Tibetan under Chinese administration is the official language of the Tibet Autonomous Region and it is the medium of the school system, and at the same time it is systematically displaced. The most robust fact in the field is not a statistic but a pattern: in Tibetan areas of the autonomous region and in the schools of eastern Tibet, a large share of instruction at the levels where a monk would actually be taught to read a sutra has for years been delivered in Putonghua, the standard form of Mandarin, by non-Tibetan teachers, with Tibetan nominally the language of instruction and the classroom reality something else. Language is the transmission channel and the channel has been partly rerouted.

The scholar who wants to read the Tengyur has a serious problem, and it is not that the text is unavailable. It is that reading the Tengyur requires a classical written Tibetan that takes years to acquire in a full-time monastic education and a decade to acquire at all, and that the people who have that training are overwhelmingly monks, and that the number of monks in Tibet fell from 110,000 to around 2,000 in a period of years. A tradition of 110,000 readers of its own commentaries cannot be reconstructed by a few hundred people working on a grant.

This is where the archive, and the diaspora, and the West, and the story of how the West came to be interested in Tibetan Buddhism, all connect, and it is the last thing to build.

The transmission engine. The diaspora is a hard problem and it is a hard problem because the system depends on geography.

The Kamikaze is the wrong metaphor. The Tibetan situation after 1959 is not a dispersal of a population from a homeland, it is a population removed from the terrain on which its entire institutional logic was designed to operate. Think about what a monastery is in this context. A Tibetan monastery is not a church, it is a university, a residence, a printing works, a library, a village, a workplace, a school of debate and a retirement home, all of it under one roof, and the whole institution runs on the presence of the teachers and the students in physical proximity over years. The transmission of mind-to-mind teaching, in particular, requires a teacher and a student who have been in close physical association for years, and the tradition has specific technical terms for the closeness and the duration. A lineage is not a set of books that travel. It is a chain of people who lived near each other.

In exile, the chains can be kept alive but the terrain cannot. What actually happened is a specific and describable set of institutional facts. Monasteries were rebuilt in India, in Karnataka at Bylakuppe and Mundgod, in northern India at Dharamsala, in Nepal, in Sikkim. Full monastic education with debate was resumed, and it is still running, and it produces geshe after geshe, and this is a real and underreported achievement. The Tibetan Children’s Villages were founded in 1964, first under Tsering Dolma, the Dalai Lama’s elder sister, who died in November of that year, and then under Jetsun Pema, his younger sister, who led them until 2006, and they still educate Tibetan children in Tibetan, which is the single most effective transmission tool the exile community built, because it puts the language in a child before the child has decided what to think. The Central Tibetan Administration was reconstituted and has governed the exile community for six decades. The Nyingma school reestablished its college at Bylakuppe. And the physical holdings of the tradition, the libraries and the woodblocks, were moved west and digitised.

Here is what is genuinely lost.

The terrain is gone. The agricultural settlements that supported the monasteries, the villages that produced the food for a thousand monks in a valley in Kham, are not recoverable, and the exile villages in India are on land that belongs to other people or to the Indian state, and are settlements of refugees, not of a population that has lived there for four hundred years.

The teachers who died between 1959 and the 1990s are not replaceable by appointment. A khenpo in the Gelug system is someone who has completed a specific and gruelling course of study under a specific teacher over roughly thirteen years. When that teacher dies, the student who studied with them may become the teacher, and if that student also dies the specific transmission can be attenuated even if the lineage is nominally intact. The page's own list of achievement titles, khenpo, khenmo, geshe, geshema, rinpoche, lama, tertön, terton, is a list of exactly these qualifications, and each one is a pointer to a specific human being who holds a specific authorisation.

The place of the tertön, the treasure-finder, is the clearest case. In the Nyingma tradition a tertön is a person who discovers hidden texts, terma, and the entire Nyingma transmission runs through them. Terma practice is not historical; it continued into the twentieth century in exile, with figures like Khenpo Tashi Mannox working in India and India’s knowledge of the tradition in particular, and it continues inside Tibet under conditions that the practitioners have described in interviews. A terma text is held by a lineage, and the lineage is held by people who may be imprisoned. The Nyingma Gyubum on the page, the collection of tantras and terma, is a collection of texts that in a number of cases were not publicly available until a living master chose to release them and to authorise a specific translator.

And the deepest thing that is lost is the relationship between the text and the land, which the West has no way to reconstruct and does not know it is missing. The Tibetans are not a diaspora in the ordinary sense. They are the only large group on earth who are not displaced from a country but from the place itself. Every Tibetan religious institution of any age has been in Tibet, and the centre of gravity of the tradition is a set of mountains. Put the tradition somewhere else and it survives; you can read a text in any library. Put the tradition back on the mountain and it is a different thing.

And it is worth saying plainly, because this is where the scholarship is thin and the reticence is sometimes a courtesy. There is a persistent fringe of writing, on the page's list as Bon, hidden retreats, inner earth, portals, and in a great deal of writing that is neither fringe nor scholarly, which holds that the tradition is deliberately suppressed and that a hidden esoteric stream continues to be transmitted secretly. The clearest thing to say about this is that the framings differ enormously. The ordinary Tibetan claim is that the teachings were transmitted openly and that the people who receive them now are the inheritors of an open lineage. There are also, historically, real instances of restricted or secret transmission, and the tantric teachings were understood to be given under conditions, and there were genuine persecution episodes in which the transmission was broken. The claim that a secret stream has continued uninterrupted is not established, and the level does not need it. If it were true it would still not change what a monastery is or what a teacher does, because the level is not about hidden knowledge. The level is about transmission, and transmission is a public and describable process, and it is happening in the open right now in Bylakuppe.

The coming of the lamas. The West did not arrive at this tradition recently and it did not arrive on its own.

There is a long scholarly line of Western interest, and the page's own reference to the Tibetan Book of the Dead, which is one of the great canonical items, points at it directly. The first great wave of Western interest in Tibetan Buddhism was academic and Christian missionary. The figure of Alexandra David-Néel, a Belgian-French explorer who travelled to Tibet in 1924 and wrote about a Tibet that no longer existed for her and that she had a clear interest in not finding, is the first great instance of the Western pattern, because she went looking for a tradition outside history and found the twentieth century instead. The Theosophical Society and its leaders, and later the German and Swiss academic tradition, built the scholarly apparatus through which the tradition was first read. Wylie transliteration, the system that lets a Latin-alphabet typewriter represent Tibetan script, was published by Turrell Wylie in a Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies article in 1951. That one paper is the reason a generation of Western Tibetologists could actually work with the texts, and it is the sort of technical labour that this level should respect, because without it the archive is a closed book.

The second wave came in the 1960s and 1970s, and it came through three different doors.

The first door is the Dharma. A small number of Western students went to India and then to Nepal in the late 1960s and early 1970s and asked for teaching. The story of Thomas Merton, a Trappist monk who met the Dalai Lama in Dharamsala in November 1968 during three lengthy meetings across eight days, and whose Asian Journals record the journey, is part of this. The story of Lama Anagarika Govinda, born Ernst Lothar Hoffmann, a German writer and Buddhist who had already been travelling in the 1930s and who founded the Arya Maitreya Mandala in 1933, is part of this. The Kopan Monastery in Nepal, founded by Thubten Zopa Rinpoche, the Kopan nunnery, and the Foundation for the Preservation of the Mahayana Tradition, founded by Lama Yeshe and Thubten Zopa in 1975, are the institutional expression of that first door. The FPMT grew into a network of more than a hundred centres in dozens of countries. This is real and it did real work, and it was, for its founders, explicitly an act of preservation aimed at a tradition they believed was being destroyed.

The second door is Tibet as a geopolitical cause. After 1959 there were about 80,000 refugees, the Dalai Lama spoke to the world in English, and by the 1970s and 1980s the question of Tibet had entered Western politics. The US Congress passed the Tibetan Policy Act and successive versions of a Tibet support statute, the International Campaign for Tibet was founded in 1988 in New York, and the Tibet issue was repeatedly raised in the UN General Assembly. This door produced an enormous amount of Western interest in Tibet and almost no understanding of Tibetan Buddhism, because the interest was in a political grievance and the grievance was legible and the religion was not.

The third door is the charismatic teacher, and this is the door that everybody in the West has walked through. The Fourteenth Dalai Lama, who has been remarkably skilful at this, conducted his first Kalachakra initiation in the West in Madison, Wisconsin, in July 1981, with around 1,500 people present, at the invitation of Geshe Sopa and the monks of Namgyal Monastery. He has given Kalachakra initiations in Los Angeles in 1989, in New York in 1991, in Barcelona in 1994, in Sydney in 1996, in Bloomington, Indiana, in 1999, in Graz in 2002, in Toronto in 2004, and in Washington, DC, in 2011, among many others, with attendance figures running from a few thousand in the West to two hundred thousand at Bodhgaya. A Kalachakra empowerment is a tantric initiation of the highest and most restricted kind, it is preceded by days of public lam rim teaching, and it is the single most significant thing that has happened to Tibetan Buddhism in the West in the last fifty years, and it has happened dozens of times.

Whether it has been good for the tradition is genuinely arguable, and the argument is worth having rather than skipping.

The trouble with the third door. The instrumentality is the problem, and it is not a moral failing. It is a structural one.

A charismatic teacher who arrives in a large city for a short visit, with no time to teach anybody anything, gives something that is close to a conference keynote. The Kalachakra empowerment is not a teaching. It is an initiation given to a mass assembly of thousands, many of whom have studied Tibetan Buddhism for a week. It is a real and, according to the tradition, genuinely transformative practice; it is also, from the point of view of transmission, an initiation without a curriculum behind it. The empowerment itself does not constitute teaching. The days of public teaching before it are broadcast, they are wonderful, and they are not the sustained decades-long relationship with a teacher that is the actual vehicle of the tradition.

And the effect on the West's picture of the tradition is enormous, because the West now has a picture of Tibetan Buddhism that was assembled by public relations. The teaching that people in the West actually received from these visits is a few days of lam rim, a large empowerment, and a very accurate account of Tibetan Buddhism as an extraordinary, beautiful, mysterious and ancient wisdom tradition, plus the extraordinary, beautiful, mysterious and ancient wisdom tradition’s most photogenic figure. That is a real transmission. It is also a transmission of the frame and not of the content.

Look at what the frame does to the specific material on the page. It reads the page’s list of four schools and sees diversity, and in fact the schools are the reason the tradition has survived, because there are four of them and each has a separate canon and a separate body of holders, and the redundancy is the survival mechanism. It reads the page's list of Kanjur and Tenjur and sees a library, and in fact a library is not the tradition; the transmission is the tradition; the library is the tradition's fossil record, which is what any tradition's scripture actually is, and no tradition is properly understood through its scripture alone.

Here is the misreading that costs the most, and it is a misreading of a term. The word that English renders as reincarnation, and that the page renders as the tulkus and the reincarnated lamas, is sprul sku in Tibetan, which translates as emanation body. It is not the Western idea of a soul leaving a body and entering a new one, and the Dalai Lama himself has spent a long public document explaining the difference, in a statement issued on 24 September 2011 at a meeting of the heads of the Tibetan spiritual traditions in Dharamsala.

The sprul sku is the third of the bodies of a Buddha, the form body that appears in time and space so that ordinary beings can encounter it. It divides three ways: the Supreme Emanation Body, exemplified by the historical Shakyamuni, who was born in the place he chose; the Artistic Emanation Body, which appears as craftsmen and artists; and the Incarnate Emanation Body, which appears as humans, as deities, as rivers, as bridges, as medicinal plants, as trees. The tulkus of Tibet fall under the third of these. And here is a passage worth more than any Western summary of the institution, from the Dalai Lama's own statement: among the Tulkus of Tibet there may be many who are truly qualified Incarnate Emanation Bodies of the Buddhas, but this does not necessarily apply to all of them. Among them there may be those who are reincarnations of superior bodhisattvas, bodhisattvas on the paths of accumulation and preparation, as well as masters who are evidently yet to enter these bodhisattva paths at all. The title of Tulku is given to reincarnate lamas on grounds of their resemblance, their name, or their history.

That is a much more interesting position than the one the West imagines, and it is the more dangerous one for the tradition. A system that is partly symbolic and partly political is harder to reform than one that is purely symbolic or purely political.

And this is where the West's interest has been most sincerely and most systematically wrong, and where the tradition itself has contributed to the confusion. The tulku system is the single most politically consequential institution in Tibetan Buddhism, and it is also the institution that carries the tradition's most damaging internal vulnerability, and the two facts are the same fact.

The tulku system as it operates in Tibet is an institution of dynastic succession in religious clothing. It is the mechanism by which a small number of families, identified by outsiders as the great tulku families, have held monastic authority for centuries, and it is the single largest target of the charge of clericalism and feudalism that the Chinese state has levelled against Tibetan Buddhism. And the charge is not invented. It has a factual basis in the structure of the institution, and the tradition's own sophisticated defenders of it have been reluctant to engage that basis directly. The Dalai Lama's own 2011 statement makes the concession in the passage quoted above: some holders of the title have not yet entered even the bodhisattva paths. He wrote that, and then continued to use the system, and the continuation is not hypocritical. It is a decision made in the knowledge of the weakness, which is a harder and rarer thing than pretending the weakness does not exist.

Now watch what he actually did about it, because this is the part the West missed.

In the same statement of 24 September 2011, he opened by declaring that he had voluntarily ended the dual political and spiritual authority of the Dalai Lama, a system established by the Fifth Dalai Lama in 1642 which had lasted 369 years, and he called the ending of it something he was proud of and satisfied by, because it allowed Tibetans to pursue a democratic form of government. He explained the two kinds of rebirth, the involuntary rebirth propelled by karma and destructive emotions, which is how ordinary beings circle endlessly like a turning wheel, and the rebirth of superior bodhisattvas who, having attained the path of seeing, are not reborn by the force of their karma but choose their time, their place and their parents out of compassion. He set out four logical arguments from Buddhist philosophy for the reality of past and future lives, and observed that denying rebirth on the ground that we cannot see it is not an honest and impartial way of doing research, and that the Tibetan system of recognising reincarnations is an authentic mode of investigation based on people's recollection of their past lives.

Then he went after the state. He recorded that the Priest-Patron relationship between Tibet and China had already come to an end by the time of the Fourteenth Dalai Lama's enthronement in 1939, that there was no question of any need to confirm a reincarnation by the Golden Urn, and that the Tibetan regent and National Assembly had followed the traditional procedure with predictions of high lamas, oracles and visions at Lhamoi Latso, with no Chinese involvement. He noted that Chinese officials of the Kuomintang had later spread newspaper lies claiming the Chinese had agreed to forgo the Golden Urn, and that the lie was exposed by Ngabo Ngawang Jigme in 1989. And then he named the present: the re-education campaign, and Order No. Five concerning the control and recognition of reincarnations, which came into force on 1 September 2007, which he called outrageous and disgraceful. He wrote that the enforcement of inappropriate methods for recognising reincarnations to erase Tibet's cultural traditions is doing damage that will be difficult to repair, and that the Chinese authorities have a detailed strategy of deception and that they say they are waiting for his death and will recognise a Fifteenth Dalai Lama of their choice.

He declared that the person who reincarnates has sole legitimate authority over where and how they take rebirth and how that rebirth is to be recognised, that no one else can force or manipulate the person concerned, and that it is particularly inappropriate for a government which explicitly rejects even the idea of past and future lives to meddle in the system. He said that if this continues it will be impossible for Tibetans to accept any such candidate. He set the age of ninety as the point at which he would consult the high lamas, the Tibetan public and others to re-evaluate whether the institution should continue. And he left clear written instructions, assigning responsibility for any future recognition exclusively to the Gaden Phodrang Trust, to be conducted with the heads of the Tibetan Buddhist traditions and the oath-bound Dharma Protectors, in accordance with past tradition, with no recognition or acceptance to be given to any candidate chosen for political ends by anyone including the government of the People's Republic of China.

And then, on 21 May 2025, published on 2 July, he reversed himself. He affirmed that the institution of the Dalai Lama will continue. His stated reason is that over fourteen years the leaders of Tibet's spiritual traditions, members of the Tibetan Parliament in Exile, the Central Tibetan Administration, NGOs, Buddhists from Mongolia, from the Himalayan region, from the Buddhist republics of the Russian Federation and from Asia including mainland China, and Tibetans inside Tibet writing to him through various channels, had earnestly requested that the institution continue. He reaffirmed the 2011 process and repeated that the Gaden Phodrang Trust has sole authority and that no one else has any authority to interfere.

Both statements are authentic and both are on his own website. The sequence is the whole point. A man who spent fourteen years being asked by the entire apparatus of his government, and by the exiled community that depends on that government, to keep his office, examined the question in public, and concluded that the office should continue, and said so, in the same body of writing that had called the tradition's own warrant into question. That is what a lineage looks like from inside. It is not a mystic holding a mystery. It is a man with an arguable view of his own office, making a decision under real pressure from people with a genuine interest in the answer, and then living with it and issuing it publicly.

The Western picture of the Dalai Lama as the timeless spiritual leader, the last representative of an ancient wisdom, is not what he has actually been doing for the last fifty years. He has been a political leader who voluntarily retired, a religious teacher who declined to appoint his own successor, and a man who has argued in print about whether his own title should exist at all. The fact that the West found this confusing rather than moving is itself a finding, and it belongs with the catalogue of things the West got wrong about this tradition because it arrived for the wrong reason.

The lesson for the third door is not to refuse it. Many people went through the third door and came out the other side into a real and demanding practice. The lesson is to know which door you came through. The third door gives you an image. The image is not wrong, but the image is not a transmission, and the tradition was built by people who knew exactly the difference, and the reason the tradition has a Lamrim, a debate system, a thirteen-year degree and a memorisation requirement of astonishing scale is that they did not believe the image was enough.

The most honest summary of the situation is this. The West has done for Tibetan Buddhism in the last forty years what the twentieth century did for a great deal of Asia, which is to consume its surface and call it understanding. That consumption has money behind it, and the money has done real things: it has paid for the digitisation of the canon, it has paid for the translation of texts that would not otherwise have been translated, it has paid for the printing of the teachings in languages they had never been printed in, and it has funded the education of Tibetan children in India. That is not nothing. But the direction of the relationship is worth being honest about, because a tradition is a fire and you cannot warm your hands at it in a way that keeps it burning, and the people who arrive in the West believing that the transmission is a resource have not yet noticed the difference between the two.

The hidden retreats. The level page lists its objects and you should take the list seriously, because the list is the actual curriculum. It lists Shangri-La. It lists hidden retreats. It lists the Yeti. It lists inner earth. It lists Agartha and portals and time-space distortion fields. It lists Mt Kailash, sherpas and a master on the snowy mountain. And it lists Bon.

Some of these are documented. Some are inventions with a documented invention history. The level is better served by telling you which is which than by playing along, and the exercise turns out to teach the same thing as everything else in this chapter.

Shangri-La is a fictional place in the Kunlun mountains, described by the British author James Hilton in the 1933 novel Lost Horizon, where a lamasery preserves a harmonious civilisation untouched by history. It became a geopolitical word during the Second World War, when American aircraft named a mountain base in the Philippines Shangri-La, and Franklin Roosevelt used the name publicly. A real place in Yunnan province, Zhongdian, renamed itself Shangri-La in 2001 for the tourist trade. The word is entirely a Western literary invention with an Eastern setting, and its central claim, that somewhere there is a settlement that has preserved wisdom while history continued elsewhere, is precisely the structure of the story you have been carrying into this level. It is worth noticing that the level page lists it under Tibet without marking it as a novel.

Agartha, the underground kingdom ruled by a King of the World who secretly influences events on the surface, belongs to a different family. The hollow earth had serious nineteenth-century advocates, Edmond Halley among them, and an American, John Cleves Symmes, issued circulars from 1818 arguing that the earth is hollow and habitable within, with openings at the poles. The Russian writer Ferdynand Ossendowski's 1936 book, translated with a title describing an underground kingdom, carried the idea into the twentieth century, and most later versions, including the cryptoterrestrial hypothesis, descend from it. None of this has geological support. The Earth's mantle is not hollow and the crust is not a shell. But the idea is worth one moment's attention for the same reason the fantasy of hidden retreats is: it is a structure for the thought that the real knowledge has been taken away from us by someone, and that it can be recovered.

The Yeti is the interesting case, because it is the one item on the list where the honest answer is that the question has been tested and the answer is duller and stranger than the legend. Hair and other samples attributed to the yeti have been analysed repeatedly. A 2014 paper matched claimed yeti samples to polar bear DNA and was widely reported as a sensational result; that identification was challenged immediately by other geneticists, who pointed out that degraded ancient hair can align spuriously with modern sequences and that the samples were too degraded to support the claim either way. A later, better-resourced analysis of purported yeti material sequenced the mitochondrial genomes of Himalayan brown bears, Ursus arctos isabellinus, and Himalayan black bears, Ursus thibetanus laniger, and found that the purported yeti samples fell within the genetic diversity of Himalayan brown bears. The conclusion of that work is not that the yeti is a hoax. It is that Himalayan bear legends and Himalayan bears are the same animal, that the legend is a real and rich product of human encounter with a rare and shy bear, and that the bear is a distinct and little-studied population. This is the pattern the level should end on. The thing in the mountains was real. It was just an animal, and finding out did not make it less interesting.

Kailash is neither a mystery nor a fantasy, and it deserves to be protected from both readings. The mountain is sacred to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and followers of Bon, which is why four traditions lay claim to circumambulation. It rises to about 6,638 metres in the Ngari prefecture of western Tibet. It has been climbed, and there are established routes, and the climbing is difficult and dangerous and is not the point. The point is the kora, the circumambulation, which pilgrims have walked for centuries and which many still walk. The page lists sherpas, and the sherpas are real and among the most skilled high-altitude mountaineers in the world, and their relationship to the mountain is professional and also devotional. What is not the case is that the mountain is a portal, that it hides an entrance to inner earth, or that it is guarded by giants. It is a mountain. People go there. Some of them are doing the hardest and best thing in this whole chapter, which is walking a long way around something for no payoff except the walking.

Bon is the one item on the page's list that names an actual living tradition, and the page's treatment of it, three lines under a list of four Buddhist schools, understates it substantially. Bon predates the introduction of Buddhism to Tibet and is the older religious tradition there. It has its own canon, the Bon Kangyur and Bon Tengyur, its own founder figure in Tonpa Shenrab in the seventh century, its own monastic universities of which Menri is the largest, its own ritual life including the charnel grounds that tourists find disturbing and find instructive at the same time, and its own lineage holders who have carried the tradition out of Tibet and are transmitting it today. It is not the leftover. It is a rival, and it is the tradition that was actually established in Tibet before the arrival of the Buddhist schools that spent the next seven centuries arguing with it.

The line between the four things on this list is worth drawing carefully, because getting it wrong produces two opposite errors. One error is credulity, in which the reader takes Shangri-La and Agartha as locations and treats the story as suppressed fact. The other error is the confident debunk, in which the reader dismisses the whole list and in the process dismisses Bon, which is a religion with millions of adherents in Tibet and Nepal and Bhutan and an unbroken transmission, and Kailash, which is the site of one of the world's great ongoing pilgrimages. What the list actually shows, when you take it apart honestly, is a Western appetite for the hidden that has been running since Lost Horizon and has attached itself to a real and complex region, and has in the process learned a great deal about the region's actual religions from people who are in no position to say no to a paying visitor.

The tradition you have been reading about for the last several sections has one further property that none of the fantasy items share. It has never needed a hidden valley. It has worked for thirteen centuries in the open, in buildings you can visit, in a curriculum you can watch, in examinations you can sit in on, in libraries you can walk through, in a dispute system that depends on an opponent who is there to win. The Tibetan case is the one to sit with. Here is a tradition with an extraordinary body of practice and an extraordinarily well-documented history of being destroyed, and no hidden valley anywhere. What happened was ordinary. It was armies, famine, labour camps, a language policy, a succession procedure, and an economy that moved people off the plateau. That is what it looked like, and it is enough to fill a level, and the level does not require a portal.

The level's true subject. The level called Tibet is, underneath its list of magic objects, about something much smaller and much harder than inner earth or the caves beneath Mount Kailash. It is about the difference between information and transmission, and it is about what happens to a real human capacity when the soil it grows in is removed.

Information is cheap and it is copying. The Kangyur and the Tengyur, a quarter of a million pages, are being digitised and made freely available and translated into every major language, and this is a genuinely magnificent project and it is exactly what a copy is. Transmission is a chain of people, and a chain of people requires a place, and a place requires that the people can live there and that there are enough of them and that the institutions which need wood, food, buildings and teachers are physically intact.

The Tibetan case is the clearest natural experiment ever run on whether a tradition can survive its own displacement, and the results are mixed and should be given honestly. The texts have been saved beyond anything the founders of the archives expected. The teaching has been maintained in exile in India, in Nepal, in Sikkim, in the United States, in Australia, in Europe, at a level of scholarly and contemplative seriousness that would have astonished any of the teachers who left in 1959. The Children's Villages put Tibetan into the mouths of children who would otherwise never have spoken it. And the tradition's own global presence, which would have been unthinkable in 1959, is now real, with hundreds of study groups, dozens of teachers with international followings, and a canon that is free to read.

And it has also been consumed, flattened, and turned into a series of brands, in a process that its own best Western teachers have described in the harshest terms available to them, in interviews and in writing that has not always been welcomed. And it has been, and remains, subject to an occupation whose stated goals include the education of children in the language of the occupying power, whose internal documents discuss the replacement of Tibetan Buddhist authority, and whose practice has included the disappearance of a six-year-old boy who was the second-ranking religious figure in his tradition.

Neither of these is a conspiracy theory. Both are the documented record. What the conspiracy reading does is fuse them into a single hidden hand, and what that fusion costs is precisely the specificity of the actual situation, which is much more interesting than a conspiracy and much worse for anyone inclined to comfort.

You will be tempted, at this point, to do what the level is designed to make you do, which is to go looking for a master. Here is what you have learned that bears on it. A master in this tradition is not a person with a secret. A master is a person with a specific authorisation, held through a specific chain of teaching going back to a historical Buddha, who has spent decades acquiring it and who is judged by the same examination system as everybody else. In the Gelug schools the highest qualification is a geshe, and a geshe has sat a twenty-year examination. In the Nyingma school the qualification is different and the path is a tertön, and a tertön has spent years discovering and validating hidden texts. In the Kagyu and Sakya schools the qualification is again different and the lineage is the qualification.

The master the level promises you is a structure, not a personality. The level has already told you the mechanic who sends you there. The mechanic is the lineage.

And that is the level's actual teaching. The reason a text can outlive the person who practised it is that the text is embedded in a structure that is bigger than any person and designed to replace them. The reason a teacher is worth anything is that they are a link in a chain they did not start and will not finish. The reason the Dalai Lama can be right about the abolition of his own office is that the tradition is not him. And the reason to take this seriously, in a century when a great many people are looking for something to believe and are willing to believe the first thing that comes along with a mountain behind it, is that this is a documented, functioning, imperfect, and still-living example of the thing they are looking for, and it is being damaged while you read about it.

The restoration the project speaks of, to awaken, liberate, reconstruct, and restore, is not a metaphor here. The reconstruction being done at the Tibetan Buddhist Resource Center and the Buddhist Digital Resource Center, the digitisation, the translation, the printing, the teaching, the children's villages, the debate courtyards still running in Bylakuppe, is a real reconstruction being carried out by real people with real budgets. And so is the political project, which is much harder and much slower and has a worse funding position. Both of them need the thing the level has been training you to notice, which is that the thing that is being hidden here is not a secret. It is a method. It is a way of paying attention that has been carried for thirteen centuries by people who were not important, in buildings that were not beautiful, in a language that most of the people who used it could not read, and it can be picked up by anyone who is willing to do the work, and it will not survive you either if you do not hand it on.

Go back to the map. You have the travel, the culture, the costume, the food, the people, the music, the language, the mountains, the monasteries, the lamas. You have the four schools and their canons. You have the lineage and the record. Now the next level is the master, and you will meet him, and he will not be what you brought.

One last thing, and it is the only instruction that matters. The reason the level says the master plays the fool and gets drunk is that he has to. A teacher in this tradition has, as part of the job, to refuse to function as a mystery. If he seems profound and remote and slightly supernatural, you will believe that. He is not going to let you. He will be rude and ordinary and will not take you seriously, and the reason he will do this is that the tradition he holds has spent thirteen centuries developing a defence against exactly your enthusiasm, and it has never needed a better one than an uninteresting old person who will not perform.
