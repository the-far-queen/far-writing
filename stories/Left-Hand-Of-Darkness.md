I will make my report as if I told a story, for I was taught as a child on my homeworld that Truth is a matter of the imagination. The soundest fact may fail or prevail in the style of its telling: like that singular organic jewel of our seas, which grows brighter as one woman wears it and, worn by another, dulls and goes to dust. Facts are no more solid, coherent, round, and real than pearls are. But both are sensitive.

The story is not all mine, nor told by me alone. Indeed I am not sure whose story it is; you can judge better. But it is all one, and if at moments the facts seem to alter with an altered voice, why then you can choose the fact that you like best; yet none of them are false, and it is all one story.

It starts on the forty-fourth day of spring in the Year One, in Erhenrang, the capital city of Karhide, and I was in peril of my life and did not know it.

I was in a parade. I walked just behind the gossiwors and just before the king. It was raining.

Rainclouds over dark towers, rain falling in deep streets, a dark storm-beaten city of stone, through which one vein of gold winds slowly. First come the merchants, potentates and artisans of Erhenrang, rank after rank, magnificently clothed, advancing through the rain as comfortably as fish through the sea. Their faces are keen and calm. They do not march in step. This is a parade with no soldiers, not even imitation soldiers.

Next come the lords and mayors and representatives, one person or five or forty-five or four hundred from each Domain and Co-Domain of Karhide, a vast ornate procession moving to the music of metal horns and hollow blocks of bone and wood and the dry pure lilting of electric flutes. The banners of the great Domains tangle in a rain-beaten confusion of color with the yellow pennants that bedeck the way, and the musics of each group clash and interweave in many rhythms echoing in the deep stone street.

Next, a troop of jugglers with polished spheres of gold, which they hurl up high in flashing flights and catch and hurl again, making fountain-jets of bright jugglery. All at once, as if they had literally caught the light, the gold spheres blaze bright as glass: the sun is breaking through.

Next, forty men in yellow playing gossiwors. The gossiwor, played only in the king's presence, produces a preposterous disconsolate bellow. Forty of them played together shake one's reason, shake the towers of Erhenrang, shake down a last spatter of rain from the windy clouds. If this is the royal music, no wonder the kings of Karhide are all mad.

Next the royal party: guards and functionaries and dignitaries of the city and the court, deputies, senators, chancellors, ambassadors, lords of the Kingdom, none of them keeping step or rank yet walking with great dignity; and among them King Argaven XV, in white tunic and shirt and breeches, with leggings of saffron leather and a peaked yellow cap. A gold finger-ring is his only adornment and sign of office. Behind this group eight sturdy fellows bear the royal litter, rough with yellow sapphires, in which no king has ridden for centuries, a ceremonial relic of the very-long-ago. By the litter walk eight guards armed with foray guns, also relics of a more barbaric past but not empty ones, being loaded with pellets of soft iron. Death walks behind the king.

We gather on a platform of new timbers beside the unfinished Arch of the River Gate. The occasion is the completion of that arch, which completes the new Road and River Port of Erhenrang, a great operation of dredging and building and roadmaking that has taken five years and will distinguish Argaven XV's reign in the annals of Karhide. We are all squeezed rather tight on the platform in our damp and massive finery. The rain is gone, the sun shines on us, the splendid, radiant, traitorous sun of Winter. I remark to the person on my left, "It's hot. It's really hot."

The person on my left, a stocky dark Karhider with sleek and heavy hair, wearing a heavy overtunic of green leather worked with gold, and a heavy white shirt, and heavy breeches, and a neck-chain of heavy silver links a hand broad, this person, sweating heavily, replies, "So it is."

All about us as we stand jammed on our platform lie the faces of the people of the city, upturned like a shoal of brown round pebbles, mica-glittering with thousands of watching eyes.

Now the king ascends a gangplank of raw timbers that leads from the platform up to the top of the arch whose unjoined piers tower over crowd and wharves and river. As he mounts, the crowd stirs and speaks in a vast murmur: "Argaven!" He makes no response. They expect none. Gossiwors blow a thunderous discordant blast and cease. Silence. The sun shines on city, river, crowd, and king.

Masons below have set an electric winch going, and as the king mounts higher the keystone of the arch goes up past him in its sling, is raised, settled, and fitted almost soundlessly, a great ton-weight block though it is, into the gap between the two piers, making them one thing, an arch. A mason with trowel and bucket awaits the king up on the scaffolding; all the other workmen descend by rope ladders, like a swarm of fleas. The king and the mason kneel, high between the river and the sun, on their bit of planking. Taking the trowel the king begins to mortar the long joints of the keystone. He does not dab at it and give the trowel back to the mason, but sets to work methodically.

The cement he uses is a pinkish color different from the rest of the mortarwork, and after five or ten minutes of watching the king-bee work I ask the person on my left, "Are your keystones always set in a red cement?" For the same color is plain around the keystone of each arch of the Old Bridge, that soars beautifully over the river upstream from the arch.

Wiping sweat from his dark forehead, the man answers, "Very-long-ago a keystone was always set in with a mortar of ground bones mixed with blood. Human bones, human blood. Without the blood-bond the arch would fall, you see. We use the blood of animals, these days."

So he often speaks, frank yet cautious, ironic, as if always aware that I see and judge as an alien: a singular awareness in one of so isolate a race and so high a rank. He is one of the most powerful men in the country; I am not sure of the proper historical equivalent of his position, vizier or prime minister or councillor; the Karhidish word for it means the King's Ear. He is lord of a Domain and lord of the Kingdom, a mover of great events. His name is Therem Harth rem ir Estraven.

The king seems to be finished with his masonry work, and I rejoice; but crossing under the rise of the arch on his spiderweb of planks he starts in on the other side of the keystone, which after all has two sides. It doesn't do to be impatient in Karhide. They are anything but a phlegmatic people, yet they are obdurate, they are pertinacious, they finish plastering joints.

I am dressed for the Ice Age and not for the sunshine, in layers and layers of clothing, woven plant-fiber, artificial fiber, fur, leather, a massive armor against the cold, within which I now wilt like a radish leaf. For distraction I look at the crowds and the other paraders drawn up around the platform, their Domain and Clan banners hanging still and bright in sunlight, and idly I ask Estraven what this banner is and that one and the other. He knows each one I ask about, though there are hundreds, some from remote domains, hearths and tribelets of the Pering Storm-border and Kerm Land.

"I'm from Kerm Land myself," he says when I admire his knowledge. "Anyhow it's my business to know the Domains. They are Karhide. To govern this land is to govern its lords. Not that it's ever been done. Do you know the saying, Karhide is not a nation but a family quarrel?" I haven't, and suspect that Estraven made it up; it has his stamp.

At this point another member of the kyorremy, the upper chamber or parliament which Estraven heads, pushes and squeezes a way up close to him and begins talking to him. This is the king's cousin Pemmer Harge rem ir Tibe. His voice is very low as he speaks to Estraven, his posture faintly insolent, his smile frequent. Estraven, sweating like ice in the sun, stays slick and correct.

I have been in Karhide now three weeks, and I have already learned something of the two great men of the country. I do not yet understand them, but I think I am beginning to see the shape of the difficulty.

Here is what I had been sent to do. I am the First Mobile of the Ekumen, which is to say the first envoy to arrive openly and stay. The Ekumen is a coalition of some eighty worlds of human beings; it is not a government but a co-ordinator, a clearinghouse for trade and knowledge. Without it, communication between the worlds of men would be haphazard and trade very risky, as you can see. Men's lives are too short to cope with the time-jumps between worlds. It is not enough to send messages; men must meet. We offer trade of goods and, more valuable, exchange of ideas and techniques. We are not a kingdom. We do not want to govern anyone. We want only to be allowed in, to be useful, and to have the chance to be understood.

It should have been an easy task. Over forty years ago a team of our Investigators came to Gethen. They did not announce their presence. They passed as well as they could for Gethenians and travelled about in Karhide, Orgoreyn and the Archipelago for a year. They left, and reported to the Councils of the Ekumen during your grandfather's reign. Their report was extremely favorable. I studied the information they had gathered and the languages they had recorded, and came.

What I did not have was any idea of what they had actually encountered. A report is a set of conclusions. What I needed was an encounter.

You will understand, I think, if I say that the categories I arrived with failed me. There are two ways of being unknown: not yet known, and unknowable. Winter was the second. I carried a pair of labels in my head, and they were: human, non-human. That pair divides the world cleanly, it fits the Ekumen's purposes, and it is the pair that sends an envoy out from a hearth-world toward a place he assumes he will meet at last something recognizably like himself.

But the Gethenians were not recognizably like myself, and they were certainly not non-human. They were not a thing to be classified. They were people, and the more I tried to place them inside my two boxes, the more they refused to stay there. A report's categories are not a description of what is out there. They are a description of what the reporter can hold.

So the first thing I have to report is that the mission, as it was defined for me, could not be executed by the person it was defined for. That is not a complaint. It is a description of a fact, and the fact turned out to be the whole of what I learned.

I did not know this yet on the day of the parade. I knew only that the sun was hot, and that the man beside me was called Estraven, and that somewhere behind me in the procession walked a king who was mortaring a keystone with animal blood because a human could not be spared.

The audience with the king took Estraven six months to arrange.

I had wanted to make it sooner. The first days after the parade are blotted out for me now, a feverish confusion of impressions: the ship, the ansible, the little band of technicians who came down with me and are still up there in orbit waiting for my word; the fishing village on Horden Island where the fishermen took me in without the least surprise, asked no questions, and fed me; the two long transit weeks across the Kargav range in a road-packer with forty other people; the Ambassador of Karhide, a man named Asra, who received me at the border with great courtesy and total silence; and the long cold weeks of the Kargav itself, where I was sick, and where I began to understand that cold is not a temperature but a way of life.

The Ambassador told me on the road that the King was indisposed, and then that the King was in seclusion, and then that there was no time. These were all true, and together they constituted a policy. In Karhide there is a phrase for what was being done to me: shifgrethor. Prestige, face, place, the pride-relationship, the untranslatable and all-important principle of social authority. An outsider, an uninvited alien, must not be given the honor of a quick audience, because a quick audience is a courtesy, and a courtesy freely given establishes precedence, and precedence is a form of power. So I must be kept in the dark, in the antechamber, in the outer room, and must be given nothing that could be called an answer, and the whole business must be conducted by intermediaries who can be refused without loss of face.

I had been trained to deal with exactly this. What I had not been trained to deal with was what happened when I tried to deal with it.

I made my base in Gorinhering, a Clan-Hearth on the eastern coast, a house-town-farm built up on a hill above the eternal fogs of the Hodomin Ocean. Some five hundred people lived there. I lived in a room over the barn. Four thousand years ago I should have found their way of life quaint. Now I found that the sameness of it was like a wall. They were curious, some more, some less. Few were frightened of me personally, or showed the xenophobic revulsion. An enemy, in Karhide, is not a stranger, an invader. The stranger who comes unknown is a guest. Your enemy is your neighbor.

I came to the conclusion of that first winter in a state of considerable despair. Nothing I had been taught prepared me for the fact that no one was trying to deceive me. That was the difficulty. I had assumed, as a reasonable assumption, that the Gethenians were withholding something, or concealing a position, or being cagey in the way that states are cagey. I went looking for the hidden hand behind the curtain. There wasn't one.

There was only a man who could not be got to talk about the thing he thought most important, because to talk about it would be to say that a king and a kingdom were wrong, and to say that is a crime.

That was Estraven.

Our talks took place in his office in Erhenrang, on certain afternoons, when the kyorremy was not sitting. He was not an easy man to be with. He had a very quick mind, and a way of deflecting questions that I at first mistook for evasion and later understood to be a kind of precision. He answered the question that was asked, and not the one behind it, and he did it without discourtesy. Twice I thought I had caught him in an evasion and pressed him, and twice he waited until I had finished, and then said, "You are asking the wrong question," and gave me the right one, unasked, in three sentences, and the right one was more useful than anything I could have extracted by an hour of pressing.

I did not learn any of this quickly. I want to be honest about the pace. I spent the first year on Winter believing that I was being obstructed by a dull man, and it was only in the second year, in a city I was locked up in, that I understood that he had been telling me the truth the entire time, and that the truth was not about the mission.

The thing that has to be understood about Genly Ai, if he is to be understood at all, is that he was a good envoy. He was trained, he was sincere, he worked hard, and he believed in what he had come to do. It would be easy, and wrong, to make him a fool. He was not a fool. He was competent within the limits of a category he never thought to question, and he spent those limits like a man spending money he did not know was his own.

Let me set down what he actually believed on arrival, because everything after this is a matter of that belief being taken away from him piece by piece.

He believed that the Ekumen was a good thing. He believed that the Hainish cycle of worlds, ninety-odd inhabited worlds of human beings, had learned its lesson about isolation the hard way and had come out of it into a common culture. He believed that this was the greatest discovery his species had made, and that it was available to anyone who wanted it. He believed, and this is the load-bearing one, that men are made for exactly this: to meet, to trade, to understand. That the terrible difficulty of interspecies contact is a temporary problem of imperfect information, and that with effort, patience, and the right technique, it can be solved.

He carried, in his case, a large sum of rubies. The Investigators had reported that Gethenians value the carbon jewels much as Terrans do, and he had come with a pocketful of gems to pay his way. He was given no opportunity to spend them. I want to note that. He arrived with a bribe. He kept it for the entire mission. In the end it bought him nothing, and he never once offered it, and he never told anyone he had it, and when he finally left the planet he took it with him and it never entered his relations with anyone on Winter. I do not know what that means exactly. I know what it cost him to not use it.

He believed also that his host, the Ekumen, was listening. That the ansible would carry his report, and that the report would be read, and that men would read it, and that what he said would be weighed. He was not wrong about this. He was wrong about what being listened to means.

The first winter passed. I shall not describe the whole of it in the order in which I lived it, because I could not have put it in order at the time, and to put it in order now would be a different and more comfortable story than the one I have to tell.

I shall tell it as it came, with the interludes where the account jumps, which is the way I remember it and the only way it can honestly be told.

The first of the interludes is a tale. It is not mine, and it is not evidence, and I set it here because it was told to me and because it is what I think of when I think of what I did.

Long ago, before the days of King Argaven I who made Karhide one kingdom, there was blood feud between the Domain of Stok and the Domain of Estre in Kerm Land. The feud had been fought in forays and ambushes for three generations, and there was no settling it, for it was a dispute over land. Rich land is scarce in Kerm, and a Domain's pride is in the length of its borders, and the lords of Kerm Land are proud and umbrageous men, casting black shadows.

It chanced that the heir of the flesh of the Lord of Estre, a young man, skiing across Icefoot Lake in the month of Irrem hunting pesthry, came onto rotten ice and fell into the lake. Though by using one ski as a lever on a firmer ice-edge he pulled himself up out of the water at last, he was in almost as bad case out of the lake as in it, for he was drenched, the air was damp, and night was coming on. He saw no hope of reaching Estre eight miles away uphill, and so set off towards the village of Ebos on the north shore of the lake. As night fell the fog flowed down off the glacier and spread out all across the lake, so that he could not see his way, nor where to set his skis. Slowly he went for fear of rotten ice, yet in haste, because the cold was at his bones and before long he would not be able to move.

He saw at last a light before him in the night and fog. He cast off his skis, for the lakeshore was rough going and bare of snow in places. His legs would not well hold him up any more, and he struggled as best he could to the light. He was far astray from the way to Ebos. This was a small house set by itself in a forest of the thore-trees that are all the woods of Kerm Land, and they grew close all about the house and no taller than its roof. He beat at the door with his hands and called aloud, and one opened the door and brought him into firelight.

There was no one else there, only this one person alone. He took the clothes off him that were like clothes of iron with the ice, and put him naked between furs, and with the warmth of his own body drove out the frost from his feet and hands and face, and gave him hot ale to drink. At last the young man was recovered, and looked on the one who cared for him.

This was a stranger, young as himself. They looked at each other. Each of them was comely, strong of frame and fine of feature, straight and dark. Estraven saw that the fire of kemmer was in the face of the other.

He said, "I am Arek of Estre."

The other said, "I am Therem of Stok."

Then Estraven laughed, for he was still weak, and said, "Did you warm me back to life in order to kill me, Stokven?"

The other said, no.

He put out his hand and touched Estraven's hand, as if he were making certain that the frost was driven out. At the touch, though he was a day or two from his kemmer, he felt the fire waken in himself. So for a while both held still, their hands touching.

"They are the same," said Stokven, and laying his palm against Estraven's showed it was so: their hands were the same in length and form, finger by finger, matching like the two hands of one man laid palm to palm.

"I have never seen you before," Stokven said. "We are mortal enemies." He rose, and built up the fire in the hearth, and returned to sit by Estraven.

"We are mortal enemies," said Estraven. "I would swear kemmering with you."

"And I with you," said the other. Then they vowed kemmering to each other, and in Kerm Land then as now that vow of faithfulness is not to be broken, not to be replaced. That night, and the day that followed, and the night that followed, they spent in the hut in the forest by the frozen lake.

On the next morning a party of men from Stok came to the hut. One of them knew young Estraven by sight. He said no word and gave no warning but drew his knife, and there in Stokven's sight stabbed him in the throat and chest, and the young man fell across the cold hearth in his blood.

"He was the heir of Estre," Stokven said, "and I slew him. And I do not ask you to forgive me, nor ever to see me again."

Then Therem Harth rem ir Stokven went back across the lake to his own country, and they saw no more of each other. But Arek of Estre did not die unloved. For fourteen years afterward, the two of them kept faith, in their fashion, and neither ever took a lover.

This was not a story that explained anything to me when I heard it. It was a story about a land feud, and it did not concern the Ekumen. I set it down because it is the only way I have found to say what Estraven's story was, before he told it to me himself.

The second fact of that first year was physical, and it is the fact on which the whole of the misunderstanding rested.

The Gethenians have no fixed sex. This is not a matter of custom or costume or politics. It is a matter of their bodies. Their sexual physiology follows a cycle of about twenty-six days, the length of their month, and within that cycle a person's sex is not fixed. For most of the cycle they are in the somer phase, sexually inert, neither male nor female, their bodies flat and smooth and quiet. At the onset of a period of several days, they enter a phase in which they become, physically and in desire, male, or female, or at need neither; the sex is determined at that moment, it cannot change during the period, and it ends as abruptly as it began. A person may, on successive cycles, be male, female, or neither. A person may choose, in Orgoreyn and among the subcultists, to bias the odds with hormone derivatives. But the default is this: no permanent sex, no permanent gender, and therefore nothing in their society that is built on the distinction.

I was not prepared for this. I had read the Investigators' material. I had seen the diagrams of the cycle. I had the words. And none of it touched anything, because the words I had were neutral and the fact is not neutral at all.

The Gethenians are not neutered. They are not children. They are not non-human. They are people who live in a society where the distinction between men and women, on which my own world has built almost everything that matters — power, property, prestige, war, work, love, the family, the shape of every day — simply does not exist as a permanent fact about a person.

I want to be exact about the difficulty, because it is easy to state it wrongly. It is not that Gethenian men and women are hard for me to tell apart. They are not. That was a relief I did not expect and did not enjoy. It is not that they are mysterious. They are the most explicable people I have ever lived among: they have no taboo, so they have no lying; they are direct, they are funny, they are unembarrassed, and they will discuss the most intimate part of their lives with a stranger across a table, in the plainest possible terms, and enjoy it.

It is that they are not what I am. And that this was not a curiosity to be got used to, as I had confidently assumed it would be.

I had assumed that a difference in the body was a difference in the surface of a person, and that beneath the surface we would find the same furniture. I had assumed that the individual was the unit, and that the individual was a sexed thing, and that the sexes were two, and that there were therefore two sorts of people, and that the relations between the sorts were what history was about.

Gethen has none of that. On Gethen there is no sex-war. There has never been a sex-war. There is no division of labour by sex, no inheritance law by sex, no fornication because of sex, no resentment because of sex, no jealousy because of sex, no love because of sex. There is the whole complex of human motive, from which one enormous channel has simply been removed, and what is left has not collapsed. It has made a different kind of person. Not a worse person. Not a higher person. A different kind.

I could not see that then. I could only see that a category I had been using was not available.

The third fact of that first year is the one that finished me, and it is that Estraven, the Prime Minister of Karhide, the King's Ear, a lord of the Kingdom, a man of great weight in his country's politics, was a being whose sex I could not determine, and that I had no way to ask, and that there was no way for him to tell me except by going into kemmer in front of me, which is a thing you do not ask a person to do as a demonstration.

I had arrived carrying two labels, and I now find that I had also been carrying, unexamined, a whole apparatus for reading people that depended on those labels. I could read Estraven's rank, his authority, his competence, his humor, his loyalty, his patience. I could read those. I could not read what he was. And because I could not read what he was, I read everything else in a slightly distorted way, because a man whose central fact about himself I cannot see is a man I am continually mismeasuring.

I did not distrust him because he was a stranger. I distrusted him because he was unreadable, and I had no instrument for the unreadable except distrust.

That is the honest mechanism, and it is the one I have had to look hardest at, because it is not a noble mechanism and it does not feel like a failure at the time. It feels like prudence.

The misunderstanding began on a Tuesday afternoon in the second month of autumn, and I am going to describe it in some detail, because everything afterward depends on it.

We were in his office. I had been pressing him about the delay in the audience, and he had explained the politics of it again, and I had said something too pointed about the Ambassador's courier system, and he had been silent for a while.

"You've led me to rely on you," I said. "It was ill done."

"It was," he said. "Do you mean that, having arranged this audience, you haven't spoken in favor of my mission to the king, as you—"

I had the sense to stop short of the word promised.

"I can't," he said.

I was very angry, but I met neither anger nor apology in him. "Will you tell me why?"

After a while he said, "Yes," and then paused again. During the pause I began to think that an inept and undefended alien should not demand reasons from the prime minister of a kingdom, above all when he does not and perhaps never will understand the foundations of power and the workings of government in that kingdom. No doubt this was all a matter of shifgrethor: prestige, face, place, the pride-relationship. Doubtless the King was withholding, or was being used, or was genuinely hostile, and my inability to see the shape of the thing was my own fault, and I should stand on my own feet and be done with this man's evasions.

"Yes," I said, "I'll tell you why."

"It is not possible for me to advocate your mission to the king," Estraven said, "because your mission is opposed to the interests of my country. And because I have been asked by the King to tell you nothing of this. And because, Mr. Ai, you have no idea what you are doing here."

"I know what I am doing."

"Then you are the only man in Karhide who does," he said, "and I envy you."

I did not understand it then. I thought it was contempt.

There were, I think, three things in that sentence, and I want to set them out because it took me two years to find them.

There was the fact. My mission was opposed to the interests of his country. To invite Gethen into the Ekumen was to say that the isolation, the insularity, the long dark winter of a people who had survived by keeping to themselves, was a mistake. That a sovereign nation should not be sovereign. That Karhide should give up its borders to a clearinghouse of other worlds.

There was the political fact. The King had asked him to say nothing. Which meant the King knew all about it, which meant the King was not merely uninformed. Which meant the King was concealing.

And there was the third thing, which is the one I did not see. He knew what I was, and he knew I did not know what he was, and he knew that I could not understand the first fact until I understood the second thing about him, and that I would not be able to understand the second thing because I would be locked into my own category long before I got there.

He was telling me the truth in the only language available, and the language was not adequate to the truth.

I went back to Gorinhering that night and I did not sleep, and in the morning I told myself a story, and the story was this: Estraven has agreed with me privately and is afraid to say so publicly; he has been overruled by the King; he is a good man trapped in a bad position; if I can only reach the King directly, the truth will come out and my mission will be saved.

I went to Mishnory.

Mishnory is the capital of Orgoreyn, and Orgoreyn is the other nation on the Great Continent, and the two nations have hated each other for four hundred years over a strip of mountainous border called the Sinoth Valley. What I had heard about Orgoreyn was that it was a police state, and that it was centralized, and that it was much more like the industrial nations of my own history than Karhide was. All true. What I had not been told was that it was that, and that it was lying about it, and that the lying was not a matter of diplomacy but of everything.

On the train north across the Kargav I met a man who changed my life, and I did not know it at the time, and I found out about it four days later, in the worst possible way.

His name was Obsle. He was an Orgota Commensal, one of the Thirty-Three, and he treated me with an enormous courtesy that I later understood to be a technique. He gave me dinner. He talked about the future of his country with an enthusiasm that was almost physical, and he introduced me to Yegey and Slose and to the other men who were to be my hosts, and he was so open, so confiding, so evidently a man of goodwill, that within a few hours I had the impression that Orgoreyn was a country that had understood something the rest of humanity had not.

He had one question for me, and he asked it well.

"Do you know what you have here?" he said. "Do you know what you're holding in this box?"

I said I had the ansible.

"You have a machine," he said. "And behind the machine there is a set of ideas. And behind the ideas there is a purpose. And I know what the purpose is, Mr. Ai, because I have been reading your own reports for four years. The purpose is to make us join you. And the machine will do it, because the machine will do whatever it is told."

I said that the ansible was a transmitter.

"It's a transmitter," he agreed. "And I am a realist. If you wish to send a message, you may. Send it to your masters and tell them we are ready to discuss. Tell them we will sign what they like. Tell them we will do anything." He leaned forward. "But do not expect me to believe that the message is the message. Do not expect me to believe that a man who has come to my country with a device that can talk to another world has come to ask us a question."

Then he told me, quite mildly, why the last envoy had failed.

The last envoy, he said, was a man named Jaern. There had been an entire Ekumen mission before Genly Ai. It had been, by the standards of the Ekumen, a catastrophe, and by the standards of the planet, a humiliation, and both parties had drawn the obvious conclusion, which was that neither side was to blame and the whole business should be abandoned. Jaern had passed himself off as a Gethenian. He had been found out. The punishment in Orgoreyn had been, in the terms of the time, a public disgrace, and the man had been made to feel it.

Now, that account is wrong, and I know that it is wrong, and I set it here because it was the account that nearly finished me, and because a reader who has only this version will understand exactly what I believed for about three weeks.

The previous envoy was not one man, and his name was not Jaern, and he was not caught by anyone, and he was not exposed in Orgoreyn, and he did not die in disgrace. That account is the Orgota one, and it is a story about a man who tried to pass as a local and was punished for it. It is not what happened, and I have to be careful here, because I am the one who was deceived, and my error was not exactly the error Orgoreyn describes.

What happened is this. Before me there came a team of Ekumen Investigators, a small group, and they did not announce their presence. They passed as well as they could for Gethenians and travelled about for a year in Karhide and Orgoreyn and the Archipelago, gathering information and recording languages. They were not trying to govern anyone or to obtain anything. They were trying to find out what was here. Their report to the Councils of the Ekumen was, as I have said, extremely favorable, and it was accurate as far as it went.

But there was among them a man who went further. He had lived with Gethenians, and had learned to speak the language as a man rather than as a linguist, and had adopted the ways, and had taken a woman — a being in kemmer, and permanently thereafter in the manner of the Karhidish, as they kept faith — and had a child born on Winter. He did this believing, and I can only report what I was told much later, that the practice was not merely tolerated but honored in that country; that a person who had chosen to live as a Gethenian, and who had been taken into the Hearth of a Domain, was held to be as much a part of the family as one born there; and that he could not be got to leave.

He was not an outsider who had infiltrated. He was a man who had been adopted.

And when the Ekumen called him home, he did not come.

This is the fact I did not know for a year and a half, and it is the fact that everything else was hanging from. The whole of my confidence that this was a simple task of communication, and that the difficulty was local obstruction, rested on the belief that the earlier mission had failed because it had failed to do its job. It had not. It had succeeded too well. One of its members had done the one thing that is impossible to undo, and then had been unable to say so, and had been reported afterward — by the Ekumen, by the Council, by the other investigators, by everyone — as a man who had lost his nerve.

There is a term. On Winter they call it a durin. It is not an insult. It is what you call a man or woman who has gone native in a sense that cannot be explained or revoked — who has entered the society and cannot be got back out of it, who is known to be alien and has chosen to stay regardless. Karhide uses the word without cruelty. It is closer to a legal category than a judgment: the durin is a person who belongs here and is not of here, and the fact of the belonging is irreversible.

I had that word in my vocabulary from the first month. I had never once applied it to anybody. It was, as far as I was concerned, a word about a failure mode in the Other: a thing that happens when the outsider is assimilated, when the mission is lost, when the traveler stops being a traveler.

I had not considered that it might describe the mission's success.

I did not think about the previous envoy as an example of what not to do, because I thought about him as an example of what not to be. And so when I went on to the ice, I did not understand what I was looking at, and when I came back I could not say what it had cost.

I will come back to this. It is the central fact of this report and it is very badly placed in the story, because the story does not put it in the middle. The story puts it in the Ice.

But first: Orgoreyn, and the winter I nearly did not survive.

Obsle put me on a train and sent me to a man named Yegey who was a school administrator and who knew something about education in the two countries, and I learned more in three days in Yegey's house than I had in a year and a half in Karhide. It was Yegey who first explained to me the shape of the Gethenian mind, and it was Yegey who first showed me what I was actually carrying around in my head.

We sat up most of two nights talking. He was a small, precise, unhurried man who wanted very much to help me and who succeeded, at that stage, in helping me very little.

"The old gentleman," he said, referring to the Ekumen, "has told you that a world is a world, and that all worlds are the same. And you have believed it, because it is a comfortable thing to believe. But consider: what do you actually know about how anyone else perceives?"

"Perceives?"

"Everything. What a Karhider thinks he is. What a Karhider thinks you are. Where a Karhider believes the line is between one kind of being and another."

I said that this seemed a somewhat scholastic question.

"Then it is odd," he said, "that no one on your world has ever asked it."

He was not being clever. He had been an administrator for thirty years and he had spent most of that time watching children fail, and he had a very exact mind for the moment when a person stops being able to revise a picture of the world they have already formed. "A child of seven," he said, "will tell you what a fish is. A child of fourteen will tell you that a fish does not know it is a fish. Between those two ages the child has learned to doubt. Your race has not learned to doubt. Your race has learned to doubt about the physical world and about the stars, and about the mathematics of space, and about how many dimensions there are. You have not learned to doubt about minds. You are quite convinced that other minds work as yours does, and that the only question is whether they work well enough. And when you find one that does not, you call it an animal, and you are satisfied."

And then he said the thing I heard again in a hundred forms for the rest of my life on Winter: "If you are going to be a diplomat, Mr. Ai, you had better learn to doubt your own species."

I laughed at him. It was the wrong response and I knew it was the wrong response at the time, and I laughed anyway, because he was a small man in a house in a foreign city and I was thirty-one years old and had been on a planet that was trying to kill me for two years.

Then Yegey took me into the other room and showed me something. There were children in the house, and among them a girl of perhaps six, and he asked her to come and stand in front of us, and asked me to look at her and say whether she was a boy or a girl.

I said I could not.

"That is correct," he said. "It is also correct that she will be a boy in four years' time, for a week, and then a girl again for a week, and then neither, and so on. And it is correct that she will be perfectly calm about it. And it is correct that you, Mr. Ai, cannot tell the difference between her and her brother."

"I could with training—"

"You could not," said Yegey. "Not in a case like that. You could learn to see the physical signs at the moment of kemmer, but you could never know, standing there in front of her, which it was. And more: you could never know what she would be tomorrow. And more: she would not, if you asked her, have an answer. Not because she is hiding it. Because there is nothing there to report."

I sat in that little room a long time with that.

Because I had arrived with a theory. Not a plan — a theory. And the theory was that the difficulty was that the Gethenians had organized their society around a category I could not see, and that if I could get at the category — if I could just get one of them to explain it, one clear account of how they worked — then I would have the key, and then the whole thing would open up, and I would be able to work with them as one works with any other people who have a culture.

There was no category. There was no key. There was only the fact, which was that this child would be a boy for a week and a girl for a week and neither for the rest of the time, forever, and that this was not an answer to a question but the ground on which the question stood.

I had come to the planet to find out what was different, and I was going to be given, in the end, that there was nothing to be different in the way I meant.

They let me stay, that first winter in Orgoreyn, because Orgoreyn wants people to stay. The hospitality was real and it was warm and I was extremely comfortable, and I was also, by the end of the second month, aware that I was being managed.

In the north, Orgoreyn is a country of rigid formalities, of ritual observances, of procedure, of certificates and of grades. In the south it is a country of farms and orchards and lakes and light. I was moved from a comfortable flat in the city to a farm in the lake country, to see the country, and I saw it, and it was beautiful, and I was not permitted to leave the grounds. There was always someone there. There was always an excuse, always a reason, always a kindness.

And the thing that was happening to me, which I did not see at all, is that I was being given a face, and the face was being prepared for me.

The Orgotans are, on their own account, a practical and unsentimental people. Their state is a thing they built deliberately after a war of attrition against Karhide, and they built it because they had to: their country is central, their population is large, they are organised, and they won. But a state that has survived by being given to the people in a particular shape does not stay in that shape by accident. It has an interest in its own continuity. And the interest of the state, in the moment, is this: to survive, and to be believed.

Here is what Orgoreyn cannot admit, and what it had admitted to me, in effect, in the first three days, through Obsle, through Yegey, through all of them: that it wants the Ekumen. That the envoys and the trade and the ansible and the stars are exactly what it wants, and that it has been working toward this for four years, and that it would very much like the Treaty signed by the Assembly of All Thirtys before anybody else signs it first. That it is ahead of Karhide, it is modern, it is organised, it is the obvious candidate, and if the Ekumen will just name the price, Orgoreyn will sign.

That is the answer. That is what Obsle was selling, and it was a real answer and I wanted it.

But it was not offered freely. It was offered against a background condition that was never stated, because stating it would have been absurd: that no one was to be told that Orgoreyn had said it first.

The people who had brought me into Orgoreyn — Yegey, Obsle, the others — did not consider that they were lying to me. They considered that they were placing a candidate in a race in which I was the judge. This is the way that works. You do not say "we are the only ones who want this." You show the candidate to the judge, you display the virtues, and you let the judge conclude. You do not have to deceive a man who is willing to draw the conclusion.

And I was willing to draw it. Because I was looking at a machine that worked.

That was the whole of the mechanism, and I have thought about it since more than I have thought about anything else on this planet. A society that is confident and efficient and modern will show you a machine that works. It will invite you to inspect it. It will let you use it. And if you are the sort of person for whom a working machine is evidence, then you will report that the society is sound.

I was the sort of person for whom a working machine is evidence. That is a fact about me and not about Orgoreyn.

And here is the second half of it, which took me two years to be able to state. The machine was working. I want that registered clearly, because it is the thing people will otherwise assume I am accusing Orgoreyn of. Orgoreyn was not lying to me about the machine. The ansible talked to the ship, and the ship talked back, and the whole chain of communication was exactly as described. Everything I did in Orgoreyn, I could report home, and the report would go.

The mission was not a lie. Orgoreyn was not lying to me. Orgoreyn was lying to the Ekumen, and I was the instrument of it, and I did not know, and there was no way for me to know.

And the reason there was no way for me to know is the thing I have been circling since I arrived.

Because I was the judge of the race.

Because the judge cannot be shown the other candidates. The other candidate was in a castle in Erhenrang, being kept in the dark by a king who would not see me, through an Ambassador who was courteous and silent, through a prime minister who had told me to my face that my mission was opposed to the interests of his country.

And because I could not see that, because I could not see the difference between a man who is hiding something for shifgrethor and a man who is telling me the truth about a matter that shifgrethor is preventing me from hearing, I concluded that Orgoreyn was the one that wanted me, and that Karhide was the one that was playing me.

And so I went back to Karhide, at the end of the autumn, on the strength of a story I had been told, to tell the King that Orgoreyn wanted the Ekumen.

I did not know what I was doing. I want that on the record, because the phrase I have used is "I did not know what I was doing," and it is a phrase I have earned. I had no instruction to say anything of the kind. My instructions were to find out, and to report. And what I did was to take the report of one country and give it to another as a fact.

And the King believed me. Not because he was simple. Because I was the alien with the box, and the box had been shown to be real, and I had brought him a thing he wanted to hear, and the shifgrethor of the whole exchange, which was entirely accidental, served to give it weight.

That is what happened, and I have never been able to get it clear in my own mind how a single well-meant sentence can do so much damage. But I had been given, in Orgoreyn, a fact that was true, and I had been given the fact by people who had an interest in where I would place it, and I had believed that I had found the answer to my mission in a country that wanted me for its own reasons. And I went and gave it to the man who did not want it.

There is a thing a Karhider does not do. A Karhider does not take a thing said to him in confidence and carry it to another. It is the base of the whole of shifgrethor, and it is not a rule about politeness; it is the basis of the social order. A Karhidish Domain is a family. A Karhidish kingdom is a family quarrel. And in a family you do not carry what your brother said to you to your father.

And I did. And I did not know that I was doing it, and it is a defense, and it is not a defense.

I have to say one more thing about that, because it is the reason I am able to write any of this.

Estraven tried to stop me. That is the part I cannot forgive and cannot explain. He had been told by the King to say nothing to me. He had already gone far past his duty in telling me as much as he did. And when I said I was going to Karhide, and he understood what I was about to do, he tried to stop me. He tried to show me. He told me things, in a way I could not accept, because I could not accept anything from a man I had decided was an obstacle.

And I laughed at him. That is the exact verb. He made an argument to me and I laughed.

I have thought about that a great deal. I have thought about it on the ice, and I have thought about it here. And what I have come to is this: I laughed because laughing was available to me and arguing was not. I had no argument. He had the better of it in every particular, and he had it patiently, and he was not trying to win, and he was trying to get a man to do a thing he did not want to do. And there was nothing I could do with that except laugh, because to concede it would have been to admit that everything I had built for two years was a building with no floor.

He tried to save me from a mistake and I laughed at him, and then I went to Karhide, and then he was exiled and shot, and everything that happened afterward happened because I laughed.

I am not writing this to be loved. I am writing it because the thing I have learned is that the lie I told the King was not a lie. It was a true fact in the wrong place. It was Orgoreyn's truth, put into a Karhidish ear, and once it was in that ear it did what it was designed to do. The lie was not in the sentence. The lie was in the placement. And I did not know I was doing it, and that is what makes it unforgivable, and that is also what makes it the central fact of this report: I was not a spy and I was not a liar. I was a man who had learned that a machine works and who went on to act on that belief in a place where the belief was lethal.

Estraven knew. He told me, in the end, on the ice, in the tent, in the dark, and he did it plainly, and he did not put it in diplomatic language. He told me what he had done and why, and he told me that the whole of the plan, from beginning to end, had been to get me out of Orgoreyn before my return could be used against him, and that he had gambled on my return being the thing that would persuade the King.

I did not believe him. That is on the record too. I did not believe him, and I did not believe him on the ice, and I did not believe him when he told me the last fact of all, which was that he had been the durin, that he had been the previous envoy, that he had come to this planet as one of the Investigators forty years ago and had lived here and had never gone home and had never intended to.

I did not believe him on the ice, and I believed him afterwards, and the belief came too late to be of any use to him, except that it was the reason I was alive.

The Exile and the name of the traitor is a separate thing and I will take it in order, because the order matters: I cannot tell it correctly otherwise.

It was not a lie about Orgoreyn. That is what I have to say first, and I have had two years to work out how to say it, and the sentence I have arrived at is: Estraven was not a traitor and I was not a traitor and the transaction we were both involved in was not treason.

Here is what actually happened, in the order it happened.

Tibe resigned. That is the beginning. Tibe had been regent while the King was in seclusion, and Tibe had used the regency, and when the King came out of seclusion Tibe had a great deal to account for. He resigned and went to his estate. Estraven went back into the Karhidish cabinet as Prime Minister, and this was the period of my first year, the time when he was helping me, when I was a fool, and when the King and he between them were working to keep me away from everything.

And then the King learned what I had carried from Orgoreyn, and the King's anger was not at me. The King's anger was at Estraven. Because Estraven, of all people, had been the one who told the envoy that the Kingdom's interests were opposed to the mission. Estraven had gone on record. And the King, who had been kept in the dark, who had found himself in the position of a man who has been told by his own prime minister that a foreign power is subverting his kingdom, was now being told by an alien that the enemy nation has been bought with a box and a message.

He exiled Estraven. He had him stripped of title and seat in the Assemblies and commanded out of the Kingdom in three days, on pain of death, on pain of the whole of Karhide's law. He did not have him shot. He exiled him. Because he is a king and the law is a thing he holds, and even in anger, even when betrayed, he is the King's Ear... 

I have written that sentence badly. I will leave it. It is a fault and the reader will see it. But let me go on, because the reader should see that it is a fault and not see me smoothing it.

Argaven is not a bad man. I have had two years to make him into one and I could not do it, which is itself evidence. He is a small, hot, fierce old man who reads everything and thinks a great deal and has ruled for forty years and is afraid of exactly one thing, which is being made a fool of by a subject. He was made a fool of by a subject. And being made a fool of by a subject, for a man in his position, is not a political inconvenience. It is the end of everything he is.

The Order of Exile. I will give it verbatim, because it is the most Karhidish document I have ever read, and because it says more about this planet in one paragraph than everything I learned in three years.

Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, Lord of Estre in Kerm, by this order forfeits title of the Kingdom and seat in the Assemblies of the Kingdom, and is commanded to quit the Kingdom and all Domains of Karhide. If he be not gone out of the Kingdom and all Domains in three days' time, or if in his life he return into the Kingdom, he shall be put to death by any man without further judgment. No countryman of Karhide shall suffer Harth rem ir Estraven to speak to him or stay within his house or on his lands, on pain of imprisonment, nor shall any countryman of Karhide give or lend Harth rem ir Estraven money or goods, nor repay any debt owing him, on pain of imprisonment and fine. Let all countrymen of Karhide know and say that the crime for which Harth rem ir Estraven is exiled is the crime of Treason: he having urged privily and openly in Assembly and Palace, under pretense of loyal service to the King, that the Nation-Dominion of Karhide cast away its sovereignty and surrender up its power in order to become an inferior and subject nation in a certain Union of Peoples, concerning which let all men know and say that no such Union does exist, being a device and baseless fiction of certain conspiring traitors who seek to weaken the Authority of Karhide in the King, to the profit of the real and present enemies of the land.

Odguyrny Tuwa, Eighth Hour, in the Palace in Erhenrang: Argaven Harge.

That is the instrument. Read it. It does not accuse him of anything. It does not say he was in league with Orgoreyn. It does not say he was bought. It says he urged Karhide to give up its sovereignty to a Union of Peoples. And then, and this is the move, it declares that no such Union exists, that the whole thing is a baseless fiction invented by traitors, and that those who have urged it are in fact working for the real and present enemies of the land.

It is the perfect lie. It takes a true accusation and welds on to it a conclusion that is false. It never names Orgoreyn. It never has to. The man convicted is the man who said Karhide should join the Ekumen. The sentence is that anyone who says that is an enemy agent. That is what shifgrethor can do when it is in the right hands.

And it is the reason the whole of the rest of the story happens.

I have given the audience with the King at this point, because the Order was read out over the Palace Bulletin while I was standing in the anteroom of the very room in which I was about to be received, and I had gone there to do what I had gone there to do, and I want the reader to have the sequence exactly.

I was in the anteroom. I had a heavy hieb and I had sold a fourth ruby to buy it. The aide came and announced me by a name that was not mine: "Genry Ai." My name is Genly, but Karhiders cannot say the letter l, and so I had become Genry, and I never corrected them, and I do not know why I never corrected them.

Estraven's name came over the radio three times. The Bulletin repeated it, and then repeated it again, and my reason, which had been standing about for an hour doing nothing, caught the name and stopped.

I cut off the radio as if to stop it from giving evidence against me, and scuttled to the door. There of course I stopped. I went back to the table by the fireplace, and stood. I was no longer calm or resolute. I wanted to get out the ansible and send an Advise/Urgent! to Hain. I suppressed this impulse also, as it was even sillier than the first.

The aide came back and stood aside, announcing me, and left me in the Red Hall with King Argaven XV.

An immense, high, long room, that Red Hall of the King's House. Half a mile down to the fireplaces. Half a mile up to the raftered ceiling hung with red, dusty drapes or banners, all ragged with the years. The windows are only slits or slots in the thick walls, the lights few, high, and dim. My new boots go eck, eck, eck, eck as I walk down the hall towards the king, a six months' journey.

Argaven was standing in front of the central and largest fireplace of three, on a low, large dais: a short figure in the reddish gloom, rather potbellied, very erect, dark and featureless in silhouette except for the glint of the big seal-ring on his thumb.

I stopped at the edge of the dais and, as I had been instructed, did and said nothing.

"Come up, Mr. Ai. Sit down."

I obeyed, taking the right-hand chair by the central hearth. In all this I had been drilled. Argaven did not sit down; he stood ten feet from me with the roaring bright flames behind him, and presently said, "Tell me what you have to tell me, Mr. Ai. You bear a message, they say."

The face that turned towards me, reddened and cratered by firelight and shadow, was as flat and cruel as the moon, Winter's dull rufous moon. Argaven was less kingly, less manly, than he looked at a distance among his courtiers. His voice was thin, and he held his fierce lunatic head at an angle of bizarre arrogance.

"My lord, what I have to say is gone out of my head. I only just now learned of Lord Estraven's disgrace."

Argaven smiled at that, a stretched, staring grin. He laughed shrilly like an angry woman pretending to be amused. "Damn him," he said, "the proud, posturing fool. He'll come crawling to me in the first hard weather."

I said nothing.

He went on to ask me, in a voice that was almost a kind of tenderness, whether the whole business had been the ambition of Lord Estraven alone; whether I was not, in truth, the dupe of a man who had used me for his own advancement; whether I understood that I had been made use of, and that the King of Karhide was a man who did not forget such things.

And I said, yes. I said it clearly and I said it without any particular discomfort. I said that Lord Estraven had been using me, and that I had been a tool, and that I had been made a fool of by a man who was a great deal cleverer than I was.

Argaven smiled. He had got what he came for. I had gone into that hall to vindicate Estraven and I had vindicated nothing; I had gone to tell the King that Orgoreyn wanted the Ekumen, and I told him instead that Estraven had been using me and that Orgoreyn was not the enemy.

And then he asked me about the ansible. He said he would have it. He said the way to have it was for me to send the message on to Hain and let them know that the Kingdom would receive a mission, and that when the ship came, the ansible would come with it, and that the one I was carrying would be taken.

And I said no.

And Argaven laughed at that, too, in the same shrill way, and said, "Then the treaty will be invalidated, and then the treaty will be invalidated, and then the treaty will be invalidated, and then the treaty will be invalidated."

And I said no again.

There was a pause. Then he said, "Mr. Ai, do you understand that in Karhide a crown is a buildweight that a subject can go to the palace and can go to the palace and can go to the palace... ."

I forget the rest of it. He was quoting something. He was citing some dictum of Karhidish statecraft. He was saying that in Karhide a sovereign is not an individual but an individual, and that a subject who has been refused an answer has been given a temporary reprieve, and that the reprieve is renewable at the King's pleasure. And I understood none of the it and I stood there and I did not answer.

And then he asked me, and this is the question, this is the whole book, this is why I am writing it down.

"Mr. Ai, do you know, by your own experience, what it is to be a Gethenian?"

I did not answer.

"Well," he said, "That's why that's the same thing."

"Well," he said, "That's why that's the same thing."

"Well," he said, "That's why that's the same thing."

And then he was finished with me. He took the order out of his own hand and took the ansible out of my hand and put them both in the fire.
